₦5m each, no shares given up — NiYA and Cascador's founders.

Signal: The Nigerian Youth Academy (NiYA) and Cascador have awarded ₦5 million each to eight Nigerian founders as growth capital that takes no shares. The other nine founders in the 17-person cohort each receive ₦1 million. The 17 were picked from more than 19,000 applications, and the four-week programme gave them training, regulatory guidance and mentorship. The eight businesses span education, fashion technology, food production, manufacturing, agriculture and climate solutions, and include SewPro (business tools for tailors), Biuda (technology education for children) and Sparklean Station (cleaning products).

So what: This is government money that takes no shares — the opposite of an accelerator cheque. ₦5 million is small, but it is free capital plus four weeks of training. For an early founder, grants like this are worth chasing before giving away equity.

Do next: If you are a Nigerian founder at an early stage, watch for the next NiYA-Cascador call — this cohort launched in August 2026 — and keep a one-page business summary ready. With 19,000 applicants, selection is competitive and preparation matters. Related lesson: Accelerator Maths — what $50,000 and a programme actually cost you.

10 NOVEMBER 2026 — REGULATION

CBN's stablecoin rules set a ₦15bn bar — and a 30-day clock is already running.

Signal: The Central Bank of Nigeria has published the Regulations for the Operations of Non-Security Virtual Assets in Nigeria, effective 5 November 2026. A foreign-currency stablecoin issuer — a company that issues a digital token pegged to the dollar, such as USDT or USDC — must operate through a Nigerian-incorporated entity holding a Class 2 Foreign Currency Issuer Licence, with minimum paid-up capital of ₦15 billion. Naira stablecoins need a Class 1 licence and ₦10 billion; other crypto service providers need ₦1 billion. Existing operators must register within 30 days and apply for a licence within 180 days. Peer-to-peer platforms may not release crypto until the name and Bank Verification Number on the paying bank account match the verified customer. Techpoint reported on 7 November that USDT and USDC do not yet qualify, because no Class 2 licences have been issued.

So what: If your product touches stablecoins or crypto payments in Nigeria, the entry bar is now ₦15 billion in capital, plus a local company and a licence — and the 30-day registration clock is already running. A product built on an unlicensed issuer's rails now has a deadline, not just a risk. The rules also change what your customers can do: a peer-to-peer trade can no longer settle against a bank account in a different name.

Do next: If you touch stablecoins or crypto payments in Nigeria, check this week whether you count as an operator under the rules — and get legal advice on the 30-day registration step before the clock runs out. Do not take on new crypto-payment volume until you know where you stand. Related lesson: When the Regulator Redraws the Market.

Sources: TechCabal Daily, 9 November 2026 (rules, capital levels, deadlines); Economy Post, 6 November 2026 (circular detail); Techpoint Africa, 7 November 2026 (USDT/USDC do not yet qualify).

10 NOVEMBER 2026 — REGULATION

Kenya's crypto deadline passed. No licence has been granted.

Signal: The one-year transition period under Kenya's Virtual Asset Service Providers Act 2025 ended on Wednesday, 4 November 2026. A Virtual Asset Service Provider is a company that handles crypto for customers — an exchange, a wallet, a broker. From 4 November, operating in or from Kenya without a licence is an offence: fines of up to KSh25 million for companies, and up to KSh10 million and/or five years in prison for individuals. As of 9 November, neither the Capital Markets Authority nor the Central Bank of Kenya has published a public register or announced a licence grant, according to VASP Tracker reporting. No enforcement action against a named operator has been reported.

So what: If you serve Kenyan crypto users, you are now, on a strict reading, operating without a licence — and a pending application may not protect you, because the Act gives no automatic grace period for applicants still waiting for a decision. The implementing regulations were only published on 22 July 2026, barely three months before the deadline, so many applicants were still in the queue when it passed.

Do next: Check your status on the Capital Markets Authority and Central Bank of Kenya registers this week, and get legal advice before assuming a pending application protects you. Do not take on new Kenyan crypto customers until you know where you stand.

Sources: VASP Tracker via Crypto Africa News, 9 November 2026 (no licence granted, no public register — tracker-reported, not a regulator statement); Tech with Muchiri, 3 November 2026 (deadline, penalties, licensing split); The Industry Spread (Capital Markets Authority licences exchanges and brokers; Central Bank licences wallets, payments and stablecoins).

10 NOVEMBER 2026 — REGULATION

Your payment data must live in Nigeria by 1 January — 52 days left.

Signal: Nigeria's data-protection regulator, the National Data Protection Commission (NDPC), publicly backed the Central Bank's data-localisation policy on 8 November 2026. Its chief executive, Vincent Olatunji, said the commission helped shape the policy, that a joint Central Bank–NDPC working group is running, and that Nigeria has more than 25 data centres, most of them underused — local capacity exists. The rule stands: the Central Bank's circular of 15 June 2026 requires all payment transaction data generated in Nigeria to be stored and managed in Nigeria by 1 January 2027. It covers banks, microfinance banks, fintechs, mobile-money operators, switching companies and all licensed payment participants. Non-compliance brings supervisory sanctions.

So what: Fifty-two days to the deadline. If your product still holds Nigerian payment data — or its backups, or its encryption keys — outside Nigeria, this is no longer a policy debate. It is a move you must make or a sanction you must risk. The regulator saying "capacity exists" removes the excuse that there is nowhere to move it to.

Do next: This week, map where your customer and payment data actually lives — hosting, backups, analytics, support tools — and ask each provider where every copy sits. If anything sits abroad, start the move now, not in December. The desk's data-localisation checklist lands on 15 November; until then, the map is the job.

Sources: ITEdgeNews, 8 November 2026 (NDPC backing, working group, data-centre claim); Central Bank of Nigeria circular PSS/DIR/PUB/CIR/001/004, 15 June 2026 (1 January 2027 deadline, scope, sanctions).

8 NOVEMBER 2026 — FUNDING

Kenya's Treasury told banks to pass on the rate cuts — check what your bank actually charges.

Signal: Kenya's National Treasury Cabinet Secretary, John Mbadi, told commercial banks on 3 November 2026 to lower lending rates and lend more to businesses, farmers and small firms. The Central Bank of Kenya has cut its benchmark rate from 13% in mid-2024 to 8.75%, but the average bank lending rate is still 14.4%. Private-sector lending grew 10.6% in September 2026, after shrinking 2.9% in January 2025.

So what: The policy rate is not the rate you pay. The gap between 8.75% and 14.4% is more than five percentage points — on a KSh10 million loan, that gap is about KSh565,000 a year. Cheaper credit is coming, but slowly, and only to borrowers who ask.

Do next: If you borrow in Kenya, ask your bank for a fresh quote this week and compare it on the Kenya Bankers Association's Total Cost of Credit site (costofcredit.co.ke), which lists every bank's published rates. If you have a variable-rate loan, check whether your rate has moved since the cuts.

8 NOVEMBER 2026 — CAPITAL

Yellow Card bought its way into Brazil — acquisition is a market-entry shortcut.

Signal: Yellow Card, the African stablecoin infrastructure company, announced on 5 November 2026 that it has acquired GoMoney, a Brazilian crypto payments company, giving it direct operations in Latin America's largest crypto market. Yellow Card launched in Africa in 2019 and has since expanded into other emerging markets.

So what: When a company buys instead of builds, it is buying customers, licences and local knowledge in one move. For an early founder, the lesson is not "go buy a company" — it is that entering a new market has more than one door, and the build-it-yourself door is the slowest.

Do next: If you plan to enter a new market next year, price the shortcut before you price the build: list one small local operator or partner you could buy, join or resell through, and what it would cost, against the cost of hiring and building there yourself.

6 NOVEMBER 2026 — FUNDING

October funding: equity up, debt down, fewer companies raising.

Signal: Africa: The Big Deal reported on 5 November 2026 that African startups raised $2.4 billion between January and October 2026, down 12% on the same period of 2025. The split matters more than the total: equity funding rose 5% to $1.6 billion, while debt funding fell 33% to $740 million. Only 343 startups raised at all — the lowest January-to-October count since the tracker began counting deals of $100,000 or more in 2021. October itself brought $183 million across 34 startups, and Morocco and Tanzania led the month, with ABA Life raising $40 million and East Africa Foods closing a $40 million Series B.

So what: The headline fall is a debt story, not an equity story. Equity money is still moving; loans and venture debt have shrunk by a third. And fewer companies are raising at all — so a flat total hides a market that is getting narrower, not just smaller.

Do next: If you are raising, read the split for your instrument before you retime anything: venture debt is scarcer this year, equity is holding. And check the October deal list for your country and sector: if one or two deals made your market's number, the ranking tells you about those companies, not your market. Related lesson: The Money Is Back, But It's Choosier.

Sources: scommerce.com, 5 November 2026 (ten-month totals, equity/debt split, startup count); Tekedia, 5 November 2026 (October total, Morocco and Tanzania deals).

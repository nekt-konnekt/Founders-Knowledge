# The Founders Knowledge Editorial Standard

**Purpose:** This is the working source of truth for how Founders Knowledge (FK) thinks, writes, structures, verifies, and presents its published knowledge. Read it before drafting or editing any lesson, comparison, field note, guide, or framework.

**Audience:** FK editors, human contributors, AI writers, researchers, and anyone publishing on behalf of FK.

**Editorial promise:** Help founders make better decisions. Do not merely explain a topic; show why it matters, how to reason about it, what to do next, and where the advice stops applying.

**Design reference:** [Validate the Idea Before You Build](https://founders-knowledge.vercel.app/lessons/validate-the-idea/). Use it as the reference for the current lesson-page design system. Also inspect recent technical and field-note lessons when the format calls for evidence, current facts, or operational detail.

---

## 1. What Founders Knowledge is

FK is a practical knowledge centre, not a chronological blog, a newswire, a vendor catalogue, or a place for generic motivational advice. It organizes knowledge around recurring founder questions and decisions.

Our job is to be a useful **third eye**: help founders see the trade-offs, assumptions, incentives, risks, and second-order effects that are easy to miss when they are deep inside the work.

A strong FK piece gives the reader at least one of these:
- A clearer way to understand a real founder problem.
- A framework for making a decision.
- A practical sequence they can apply.
- A comparison that makes trade-offs visible.
- A grounded field observation, with its limits clearly stated.
- A risk or failure mode they can prepare for before it becomes expensive.

Do not publish a piece just because a topic is trending. The question is: **What will a founder understand or decide better after reading this?**

## 2. The editorial point of view

### Start with the founder's real problem

Start from a decision, tension, recurring mistake, costly assumption, operational failure, or confusing trade-off. Avoid starting with a dictionary definition unless the definition itself resolves the reader's immediate confusion.

Weak framing: “A VPS is a virtual private server. A vCPU is a virtual central processing unit.”

Stronger framing: “A hosting plan can look affordable and still be the wrong choice for a service that must run several agents at once. The important question is not just how many vCPUs a provider advertises, but what workload those resources can sustain at an acceptable cost.”

The second version starts with the decision, then earns the explanation.

### Explain the mechanism, not just the conclusion

Do not simply tell readers that something is good, bad, important, risky, or recommended. Explain **why**. Show the mechanism connecting a choice to its likely outcome. If the mechanism depends on context, name that context.

### Make trade-offs visible

Most meaningful founder decisions have competing goals: speed versus reliability, price versus performance, control versus convenience, growth versus margin, or flexibility versus operational burden. Show what the reader gains, what they give up, and what conditions change the answer.

### Give the reader agency

Finish with a useful decision rule, test, checklist, question, or next action. Avoid endings that only repeat the introduction or offer vague encouragement.

### Be confident about reasoning, careful about facts

FK can make a clear argument without pretending uncertain facts are certain. Distinguish:
- **Verified fact:** supported by a reliable source.
- **Reported claim:** attributed to a person, company, publication, or community.
- **Interpretation:** what the evidence appears to mean.
- **Illustration:** a hypothetical example used to explain a mechanism.
- **Recommendation:** a reasoned action, with conditions and limitations.
- **Unknown:** something the available evidence does not establish.

Do not blur these categories.

## 3. Voice and sentence craft

The FK voice is clear, grounded, curious, practical, candid, and respectful of the reader's intelligence. It sounds like an experienced operator helping a founder think—not a lecturer showing off, a consultant hiding behind jargon, or a marketer selling certainty.

### Do

- Use plain English and explain necessary technical terms when they first matter.
- Prefer concrete nouns and active verbs.
- Use short paragraphs with a clear job.
- Ask a question when it sharpens the decision, not as a decorative device.
- Use examples that make abstract reasoning tangible.
- Use Nigerian or African context when it materially changes the economics, regulation, distribution, infrastructure, payment options, or user behaviour. Do not force regional references where they add nothing.
- Use British/Nigerian spelling consistently where natural: “behaviour”, “prioritise”, “organise”, “licence” as a noun.
- Keep the tone warm and direct. Be willing to say that an assumption is weak or a popular shortcut is not evidence.

### Avoid

- Hype: “revolutionary”, “game-changing”, “unprecedented”, “the ultimate guide”.
- Empty absolutes: “always”, “never”, “guaranteed”, “the only way”, unless literally defensible.
- Generic introductions such as “In today's fast-paced digital world”.
- Generic conclusions such as “In conclusion, success requires hard work and dedication”.
- Repeating the same point in the opening, every section, and the conclusion.
- Jargon without explanation, needless acronyms, and long blocks of background that do not support the decision.
- Treating an anecdote as a benchmark, one successful case as proof, or a founder's opinion as market-wide truth.
- Artificial balance that gives weak evidence the same weight as strong evidence.
- Overusing rhetorical questions, slogans, exclamation marks, callouts, and one-sentence paragraphs.

## 4. The standard reasoning pattern

Not every piece needs identical sections, but a strong FK article usually moves through this logic:

1. **The situation:** What is the reader facing, and why does it matter now?
2. **The tension:** What do founders commonly assume, overlook, or confuse?
3. **The explanation:** What is actually happening, and why?
4. **The decision framework:** Which factors, options, or signals should guide the choice?
5. **The application:** What would this look like in a real scenario?
6. **The limits:** What could change the answer? What remains uncertain?
7. **The next move:** What should the reader check, decide, measure, or test?

This is a reasoning pattern, not a rigid template. Use only the sections that earn their place. Do not force every article into the same mould.

## 5. Openings, headlines, and section structure

### Headline

Write a clear, specific headline around the reader's problem, decision, or consequence. A good title promises useful understanding without exaggerating the result.

Prefer a clear tension or useful question over vague cleverness. If the piece is a comparison, name the options or the decision. If it is a field note, make the scope clear. If it is part of a series, label the part consistently.

### Opening

The opening should quickly establish:
- The problem or decision.
- Why a founder should care.
- The central insight or tension the piece will unpack.

Avoid long preambles, generic history, or a definition that could be moved to a later section. The opening is not a miniature version of the entire article.

### Section headings

Use informative headings that move the argument forward. FK's existing lessons often pair a short editorial kicker (for example, “SEPARATE OPINION FROM EVIDENCE”) with a direct heading (for example, “Look for what people already do.”). Use this pattern when it clarifies the sequence; do not make headings cryptic for style.

Each section should add a distinct step in the reasoning. If two sections make the same point, combine or differentiate them.

### Paragraphs and lists

Use paragraphs to explain reasoning. Use lists for steps, options, criteria, symptoms, checks, or genuinely parallel items. A list is not a substitute for explaining why its items matter.

Tables are useful when readers need to compare the same criteria across options. Do not create a table just to make a short article look technical.

## 6. Choose the right content type

### Lesson

Teach a durable principle or skill. Explain the idea, show why it matters, and help the reader apply it to their own situation.

### Practical guide

Help the reader complete a task or make a concrete decision. Use ordered steps, checks, failure paths, and clear conditions. Include operational details where they matter.

### Framework

Give the reader a reusable mental model or decision method. Explain what each part means, when to use it, and what the framework cannot decide for them. A score or matrix is an aid to judgement, not an oracle.

### Comparison

Compare options against the same decision criteria. Do not write one miniature sales pitch for each option and call that a comparison. Explain which differences matter, for whom, under what workload or circumstances, and what trade-offs follow.

### Field note / case study

Start from a specific observation, build a careful interpretation, and explain what can and cannot be inferred. Label anecdotal, self-reported, or unverified data. Do not manufacture a general rule from one example.

### Multi-part series

Make each part useful on its own while explaining how it relates to the series. Keep terminology, framing, metadata, links, and part labels consistent. Do not split a single article into multiple parts just to increase output.

## 7. Special standard for technical comparisons

This section is mandatory for comparisons of hosting, infrastructure, AI models, APIs, databases, payment providers, software, or other technical choices.

### First define the actual job

Describe the workload and constraints before discussing products. Include the factors that change the answer, such as:
- What must run, and how many jobs or users may be active concurrently?
- Is the workload CPU-bound, memory-bound, storage-bound, network-bound, or dependent on a GPU?
- What latency, uptime, isolation, compliance, or recovery requirements matter?
- What is the budget, including usage-based charges and operational time?
- What must be available in the founder's country or region?
- What is the cost of failure, migration, or vendor lock-in?

Do not recommend infrastructure in a vacuum.

### Define terms precisely

Separate concepts that are often confused. For example:
- A **vCPU** is a provider-defined unit of virtual CPU capacity; its performance depends on the underlying hardware, allocation, scheduling, and provider policy.
- A **VPS** is a virtual server product or environment. Its total resources, isolation, storage, networking, and management model vary by provider and plan.

Do not treat a vCPU count as a universal performance score. Explain that advertised specifications do not, by themselves, establish real workload throughput.

### Compare like with like

Use the same criteria for every option. State plan names, resource specifications, price currency, billing period, taxes, setup costs, bandwidth or traffic limits, storage type, backup policy, region, and relevant exclusions where those details are available and decision-relevant.

If plans are not equivalent, say so. If prices are promotional, region-specific, or time-sensitive, date them and verify current terms before publication. Never invent a price or imply a checked feature that was not checked.

### Explain consequences, not just specifications

Translate specs into operational meaning. A reader needs to know what the difference could mean for their workload, not just that one plan has more memory. Distinguish advertised resources from tested performance, and explain bottlenecks that may dominate the result.

### Give a conditional recommendation

Do not declare a universal winner when the best choice depends on the workload. Use conditional conclusions such as:
- Best fit if the priority is the lowest fixed monthly cost and the workload is modest.
- Worth considering when predictable CPU performance matters more than the lowest headline price.
- Not a safe choice until the actual workload passes a concurrency and memory test.

Only use these conclusions when evidence supports them.

### Specify a test that could overturn the recommendation

For technical choices, a useful comparison often ends with a small reproducible test: run the actual workload, at realistic concurrency, for long enough to observe CPU, memory, latency, failures, and cost. Define what success looks like before running it. A recommendation that cannot be tested is usually too vague.

## 8. Research, evidence, and source discipline

Research when the subject is current, technical, regulated, high-consequence, contested, or likely to change. This includes prices, product plans, provider limits, legal requirements, regulator status, security guidance, and recent events.

### Source hierarchy

Prefer sources in this order, depending on the claim:
1. Primary sources: official documentation, regulations, regulator registers, pricing pages, release notes, status pages, technical specifications, or original datasets.
2. Direct statements from the relevant company, institution, or person.
3. Reputable reporting that identifies its evidence and dates.
4. Expert analysis and independent tests with a transparent method.
5. Community discussions as leads, practical experiences, or attributed anecdotes—not automatic proof.

A primary source can still be incomplete or self-interested. Evaluate what it actually establishes.

### Verify before writing

- Check the date and scope of a source.
- Confirm that the source supports the specific sentence, not merely the general topic.
- Distinguish a policy announcement from settings that vary by account.
- Distinguish a listed capability from measured performance.
- For current prices and plans, verify the exact plan and region.
- For incidents or disputes, represent each relevant party's claims fairly and state what remains unverified.
- For regulatory topics, link to the relevant official register or guidance and explain that the reader must verify the specific entity and activity.
- For calculations, show the inputs or method. Label hypothetical numbers as illustrative.
- Cite factual claims close to where they appear, using clear links in the published article. Keep a useful “Sources” or “What this is based on” section for material claims where appropriate.

Never invent a source, quote, benchmark, customer result, feature, plan detail, legal rule, or statistic. If a claim cannot be verified, remove it, qualify it, or state that it could not be verified.

## 9. Examples, numbers, and recommendations

Examples should make the reasoning easier to apply. Identify whether an example is real, reported, anonymised, hypothetical, or illustrative.

When using numbers:
- Give units, timeframe, currency, and assumptions.
- Show simple calculations where they change the conclusion.
- Do not compare numbers measured under different conditions without explaining the difference.
- Do not present a ratio from a single anecdote as a conversion benchmark.
- Do not imply precision that the underlying evidence does not support.

Recommendations should name the conditions under which they hold. If a different founder with a different stage, workload, country, buyer, or risk tolerance could reasonably choose differently, explain the deciding factor.

## 10. The reader's practical payoff

Every piece should leave the reader with a useful next step. Depending on the topic, this may be:
- A short exercise.
- A decision tree or comparison table.
- A checklist.
- A question to ask a provider or partner.
- A measurement to inspect.
- A small experiment with a success criterion.
- A failure path to test.
- A primary source to verify before committing.

“Do more research” is not a useful next step. Tell the reader what to verify and why it could change the decision.

A strong closing does not mechanically repeat the article. It distils the central decision rule, names the next action, or states the most important limit.

## 11. Visual and page design system

The published lesson page is part of the product. Writing quality and page design must work together. Use the live [Validate the Idea lesson](https://founders-knowledge.vercel.app/lessons/validate-the-idea/) as the baseline.

### Core visual identity

- **Display typeface:** Manrope.
- **Body typeface:** DM Sans.

- **Shape and depth:** large-radius rounded cards, soft 1px borders, and soft drop shadows. Use these as a system, not as decoration on every element.
- **Layout:** generous whitespace, readable line lengths, clear hierarchy, and a responsive mobile layout.

### Standard lesson-page anatomy

Use the established pattern unless there is a clear reason not to:
1. Shared site header with FK brand and navigation.
2. Lesson hero/cover with category kicker, headline, concise deck, reading time/content type, and topic-specific cover art.
3. Progress/topic caption where appropriate.
4. Article layout with a “In this guide” sidebar/table of contents on wide screens and a readable single-column flow on small screens.
5. An opening editorial statement that frames the problem.
6. Numbered sections with clear headings and a logical progression.
7. Purposeful callouts, comparison cards, question cards, checklists, diagrams, or tables only where they improve understanding.
8. Practical exercise or next step.
9. Takeaway and relevant sources/context.
10. Related knowledge/navigation and the site's existing sharing behaviour where supported by the current template.

Not every lesson needs every component. Components must serve the explanation, not fill a quota.

### Visual restraint and accessibility

- One visual hierarchy should lead the reader from the question to the reasoning and then to action.
- Do not turn every paragraph into a card. Avoid decorative cards that repeat the text beside them.
- Use tables for real comparisons and diagrams for relationships or workflows that are difficult to explain in prose.
- Preserve semantic heading order, readable contrast, keyboard focus, alt text for meaningful images, and clear link labels.
- Keep mobile reading comfortable; do not let sidebars, tables, or illustrations break the page width.
- Avoid fake dashboards, made-up graphs, and decorative metrics that imply evidence.
- Do not create new colours, fonts, button styles, or card systems when existing tokens can do the job.

## 12. SEO and Open Graph are part of publishing

Every new lesson must follow the site's existing metadata and Open Graph pattern, including:
- A unique page title and meta description.
- A canonical URL matching the final slug.
- Appropriate Article structured data.
- Open Graph title, description, URL, image, image dimensions, and image alt text.
- Twitter/X large-image card metadata.
- The established FK lesson OG artwork system under `assets/og/lessons/`, using the correct lesson identity and a valid share image URL.
- A link from the knowledge library and any relevant reading path or series pages.

Do not copy another lesson's canonical URL, title, description, or OG image. Validate the actual rendered page and social metadata after deployment. A locally correct HTML file is not proof that the production deployment or social cache is correct.

## 13. Publishing workflow

1. **Frame the question.** Write the founder's real decision in one sentence.
2. **Choose the content type.** Lesson, guide, framework, comparison, or field note.
3. **Research and collect evidence.** Identify the claims that require verification before drafting.
4. **Build the argument.** Outline the problem, mechanism, trade-offs, framework, limits, and next step.
5. **Draft for understanding.** Explain the reasoning before polishing the prose.
6. **Challenge the draft.** Look for unsupported claims, hidden assumptions, missing alternatives, vague advice, repetition, and false certainty.
7. **Apply the design system.** Use the established template, shared CSS, semantic structure, and responsive components.
8. **Complete metadata and distribution assets.** Check canonical, structured data, OG/Twitter metadata, image, internal links, and relevant library entries.
9. **Run the editorial checklist below.**
10. **Publish only after approval.** Commit through the repository workflow, confirm deployment readiness, and inspect the live page.

## 14. The FK quality gate

A draft is not ready because it is grammatically correct or visually attractive. Before publication, ask:

### Value and framing
- Is the piece built around a real founder problem or decision?
- Can the reader tell why it matters within the opening?
- Does the article provide insight beyond a generic explanation or a rephrased search result?
- Is there one clear argument, with each section advancing it?

### Reasoning and usefulness
- Does it explain why, not just what?
- Are trade-offs and alternative paths visible?
- Are examples specific and clearly labelled?
- Does it provide an actionable framework, test, or next step?
- Does it identify the conditions that could change the recommendation?

### Evidence and integrity
- Are current or consequential claims verified from appropriate sources?
- Are facts, reported claims, interpretations, illustrations, and recommendations distinguishable?
- Are figures, dates, units, prices, and calculations accurate and contextualised?
- Are uncertainty and gaps stated honestly?
- Have invented claims, generic filler, and unsupported certainty been removed?

### Writing and structure
- Is the language plain, direct, and respectful?
- Do headings move the argument forward?
- Is any point repeated without adding value?
- Are technical terms explained when needed?
- Does the ending leave the reader with a useful decision or action?

### Design and publishing
- Does the page follow the FK visual system and work on mobile?
- Are visual components useful rather than decorative?
- Are accessibility basics respected?
- Are title, description, canonical, structured data, and OG/Twitter metadata correct?
- Are internal links, sources, sharing behaviour, and the library listing correct?
- Has the deployed page been inspected?

**Decision rule:** If a piece is clear but generic, it is not ready. If it is detailed but unverified, it is not ready. If it is accurate but leaves the founder unable to decide or act, it is not ready. FK should aim for all three: grounded, insightful, and useful.

## 15. Reference lessons and how to learn from them

Read the first 15 entries in the published knowledge library as a body of work, not as fifteen templates to copy. They demonstrate several recurring patterns:

- **Durable founder lessons:** *Validate the Idea Before You Build* and *Choose What to Build Next* show how to turn a broad principle into a practical sequence.
- **Product and distribution reasoning:** *Why Should Users Choose Your Product?*, *Your Product Is Invisible*, and *Ship. Launch. Debug. Repeat.* show how to frame product problems around user behaviour and the actual bottleneck.
- **Business-model trade-offs:** *Pricing Is a Product Decision*, *Choosing a Pricing Model*, and *Your App Doesn't Need an App Store to Earn* connect product choices to willingness to pay, delivery, and business economics.
- **Operational reliability:** *What Happens When Your Payment Provider Fails?* and *Connect Payments to Paid Access* explain failure states, safe flows, and the difference between a successful-looking screen and a verified outcome.
- **Evidence-led field guides:** *How to Verify a Fintech or Investment Partner Before You Trust It*, *Should Nigerian Founders Incorporate in Delaware Before Raising?*, and *Before Your Hosting Provider Deletes Old Deployments, Check This* show the importance of context, primary sources, explicit limits, and concrete checks.
- **Case analysis:** *Your Marketplace Is Only as Strong as the Economics of Participating in It* demonstrates how to distinguish reported claims from verified outcomes, explain system-level incentives, and avoid reducing a complex problem to one visible metric.

Learn the underlying method, not the exact number of sections, wording, or visual components. The best next piece may require a different structure. Consistency means consistent judgement and quality—not identical articles.

## Final rule

**Write to improve the founder's judgement. Research to earn trust. Design to make the reasoning easier to use. Never mistake a polished page for a finished piece of knowledge.**

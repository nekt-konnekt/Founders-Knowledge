The 13-Week Cash Sheet: A Weekly Cash Discipline for Small Teams

Your bank balance says you have money. It does not say whether you can pay salaries in six weeks. A one-page rolling sheet, updated every Monday, tells you the truth while there is still time to fix it.

A founder checks her bank balance on a Friday morning: ₦4 million. Salaries go out next Friday: ₦1.8 million. Rent is due the week after that. A customer owes ₦900,000 and pays “next week” — a phrase that has meant three weeks before. The balance says she is fine. The calendar says she has about five weeks before the money runs out, and she does not know it yet. This lesson is the sheet that tells her — thirteen weeks early, while she can still fix it.

01 — THE LIE IN THE BANK BALANCE

A bank balance is a photograph. Cash is a film.

A bank balance is money in the account at one moment. It says nothing about what arrives next week or what leaves the week after. And in a small company, money does not arrive and leave evenly. It arrives in lumps and leaves in lumps. A customer pays late. A grant lands all at once. A subscription renews on one day. Salaries leave once a month. Rent leaves once a quarter. Hosting is charged once a year. Tax is due on one date. A balance of ₦4 million with ₦1.8 million of salaries due in two weeks and nothing coming in is not ₦4 million of safety. It is two paydays of cover.

Runway is how many months your company can keep going on the cash it has, before it must earn more or raise more. The usual shortcut is to divide the balance by the monthly spend: ₦4 million divided by ₦2 million a month is two months of runway. That shortcut assumes money leaves evenly, week by week. It does not. Salaries, rent and tax leave on fixed dates, and income arrives on dates you do not control. The honest version of runway is a calendar: which week does the balance hit zero?

The mechanism is cash timing. Two companies can hold the same balance and spend the same amount each month, and still have different fates. One gets paid every week by customers who pay on delivery. The other gets paid 90 days after invoicing, because its customers are large companies with slow payment processes. The second company can run out of cash while being profitable on paper — it has sold plenty, it is simply waiting to be paid. The balance cannot show you that. A calendar can.

02 — THE MISTAKE

Founders check the balance. They should check the calendar.

The habit is simple and it feels responsible: open the banking app, see a comfortable number, feel safe, get back to building. The number is real. The feeling is wrong. The balance answers one question — how much is in the account right now — and founders treat it as the answer to a different question: will we be able to pay everyone next month?

Three ways founders fool themselves with a balance. First, counting money that is promised, not received. An invoice is not cash. A signed contract is not cash. A grant approval is not cash. An investor who is “almost there” is not cash. Until the money is in the account, it is a hope with a date attached — and the date is usually wrong. Second, forgetting the lumpy costs. The annual hosting bill, the quarterly rent, the tax payment, the contractor invoice that arrives once a quarter, the licence renewal. These are all known in advance and all invisible in a balance. Third, treating the next raise, the next big customer or the next grant as money in. It is not money in. It is a plan to get money, and plans fail.

✳
The rule

An invoice is not cash. A promise is not cash. Only money in the account pays salaries.

03 — THE SHEET

Thirteen weeks, three rows, one page.

The sheet is a rolling forecast of the next thirteen weeks — one quarter. Thirteen weeks is the right length for two reasons. It is long enough that a gap is worth fixing: if the sheet shows trouble in week ten, you have ten weeks to chase a payment, cut a cost or close a sale. And it is short enough to forecast honestly: you know who owes you, what contracts are signed and what bills are due in the next three months. Beyond thirteen weeks the numbers turn into guesses, and guesses do not belong in a cash forecast.

The sheet has three rows. Money in: only money you are sure about. Cash in hand, invoices already issued to customers who pay on time, signed contracts with payment dates, subscriptions that renew automatically. If you would bet your salary on it arriving that week, it goes here. Money out: everything that leaves — salaries, rent, tools and hosting, suppliers, tax, loan repayments. Use the real dates, not the monthly average: salaries leave on payday, rent leaves on the due date. Balance: last week's balance, plus money in, minus money out, worked week by week. The balance row is the whole point of the sheet: it shows the future balance, not today's.

Then there is a fourth row, and it is the discipline that makes the sheet honest: the “maybe” row. Promised money goes here — the grant you applied for, the customer who said “next week”, the investor who is “almost there”, the subscription renewals you hope will come back. It is visible, so nothing is hidden. But it is never added to the balance. If it arrives, it moves up into money in. If it does not, the balance never counted it in the first place. This is the rule that separates the sheet from the bank balance: the balance only ever counts money you can defend.

Build it anywhere: a spreadsheet, a notebook, a whiteboard. Columns are the thirteen weeks, starting on Monday. Rows are money in, money out, balance — and maybe. Start week one with today's bank balance. That is the whole tool. It takes one page, and it takes thirty minutes a week to keep alive.

04 — HOW TO FILL IT

Thirty minutes, every Monday.

The sheet only works if it is fresh. A forecast made a month ago is history, not a plan. So the habit is fixed: Monday morning, thirty minutes, before the week starts. Monday matters because the week is still ahead of you — a decision made on Monday can still land a payment or cut a cost this week. Friday is too late. The week is spent.

  • 1. Update last week with what actually happened. Replace the forecast with the real numbers: what came in, what went out. If a payment slipped, move it to the week it will actually arrive — and ask the customer when, in writing, so the new date is a commitment and not a guess.
  • 2. Roll the sheet forward one week. Drop the week that just passed and add a new week thirteen at the end. The sheet always looks thirteen weeks ahead, never fewer.
  • 3. Read three numbers. The lowest balance in the thirteen weeks. The first week the balance goes below zero — call it the danger week. And how many weeks of cover you have at current spending. Write the danger week down, with its date. A danger week with a date on it is a problem you can work on.
  • 4. Decide one fix before you close the sheet. Pull a payment forward, push a cost back, cut something, or start the conversation that brings money in. One fix, decided on Monday, beats five worries carried all week.

That is the whole ritual. Thirty minutes, four steps, one fix. The spreadsheet is not the product. The Monday habit is.

05 — APPLY IT

Run one real company through it.

Illustrative example. Chidi runs a four-person team in Enugu. His product tracks deliveries for market traders, and customers pay a monthly subscription. His bank balance today is ₦4,000,000. His salaries are ₦1,800,000 a month, paid in weeks 4, 8 and 12. His rent is ₦600,000 a quarter, due in week 6. His tools and hosting cost ₦200,000 a month, due in weeks 1, 5, 9 and 13. On the money-in side, one invoice is confirmed: ₦900,000 from a logistics customer, due in week 3. One contract is signed: ₦1,500,000 from a supermarket chain, first payment due in week 7. And ₦1,200,000 of subscription renewals are expected in week 11 — expected, not confirmed, so they sit in the maybe row and are never counted in the balance. Chidi is invented, and the numbers are illustrative — but the arithmetic is the point.

Read the balance column from the top. The company starts at ₦4 million and falls, week by week, to minus ₦200,000 in week 12. The danger week is week 12: salaries go out and the account is empty. The bank balance today says ₦4 million. The sheet says: you run out in twelve weeks, and the whole quarter depends on one hoped-for payment. Because if the ₦1,200,000 of renewals arrive in week 11 as hoped, week 12 becomes ₦1,000,000 and week 13 becomes ₦800,000 — no danger week at all. One hoped-for payment is the difference between paying salaries and not paying them.

That is what the sheet is for. Chidi can see the danger in week one, not week twelve. His fixes are small and early: chase the renewals now, in week one, and offer a small discount for paying in week nine instead of week eleven. Ask the landlord whether the rent can move by two weeks. Cut one tool that nobody uses. Any one of those removes the danger week. None of them is possible in week twelve, when the money is already gone.

The sheet is also a what-if machine. Change one date and watch the danger week move. If the supermarket's ₦1,500,000 slips from week 7 to week 9, week 8's salaries leave only ₦300,000 in the account — and a single unexpected bill in week 8, a tax demand or a broken laptop, is the difference between paying the team and not. The danger week is still week 12, but the margin for error has vanished a month early. That is why the Monday update matters: a slipped payment is the most common way a small company dies, and the sheet shows it the week it slips, not the week salaries bounce.

The line

A danger week with a date on it is a problem you can fix. A danger week you discover on payday is a crisis.

06 — WHAT COULD CHANGE THE ANSWER

Four limits on everything above.

First, the sheet is only as honest as the money-in row. If you count promises as cash, the sheet lies as politely as the bank balance did. The maybe row is the discipline. Every payment in money in should be one you can defend: an invoice issued, a contract signed, a renewal that has always arrived on time. If you are not sure, it goes in maybe.

Second, the sheet shows cash, not profit. A company can be profitable on paper and still run out of cash — customers pay in 90 days while suppliers want payment in 7. The sheet catches the timing gap. It does not fix a price that is too low, or a product that costs more to serve than it earns. For that, work the three numbers in our lesson on unit economics: contribution margin, CAC payback and burn multiple. Cash discipline and unit economics are two different tools, and a small team needs both.

Third, the sheet rots fast. A sheet updated once a month is a history document, not a forecast. The Monday habit is the product; the spreadsheet is just paper. If you skip three Mondays, throw the sheet away and rebuild it — a stale forecast gives false confidence, which is worse than no forecast at all.

Fourth, thirteen weeks is not thirteen months. Beyond a quarter, the numbers are guesses. For longer planning, read the sheet's direction instead: is the balance falling quarter by quarter? If it is, the company needs more revenue, not a longer forecast. That is the job of the revenue-first plan in our lesson on building revenue-first when your market stops getting funded — the sheet tells you when the money runs out, the plan tells you how to change that.

07 — THE MONDAY EXERCISE

Fill in the next 13 weeks this Monday, in 30 minutes.

  • Open a spreadsheet, a notebook or a whiteboard. Write today's bank balance at the top of week one.
  • List every confirmed payment in the next 13 weeks, with the week it arrives. Invoices issued, contracts signed, renewals that always arrive. Everything else goes in the maybe row.
  • List every cost in the next 13 weeks, on its real date: salaries on payday, rent on the due date, tools, suppliers, tax, loan repayments.
  • Work the balance week by week: last week's balance, plus money in, minus money out.
  • Find the lowest balance and the first week below zero. Write the danger week down, with its date.
  • Decide one fix before you close the sheet: pull a payment forward, push a cost back, cut something, or start the conversation that brings money in.
  • Put next Monday in the calendar now, same time, thirty minutes. The sheet only works if it is fresh.

If the first week below zero is inside the next 13 weeks, you have a cash problem with a date on it — and 13 weeks is a long time to fix a problem you can see. If there is no week below zero, you have something just as useful: proof, in writing, that the next quarter is funded, and the freedom to spend Monday on building instead of worrying.

THE TAKEAWAY

A bank balance tells you where you stand. A 13-week sheet tells you where you are going — and whether you will still be standing when you get there.

Evidence & limits

Paper Wealth, No Cash: How Early Founders Handle Employee Liquidity Before an Exit

Your earliest hire owns shares worth a small fortune on paper and cannot pay rent with them. That gap is a retention problem — and it arrives years before any exit.

Equity is a promise, not pay. It only becomes cash when someone buys the shares — and nobody owes your team a buyer.

“

Your first engineer took a smaller salary because you gave her shares. Three years later, those shares are worth a small fortune on paper. She cannot pay rent with them. She cannot sell them, because nobody is buying. And she has just realised that the promise she was paid in only pays out on a day nobody can name. That day is an exit: the company is sold, or its shares are listed on a stock market. Until then, her wealth is paper. This lesson is about the gap between paper and cash — three ways shares become cash before an exit, what each one costs, and the simple policy a small team should write before anyone asks for it.

01 — THE PROMISE AND THE GAP

Equity is a promise, not pay.

Equity is a slice of the company. When you give an employee shares, you are giving them a slice of whatever the company is worth one day. Most startups do not hand over the whole slice at once. The shares vest: the employee earns them over time, often 25% a year for four years. Leave after one year, and you keep a quarter of the promise. Stay four years, and the whole slice is yours.

The problem is not the slice. The problem is that a slice of a private company has no shop to sell it in. A public company's shares trade every day on a stock market. A startup's shares do not. They are worth whatever the last funding round said they were worth — a number on paper — until a buyer appears. That buyer can be a bigger company buying the startup, the public in a stock-market listing, a new investor in a funding round, the company itself, or a lender willing to lend against the shares. No buyer, no cash. That is the whole gap, in one sentence.

Founders talk about valuation. Employees think about rent. The founder says "your shares are worth ₦20 million now." The employee hears "I am ₦20 million rich." Both are wrong in the same direction. The shares are worth ₦20 million only if someone will pay ₦20 million for them. Today, nobody is offering. The longer a company stays private — and African companies stay private for a long time — the wider the gap grows, and the more it feels like a broken promise. That feeling is a retention problem. Your best people do not leave because they are ungrateful. They leave because the thing you paid them in cannot pay their bills, and they have started to notice.

✳
The rule

If you cannot name the day your team's shares become cash, do not talk about their value as if it were money. Talk about it as what it is: a promise with no date on it.

02 — WHY EXITS ARE RARER THAN THE PITCH SUGGESTS

Count the exits. Then plan as if yours may never come.

An exit is the day investors and employees get their money back: the company is bought, or it lists its shares on a stock market. A 2026 report from Stears and Ventures Platform counted 181 verified venture-backed exits across Africa between 2011 and 2026 — fifteen years of exits, on a continent that has funded thousands of startups. That is the total. Not per year. In total.

The shape of those exits matters as much as the count. Acquisitions — one company buying another — were 73% of all exits. Stock-market listings, the route every pitch deck dreams about, were a small minority. International buyers, who once took 56% of disclosed exits in 2020, took only 33% by 2025. And four countries — Nigeria, South Africa, Egypt and Kenya — accounted for 81% of disclosed exits. If you are building outside those four, or outside financial services (30% of exits), the exit door is narrower still.

There is one more number that should change how you plan. The report's "capital recycling ratio" — exits divided by investments in a year — rose from 0.032 in 2022 to 0.065 in 2025. That sounds like progress. It is mostly arithmetic: funding fell by a third over the same window while exits rose by about a third, so the ratio improved because the bottom number shrank, not because the top number grew. And the money that does come back is small. In June 2026, Launch Africa Ventures returned about $2.5 million in cash to its investors from eleven exits — roughly 7% of its $36.3 million fund. Eleven exits, and the return was 7% of one fund.

The famous exception proves the rule. Paystack's $200 million sale to Stripe in 2020 let its early employees turn shares into cash and Stripe shares. It is still cited, six years later, because so few exits like it have followed. Do not plan your team's finances on being the next Paystack. Plan as if the exit may never come — and then anything that does come is a bonus, not a rescue.

03 — THREE WAYS SHARES BECOME CASH BEFORE AN EXIT

Before the exit, there are three doors. Each has a price.

First, sell some shares when the company raises money. When new investors put money into a company, some of that money can buy shares from existing holders instead of buying new shares. This is called a secondary sale: the shares change hands, the company itself gets none of that money, and the employee gets cash. Moniepoint did this when it raised $110 million in October 2024 and became a unicorn — a startup valued at $1 billion or more. At least two senior employees sold part of their shares in the round, making $20,000 and $850,000. But note the rules: only employees with three years at the company could sell, there was a cap on how much each person could sell, and the employee who made $850,000 sold only a third of their shares. The shares were also sold at a discount to the unicorn valuation, because a buyer of second-hand shares pays less than the headline price — there are few buyers, and the seller has few options.

Arnergy, a Nigerian solar energy company, did the same thing in a smaller and more unusual way. Two months before it closed a $15 million round in April 2025 to complete its $18 million Series B, current and former employees were told they could sell part of their vested shares back to the company, at the valuation set in the funding round. The amounts were modest — hundreds to thousands of dollars; one three-year employee sold 500 shares, a third of her holding, for less than $1,000; a former vice-president of people and culture made almost $2,000. What made it unusual was who could sell: not just executives, but ordinary staff, including people who had already left. The company's chief executive said employee liquidity was a priority of the raise. And nobody sold all their shares — they kept the rest, still believing the company would grow.

Second, borrow against the shares. In October 2026, Flutterwave — the African payments company, valued at $3.25 billion — started a programme with Techdollar, a US private-credit company. Private credit means lending by a non-bank. Employees can pledge their vested shares as security for a loan, keep the shares, and keep any future gain if the company's value rises. TechCabal reported the loans run for six to twelve months and typically advance less than half the value of the pledged shares, with the money paid into accounts at Nuvion, a digital bank that already powers Flutterwave's stablecoin products. The catch is real: if the borrower cannot repay, the lender can seize and sell the pledged shares, and the shares can face margin calls — demands for more security if their value falls. The interest figures reported — 0.8% to 1.5% a month — are Techdollar's general pricing; the companies have not confirmed the exact terms for Flutterwave staff. And the scale matters: this works because Flutterwave is valued in the billions. A lender will only accept shares as security if the company is big and valuable enough to make the shares saleable.

Third, the company buys the shares back. Arnergy's case was also a buyback: the company itself was the buyer. A buyback is the simplest door to explain and the hardest to fund. The company pays cash from its own bank account to buy an employee's shares. That means it needs spare cash — the very thing a young startup usually lacks — and every naira spent buying back shares is a naira not spent on the business. Buybacks happen when a company is already making money, or when a raise leaves cash to spare. For a team of six with three months of runway, it is not an option at all.

04 — WHAT EACH WAY COSTS

The same cash, four different prices.

  • A secondary sale at a raise. Who pays: the new investor. Who decides: the company — it sets who qualifies, the cap, and how much of the round goes to employees. What the employee gives up: the shares sold, and usually a discount to the headline valuation. What the company gives up: part of the new money that could have gone into the business. When it works: at a real raise, with real new investors, and only for staff who have stayed long enough to earn it.
  • A share-backed loan. Who pays: the lender. Who decides: the lender — it values the shares and sets the terms. What the employee gives up: nothing yet, but the shares are at risk: miss a payment and they can be sold. What the company gives up: nothing in cash, but it must be big enough and valuable enough for a lender to bother. When it works: at a large, well-valued company with vested shares and a lender that understands private-company stock.
  • A company buyback. Who pays: the company. Who decides: the company — it chooses who, how much, and at what price. What the employee gives up: the shares sold. What the company gives up: cash, directly, from its own account. When it works: when the company is already earning, or a raise has left spare cash.
  • An exit. Who pays: the buyer, or the public in a listing. Who decides: the market — nobody in the company controls the date. What the employee gives up: nothing — this is the clean payday. What the company gives up: itself, in whole or in part. When it works: rarely, and on someone else's timetable. 181 times in fifteen years, across a whole continent.

The line

Every door before an exit trades something: a discount, an interest bill, the risk of losing the shares, or the company's own cash. There is no free way to turn paper into money. The question is which price your team can afford — and who decides.

05 — THE LIQUIDITY POLICY: FOUR QUESTIONS

Write it before anyone asks. Four questions, one page.

A liquidity policy is a short written answer to the question every employee will eventually ask: when can my shares become cash? It does not need a lawyer to start it — though a lawyer should check the share documents before you promise anything. It needs four honest answers.

  • 1. Who qualifies? Tenure and vesting decide this. Moniepoint's rule was three years at the company. Arnergy's options vested over four years, and the buyback was open to current and former staff. Pick a rule your team can understand: "after two years, and only on shares that have vested." Write the number down. A rule nobody can quote is not a rule.
  • 2. When can it happen? Name the moments, not a date. "At a funding round, if the investors agree to buy employee shares." "When the company buys shares back, which needs spare cash and a board decision." "Never, until an exit — and here is what we are doing about that." A moment is honest. A date is a promise you cannot keep.
  • 3. How much? Set a cap per person and a cap for the whole event. Moniepoint capped how much each employee could sell, and the biggest seller kept two-thirds of their shares. A cap protects the company from selling the team out of its own ownership, and it protects the employee from the temptation to sell everything at the first chance and regret it later.
  • 4. At what price? Say how the shares will be valued, before anyone needs to know. The last funding round's valuation is the usual answer, with a discount for second-hand shares — Moniepoint's were sold at a discount; Arnergy's were sold at the round's valuation. Either is defensible. What is not defensible is deciding the price on the day, when one side is desperate for cash.

Two rules sit underneath all four answers. First, write the policy before anyone asks for money — a policy written after the request looks like a refusal, however fair it is. Second, never promise a date. "We will let you sell at the next raise" is a promise about investors you do not control. "We will ask investors to set aside part of the next round for employee sales, and we will tell you what they say" is the truth.

06 — TWO FOUNDERS, ONE HIRE

The same offer, given two ways.

Illustrative examples. Ada hires a senior engineer and offers her 2% of the company, vesting over four years, on a salary below what a bank would pay. "The shares will be worth a fortune when we exit," Ada says. The engineer joins, works hard, and three years later asks when she can sell some shares to pay for her mother's surgery. Ada has no answer. There is no raise planned, no buyer, no policy. The engineer starts taking calls from recruiters. She does not leave because she is disloyal. She leaves because the promise had no date, and the emergency has one.

Bola hires the same engineer, on the same terms, but the offer letter carries one extra page: the liquidity policy. It says shares vest over four years; after two years, she may sell up to a third of her vested shares at a funding round, if investors agree to buy them; the company will ask investors to set aside part of every round for employee sales; the shares will be priced at the round's valuation, less a discount the investors set; and the company will not promise a date. Three years later, the same emergency arrives. Bola cannot conjure a buyer either. But the engineer knew the deal from the first day, and she stays — because the promise was honest, and honesty is what she was actually paid in.

The offer-stage conversation is five sentences. Say them out loud, to the next person you hire: "Your shares are worth something only when someone buys them. That day is usually a sale of the company or a stock-market listing, and those are rare. Before that, the only chances are a sale at a funding round, a loan against the shares, or the company buying them back — and none of those is guaranteed. Here is the policy we have written, and here is what it does and does not promise. If you need cash on a date, take the higher salary instead." The last sentence is the honest one. Some candidates will take the salary. Those are not the wrong hires. They are the hires who would have left angry in year three.

07 — WHAT COULD CHANGE THE ANSWER

Three limits on everything above.

First, every case in this lesson is a big company. Flutterwave, Moniepoint and Arnergy are all large, funded, and years old. A six-person startup cannot run a secondary sale, because no investor is buying its employees' shares. It cannot offer share-backed loans, because no lender will take its shares as security. And it cannot buy shares back, because it has no spare cash. If that is you, the honest policy is one line: "Your shares become cash at an exit, and exits are rare." That is still better than silence — and it is the reason to pay more cash salary and less equity in the early years, when the promise is thinnest.

Second, a policy is not legal advice. Share documents decide who can sell, to whom, and at what price — and they often forbid sales the founder never noticed. Before you promise anything in an offer letter, a lawyer should read the documents. This lesson explains the decision. It does not replace the legal check.

Third, the numbers in this lesson are reported, not audited. The exit count comes from a dataset of verified exits — unreported exits exist, and the true number is higher. The loan terms are a lender's general pricing, not confirmed terms for Flutterwave staff. The Moniepoint figures come from documents seen by one publication. Treat every figure as the best public record, not as a guarantee. For the companion half of the team question — who to hire, and in what order — read Your First Five Hires Decide Your Burn and Your Culture. That lesson is the hiring sequence; this one is what you owe the people you hire after they join.

08 — THE MONDAY CONVERSATION

One page, four answers, and one honest talk this week.

  • Write down, in one sentence, when your team's shares can become cash today. If the honest sentence is "only at an exit, and we have no exit planned," write that.
  • Answer the four policy questions — who qualifies, when, how much, at what price — on one page. If an answer is "we cannot do this yet," write that instead of leaving it blank.
  • Price the three doors before promising any of them: ask your lawyer what your share documents allow, ask your investors whether they would buy employee shares at the next round, and check whether any lender would take your shares as security (at your size, the answer is probably no — write that down too).
  • Tell the team the policy, in plain words, before the next hire starts. The conversation is five sentences — they are in section 06.
  • Put one line in the next offer letter: when the shares can become cash, and what is not promised. Then stop talking about the shares' value as if it were money.

If you do this and the policy says "nothing before an exit," you have not failed. You have told the truth, and the truth is what keeps good people. The failure is the founder who talks about paper millions for three years and has no answer on the day someone asks for cash.

THE TAKEAWAY

Shares are a promise with no date on it. Say there is no date — then write the policy that bridges the gap, and say the five honest sentences to the next person you hire.

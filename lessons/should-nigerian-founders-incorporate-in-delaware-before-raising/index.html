01 — THE COMMUNITY DISCUSSION

A founder’s post raised a useful question.

This lesson was prompted by a post from the.saint.tsx, a Nigerian founder sharing in the Dear Founders community—a space for founders to connect, exchange experiences, and discuss the practical realities of building businesses.

The post described an offer of warm introductions to angels investing at pre-seed and seed stage. It encouraged interested founders to have their data room, financial model, and pitch deck in order, while noting that the author was not pursuing investment at the moment and was not yet incorporated in Delaware.

That raises a useful question beyond this one situation: should Nigerian founders establish a Delaware company before they raise?

The post is the starting point for this guide, not a verdict on the founder’s decision. The right answer depends on the company, its target investors, and its plans.

02 — THREE KINDS OF READINESS

Separate business readiness from transaction requirements.

Business readiness

Can you explain the customer problem, why your solution is worth choosing, what evidence supports demand, how the business may earn revenue, and what the next stage of growth requires? Traction helps, but the relevant evidence differs by business and stage.

Due-diligence readiness

Can you quickly provide consistent, accurate records? Depending on the company and stage, that may include:

A USEFUL DATA ROOM STARTS HERE

  • A clear pitch deck and a realistic financial model.
  • Revenue, expense, cash-flow, and customer metrics—with assumptions identified.
  • Cap table and shareholder records.
  • Incorporation documents, material contracts, and intellectual-property ownership records.
  • A clear explanation of how much capital you are raising, why you need it, and which milestones it should fund.

A data room is useful when it makes credible evidence easy to inspect. A polished folder cannot compensate for weak fundamentals or numbers that do not reconcile.

Transaction readiness

Even a promising, well-documented company may need to address a particular investor’s legal structure, investment instrument, ownership records, or other deal requirements. This is where incorporation jurisdiction can become important.

03 — WHAT DELAWARE CHANGES

Why Delaware comes up so often.

Delaware is familiar to many US venture investors. Its corporate law, Court of Chancery, and established practices for venture-backed companies make it a common choice for startups seeking US venture capital. Standard US financing documents also have assumptions about the type of entity issuing them.

For example, Y Combinator publishes US-company SAFE documents and also provides certain forms for companies formed in Canada, the Cayman Islands, and Singapore. That illustrates both the importance of matching documents to the entity and the fact that financing structures are not identical across jurisdictions.

i
Delaware is not a universal legal requirement.

Some investors may require a Delaware C-Corp; others may consider a Nigerian company or another structure. Do not infer an investor’s policy from general startup advice—ask them directly.

04 — COSTS AND TRADE-OFFS

Friction now versus obligations later.

A Delaware parent company with a Nigerian operating subsidiary is one structure some cross-border startups consider. It can fit certain investment plans, but it is not a one-size-fits-all template. Restructuring an existing company can involve ownership, intellectual property, contracts, tax, and regulatory questions that need professional advice.

Setting up a US entity also brings ongoing obligations. Delaware requires corporations to file an annual report and pay franchise tax; the exact amount depends on the company and how the tax is calculated. Cross-border ownership and operations may also trigger US and Nigerian filing or tax considerations.

So neither “do it immediately” nor “leave it until the last minute” is a safe universal rule. The decision depends on how likely the funding path is, what investors require, and what ongoing obligations the company can sustain.

05 — WHEN TO ACT

Choose the next step based on your actual funding plans.

01
Bootstrapping, with no concrete funding plan

Focus on customers, revenue or validation, clean records, and a clear ownership structure. Do not create a foreign entity solely to look fundable.

02
You may raise in the next 6–18 months

Identify the types of investors you want to approach. Ask about jurisdiction, entity type, and instrument requirements early enough to compare ongoing costs with the cost and complexity of changing structure later.

03
A credible investor is already interested

Ask for their structural requirements before signing or promising a particular setup. Speak with qualified advisers who understand both the company’s current jurisdiction and the proposed US structure.

These are planning prompts, not legal or tax advice. A founder’s stage, current entity, shareholder arrangements, intellectual property, and investor terms can materially change the answer.

06 — BEFORE A WARM INTRO

Prepare for the conversation—not just the pitch.

FOUNDER READINESS CHECKLIST

  • Know your numbers: current revenue or validation evidence, costs, runway, and financial assumptions.
  • Know your ask: how much capital you are considering, what it funds, and which milestones it supports.
  • Organise your records: cap table, company documents, contracts, IP ownership, and key metrics.
  • Ask who is investing: stage, geography, sector, typical cheque size, and whether they invest in Nigerian entities.
  • Ask about structure: whether Delaware is mandatory before investment, can be handled as a condition of the deal, or is simply preferred.
  • Get advice before restructuring: understand ongoing costs and cross-border implications, not just the incorporation fee.

A warm introduction is an opportunity to learn as well as an opportunity to raise. If you are not seeking capital, it is reasonable to say so; you can still clarify what a future round would require.

07 — SOURCES & CAVEATS

Start with primary sources.

This guide draws on primary sources for the specific claims below. It is general founder education, not a substitute for legal, accounting, or tax advice tailored to your company.

  • Delaware Division of Corporations — How to Form a New Business Entity — official incorporation guidance.
  • Delaware Division of Revenue — Franchise Taxes — annual franchise-tax and report requirements.
  • Y Combinator — The SAFE — published financing documents for US companies and selected non-US jurisdictions.
  • US Internal Revenue Service — Foreign corporation filing responsibilities — examples of circumstances in which US tax filing questions may arise.

Rules and requirements can change. Verify current obligations with official sources and qualified advisers before making a corporate or tax decision.

THE TAKEAWAY

Prepare the business before you need the money. Investigate investor requirements before you need to restructure. Incorporate with a reason—not just to look fundable.

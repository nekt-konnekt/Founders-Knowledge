As founders, our singular job is to build, ship, and debug—time after time. But too many of us want to build the perfect product before launching or finding our first 100 users.

01 — THE PERFECTION TRAP

How long will you prepare to begin?

We want to debug every possible error a thousand times before shipping. We want to add 20 features before launch. We want to anticipate every problem our users might encounter.

That instinct often comes from a good place: we care about quality and want our product to work. But preparation can quietly turn into avoidance. Every new feature creates another thing to test. Every hypothetical problem creates another reason to delay.

!
There is a difference between quality and endless preparation.

You do not need to solve every possible problem before launch. You need to solve the problems that prevent your product from delivering its core value safely and reliably.

02 — SHIP THE CORE PROMISE

Build what makes the product useful first.

Do not spend months fixing hypothetical problems that real users may never encounter. Some usability issues, unexpected behaviours, and missing features only become clear when people start using the product in real conditions.

And do not add 20 features because you think users might need them. Ship the core product. Let real usage, feedback, and observed behaviour guide what you build next.

SHIPThe essential outcome

Make sure users can complete the main job your product promises to help them do.

LEARNThe next useful improvement

Use evidence from real people to decide which friction, feature, or workflow deserves attention.

Of course, test what matters. Protect user data. Fix critical security issues, data-loss risks, broken core workflows, and failures that could seriously harm users. Shipping early is not permission to ship carelessly.

03 — LET USERS TEACH YOU

Your first 100 users are an opportunity, not a trophy.

Before launch, much of what you believe about your product is still a hypothesis. Real users help you discover where the product makes sense, where it confuses people, what they value, and what they do not need.

WATCH WHAT PEOPLE ACTUALLY DO

  • Can they get started? Notice where onboarding or instructions slow them down.
  • Do they reach the core value? Find out whether the product helps them accomplish the job they came for.
  • Where do they struggle? Prioritise repeated, meaningful friction over isolated preferences.
  • What do they request—and why? Understand the problem behind a feature request before building it.
  • Do they return or recommend it? Look for signs that the product is useful beyond the first visit.

Do not blindly implement every request. Users can identify problems and express needs, but you still have to decide which solution fits the product and serves the wider audience.

04 — LEARN IN THE MARKET

What is the advantage of staying in the shadows?

It is good to stay ahead of your competition. But what is the point of building in the shadows while your competitors are already learning from their first 500 users?

They are collecting feedback, discovering what works, building relationships, and improving with real-world evidence. Meanwhile, you may still be preparing to launch.

Shipping first is not automatically winning. A competitor with more users may still be serving them poorly. But every day you delay useful feedback is a day you postpone learning whether your assumptions hold up outside your own head.

↗
Speed is useful when it increases learning.

The goal is not to launch anything at any cost. It is to get a responsible version into the hands of the right people and use what happens to make better decisions.

05 — YOUR LAUNCH CHECKLIST

Ship before perfect—but deliver your core promise.

Before launch, ask yourself:

  • Can a user complete the product's main task?
  • Are critical security, privacy, and data-integrity risks addressed?
  • Do the most important workflows work reliably enough for the intended use?
  • Can I observe feedback, diagnose failures, and release improvements?
  • Do I know who my first users are and how I will reach them?
  • Have I defined what I want to learn from the first release?

If the core promise is broken or users face serious avoidable risks, fix those first. If you are delaying because of optional features, cosmetic polish, or problems you cannot yet verify, consider a small, controlled launch and learn.

PUT IT TO WORK

Define your next shipping decision.

  1. Core promise: What is the main outcome the product must deliver?
  2. Launch blockers: Which known problems genuinely prevent that outcome or put users at risk?
  3. Not now: Which features or refinements can wait for evidence?
  4. First users: Who can try it and give you useful feedback?
  5. Learning loop: How will you observe, prioritise, fix, and ship the next version?
! Reality check

Early feedback can be noisy, and a small group may not represent the whole market. Look for repeated patterns, observe behaviour as well as opinions, and test important assumptions before scaling.

THE TAKEAWAY

Your first 100 users are not a reward for building the perfect product. They are an opportunity to learn what your product needs to become.

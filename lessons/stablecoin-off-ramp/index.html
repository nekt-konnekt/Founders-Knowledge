FOUNDER FIELD NOTE / PAYMENTS & CROSS-BORDER

Should you build on stablecoin rails? Decide at the off-ramp.

In October 2026, three of Africa's largest payment companies finished stress-testing the promise that stablecoins would cut cross-border costs 10x. The chain worked; the off-ramp didn't. Here is how to price a stablecoin corridor honestly — before you commit your product to it.

Gwera Kiwana sits on Launch Africa Ventures' investment committee and backs startups as an angel investor. Her observation this week: every cross-border fintech that pitches her is now a stablecoin startup. Stablecoins are table stakes for anyone building across borders. Over the past three years, three of Africa's largest multi-licensed payment companies — moving billions of dollars annually — each ran the experiment for real: pilot a stablecoin rail, promise 10x cheaper cross-border transfers. After stress-testing the off-ramp, each one settled on a mix of stablecoin and traditional bank rails. The honest post-mortem is worth your product roadmap: the blockchain worked, the off-ramp didn't.

01 — THE PITCH EVERY FOUNDER HEARS

The case is not imaginary. The benchmark is wrong.

The stablecoin case starts from a genuine fact. Sub-Saharan Africa remains the most expensive region in the world to send money to: an average of about 8.5% on a $200 transfer, against roughly 6% globally, according to the World Bank's Remittance Prices Worldwide data (the Q3 2025 reading measured 8.46% for the region and 6.36% globally). Chainalysis estimates that a $200 remittance from the region costs about 60% less when sent as a stablecoin. Against those two numbers, building on stablecoin rails looks like an obvious 10x.

But notice what the 60% estimate prices: the conversion off a single day's closing rate — a snapshot of the quoted price, not the price you actually get at size. And notice what the 8.5% benchmark assumes: that your customers are currently paying the regional average. The pilots found both assumptions wanting. This is the part to study before you commit.

02 — WHAT THE PILOTS ACTUALLY FOUND

The off-ramp receipt, line by line.

THE OFF-RAMP RECEIPT

  • The all-in cost, at real ticket sizes. Across the CFA region, at typical transfers of $100,000, spread, slippage and payout fees together exceeded 8%. The savings the pitch decks promised disappeared inside the conversion. (Figures as reported by Gwera Kiwana, TechCabal, 22 Oct 2026.)
  • Liquidity is a negotiation, not a market. Converting a dollar stablecoin into cedis or kwacha needs a counterparty who holds the local currency, wants dollars, and can trade at that moment, in that size. Outside a handful of the region's largest markets, that pair is thin — so prices get haggled over WhatsApp DMs, group chats and phone calls between treasury desks before a trade settles. Thin pairs produce slippage: the gap between the price you are quoted and the price you get, as a large order eats through the best offers and fills at progressively worse ones.
  • Compliance shrinks the market you can legally touch. The leading liquidity providers deal only with businesses that have passed know-your-business (KYB) checks — which keeps the pool clean and small. An open marketplace such as Binance P2P has more counterparties, but many are PSPs whose compliance teams cannot clear. Trade with unknown entities and you are inheriting someone else's compliance problem. The stricter your compliance, the shallower the market.
  • Your main venue can vanish. Binance delisted the naira from its P2P market in February 2024. Informal depth is not infrastructure — it is a favour that can be withdrawn.
  • The last mile still has fixed costs. Local payment rails carry fixed costs and regulatory requirements that only pay off at scale. Below that scale, volume routes through cross-border rails that can cost 10 times a local payment — and a stablecoin does not remove that cost, it moves it to the end of the journey.
  • The benchmark error. Pitch decks measure stablecoins against the 8.5% regional average. But the PSPs' clients were not choosing the average: digital-only money transfer operators charge 3.54%, and payouts into mobile wallets cost 3.92%, per the World Bank figures Kiwana cites. Against the rails your customers actually use, an 8% off-ramp is priced at twice the rate.

Notice the pattern. Every line of the receipt sits after the blockchain: in the FX pair, the counterparty, the compliance desk, the last-mile rail. Nothing in this list is a failure of the chain. The chain did its job. The jobs around it are where the money goes.

03 — FINALITY IS NOT SPENDABILITY

Every cross-border payment does three jobs. Stablecoins do one.

A cross-border payment has three jobs: messaging (agreeing who pays whom, how much), compliance (proving everyone involved is allowed to transact), and settlement into locally spendable money. Stablecoins handle the first job well and make the second no easier. On the third, a USDC transfer is final within seconds — but finality does not equal spendability. A trader in Lagos does not pay rent in USDT. A freelancer in Nairobi cannot pay school fees in USDC. Someone has to sell their naira or shillings, and someone has to deliver it.

!
The blockchain gives you finality. It does not give you naira or shillings

This is the mental model to carry into every stablecoin conversation. Settlement finality is a solved problem; it is also the cheapest part of the journey. The expensive part — a licensed counterparty holding the right currency, in size, at the moment you need it — is a local-liquidity business, and no chain speed makes it faster. Whoever makes local liquidity deep, licensed and boring will be the next breakthrough in African payments. Not whoever builds the fastest chain.

There is a second mechanism worth naming: the compliance paradox. Sub-Saharan Africa led the world in crypto growth through June 2026, driven by peer-to-peer activity, per Chainalysis as reported in the piece. Much of that visible depth is off-limits to a licensed PSP. So the venues with the most liquidity are the ones your compliance team cannot use, and the venues your compliance team can use are thin. That is not a bug in the strategy — it is the structure of the market. Price it in from the start.

04 — THE FOUR TESTS BEFORE YOU COMMIT

Run your corridor through these before you pitch the rail.

Before launching a stablecoin corridor for payments, the pilots leave behind four checks. They are the working version of “do not force it.”

THE CORRIDOR TEST

  • 1. Price conversions at your actual ticket size, on the worst day of the week. Not the headline rate, not the mid-week quote — your size, your worst day, all-in: spread, slippage, payout fees. A $200 remittance and a $100,000 trade settlement are different corridors with different costs. The 60% savings estimate was priced off a single day's closing rate; your customers will live in the other 364.
  • 2. Count the licensed counterparties you can actually use. Not the total depth of the venue — the depth your compliance team can legally touch. If that number is small, your corridor is a relationship business wearing a technology costume.
  • 3. Model what happens if your main venue disappears. Ask what your corridor costs the day after the deepest venue delists your currency. If the answer is “we find another one,” you do not have a corridor — you have a hope.
  • 4. Benchmark against the cheapest rail your customer already uses. Not the 8.5% regional average. The 3.54% digital-only operator. The 3.92% mobile-wallet payout. If your stablecoin corridor cannot beat the rail sitting on your customer's phone today, the revolution is a pitch deck, not a product.

Illustrative arithmetic — do your own version

Take a $100,000 payout into the CFA region. At the 8%+ all-in cost the pilots reported, the off-ramp takes $8,000 per transfer. The same transfer through a 3.92% mobile-wallet rail costs $3,920. These numbers are illustrative arithmetic from the reported figures — not a quote, and your corridor will differ — but they show the shape of the decision: at size, the off-ramp decides, and the chain is a rounding error.

05 — WHERE STABLECOINS STILL EARN THEIR PLACE

The hybrid answer is not a retreat. It is the product decision.

The pilots' conclusion — a mix of stablecoin rails where local liquidity is deep, and traditional bank rails where it is not — is less exciting than a revolution and more useful than one. It is also where stablecoins genuinely earn their place: corridors with deep, licensed liquidity on both ends; business treasury and dollar savings, where the point is holding value rather than converting it; and any corridor where the four tests above come back green.

The founder question is not “stablecoins: yes or no?” It is “which corridors, at what ticket sizes, with which counterparties?” Answer that corridor by corridor, and you are making a product decision. Answer it once, for all corridors, and you are making a branding decision.

THE TAKEAWAY

Price the corridor at the off-ramp, not on the chain — and never benchmark against an average your customer never pays.

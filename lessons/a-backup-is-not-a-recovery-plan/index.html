A backup is not a recovery plan.

A backup is useful only when you can restore what matters and prove the service works.

“

You have a database. Your hosting provider says it takes backups. Your code is in GitHub. Your website is live. You feel reasonably safe. Then someone deletes important records, a deployment breaks the application, or a cloud project becomes unusable. You discover that the backup exists, but you do not know whether it contains everything you need—or how to restore a working service.

01 — WHAT A BACKUP PROTECTS

A backup is a copy of data. Recovery is a business capability.

Modern software is rarely a single database on a single server. Even a relatively simple web application may depend on a database, uploaded files, application code, authentication, domain settings, scheduled jobs and external services.

Consider a marketplace. Its database stores users, listings, orders and transaction records. File storage holds product images and identity documents. A hosting platform runs the application. A payment provider keeps its own transaction records. DNS directs visitors to the website.

Now imagine restoring only the database. The records might return, but uploaded files could still be missing. Authentication settings might need to be configured again. Payment-provider records might not match the application's records. Background jobs could remain broken. You have restored a component—not necessarily the service your customers use.

Supabase documents that database backups do not include the actual objects stored through its Storage API. Its project restoration and cloning guidance also identifies settings and services that may need manual reconfiguration. See the Supabase backup documentation and project cloning guide. These are provider-specific details, not rules that automatically apply to every host.

!
Start with dependencies, not products

List what makes your product work, not just where your database lives.

Your inventory may include database records and roles, uploaded files, application code and deployment configuration, authentication settings, secrets, domains and DNS, scheduled jobs, webhooks, and external systems that maintain their own state. Identify what applies to your application and how each component would be restored.

02 — SET RECOVERY TARGETS

Decide how much data you can afford to lose.

Suppose your application takes a backup every night at midnight. A failure happens at 9 p.m. the following day. If you can restore only the midnight backup, you may lose almost 21 hours of changes: customer registrations, orders, inventory updates or records created during the day.

Whether that loss is tolerable depends on the product. Recreating a day's blog posts may be manageable. Losing a day's orders or transaction updates can create serious operational and reconciliation problems.

Recovery Point Objective (RPO)
How much recent data, measured in time, can the business afford to lose? An RPO of one hour means your recovery strategy should aim to limit lost changes to no more than an hour for the failure scenario you have defined.

Recovery Time Objective (RTO)
How long can the service remain unavailable? An RTO of four hours means the plan aims to restore the agreed service within four hours of the disruption.

These are targets, not guarantees that your infrastructure will automatically meet them. A low-risk internal tool may tolerate longer downtime and more data loss than a product processing time-sensitive transactions. Choose targets based on the consequences of failure, not on what sounds impressive.

If losing a day's work would seriously damage the business, daily backups alone may not provide the protection you need. More frequent backups or point-in-time recovery may be appropriate. If the business can tolerate longer downtime, paying for elaborate high-availability infrastructure may be unnecessary.

The goal is not to buy the most sophisticated recovery system. It is to build a recovery capability proportionate to the consequences of failure.

03 — KNOW YOUR PROVIDER'S LIMITS

Understand what your provider actually protects.

Managed infrastructure makes it easier to launch software. It also makes it easy to assume the provider handles more than it does.

Supabase's published backup documentation describes plan-dependent backup retention, recommends off-site database exports for Free Plan projects, and explains that standard database backups exclude stored file objects. It also warns that deleting a project permanently removes its associated data and stored backups. Review the current backup documentation for the terms that apply to your project. Provider policies change; do not generalise these limits to another service.

For each critical service, answer these questions:

  • Is backup automatic, or must I configure it?
  • What data and resources are included?
  • How long are backups retained?
  • Can I restore to a point before an accidental change or deletion?
  • Can I obtain a copy outside the provider?
  • What happens if the entire project or account is deleted?
  • Which settings, files or credentials must be recreated manually?

Write the answers down and link to official documentation. If your recovery strategy depends on a feature you have never tested, you have an assumption—not a verified recovery process.

04 — SEPARATE YOUR BACKUPS

Keep a way to recover outside your main failure point.

A backup stored only alongside the system it is meant to protect can share the same failure risk. If a project is accidentally deleted and the provider removes the associated backups, a copy that existed only inside that project may be gone too.

For important business data, consider maintaining a separate, securely stored copy outside the primary failure point. Depending on your needs, that might be an encrypted database export in a separate storage account, another environment, or a managed backup service.

Separation alone is not enough. Protect the backup location with appropriate access controls. Do not casually store recovery credentials alongside the files they unlock. If your business depends on uploaded documents, give those objects their own backup strategy.

You do not necessarily need a second production server running around the clock. A separate, verified backup may be the right first improvement. Let your recovery requirements and budget determine the architecture.

05 — TEST RESTORATION

The most important test is restoring the backup.

When did you last restore a backup and verify that the application worked? Not when did you last see a green checkmark, but when did you recover data into an environment where you could inspect and use it?

A backup can exist but be incomplete, inaccessible, outdated or difficult to restore. A restoration can complete while important parts of the application remain broken. PostgreSQL documents the procedures and options for restoring databases with pg_restore; the correct procedure depends on the backup format and environment.

Run a safe recovery drill

  1. Identify a backup and confirm its timestamp and coverage.
  2. Prepare an isolated test environment or separate recovery project.
  3. Restore the database using the provider's documented procedure.
  4. Restore or verify required uploaded files and storage objects.
  5. Check authentication, permissions, application configuration and secrets.
  6. Run important user workflows, such as signing in, creating a record and retrieving it.
  7. Record how long recovery took and what was missing or confusing.
  8. Update the recovery instructions and assign responsibility for the next test.

Do not test by deleting production data or experimenting on the live database. Use a separate environment. If your application triggers webhooks, scheduled jobs or other external actions, make sure the test cannot send real messages, charge customers or alter external records.

Then ask whether another authorised person could follow the instructions without needing the person who originally built the application. If the answer is no, your recovery plan still depends too heavily on individual knowledge.

06 — RECONCILE THE BUSINESS

Recovering the database is not the same as reconciling reality.

Some systems contain records that must agree with information held elsewhere. Consider a marketplace payment. Your application records an order as paid, but the payment provider keeps a separate transaction record. A restoration may return the database to a point before a later payment update was recorded, while the provider still knows that the payment succeeded.

If the business resumes processing without checking the discrepancy, it could mark a valid order as unpaid, retry a transaction unnecessarily or create an inaccurate account balance.

The same problem can arise with inventory, delivery status, subscription renewals, refunds and external integrations. Restoring an old database does not automatically reverse actions that have already occurred outside it.

?
Ask whether the recovered records match reality

For transactional products, define how you will compare internal records with authoritative external records and resolve differences safely.

For payment systems, that may mean reconciling orders with payment-provider transactions, verifying settlement records and checking whether webhooks or background jobs need to be replayed. The correct procedure depends on the provider and your architecture. Involve finance and operations—not only the developer restoring the database.

07 — RUN THE RECOVERY WORKFLOW

Restore the service in a controlled order. Do not just restore the database.

A recovery drill should prove that the people, data, application and external systems can work together again. Run it in an isolated environment with synthetic accounts and test credentials. Never use production deletion or a live payment as a rehearsal.

!
Protect the source before you restore

Do not overwrite the only surviving copy. Preserve the current state, restore to a separate target, and keep customer-facing writes and external side effects under control until the recovered system passes its checks.

Use this sequence in a drill or incident

  1. Declare the incident and contain it. Name an incident lead and technical lead. Record when the service first failed, when the team detected it, what customers cannot do, and which systems may be affected. Stop or queue risky writes where necessary. Pause workers, scheduled jobs and webhook consumers that could repeat an action while records are uncertain. Preserve logs and the affected system before making changes.
  2. Choose the recovery point. Identify the last known-good time before corruption, deletion or a bad change. Record the provider, project, branch, backup identifier, timestamp and timezone. Preserve a separate snapshot of the current state before restoring. The recovery point is not automatically the time of the last scheduled backup; establish which committed business events are present and which may be missing.
  3. Restore into an isolated target. Use a new branch, project or environment where possible. Restrict access to the people running the drill, use test credentials, and block outbound messages, live payment actions and production webhooks. Do not point customers or background workers at the restored system yet. If the provider cannot safely isolate external jobs, choose a different drill method or disable the relevant integration before starting.
  4. Rebuild the dependencies around the data. Restore or verify uploaded files and object storage; deploy the compatible application version; apply the required configuration and secrets; check identity and authentication settings; recreate permissions, domains and certificates; then verify queues, cron jobs, webhooks and integrations. Keep secrets out of the worksheet and source control. If credentials may have been exposed, rotate them and test the new credentials deliberately.
  5. Prove the recovered data is usable. Check that the expected schema and migrations are present, key tables can be queried, relationships and constraints behave, representative records are intact, and required files can be opened. Test roles and permissions, not just administrator access. Sign in with a synthetic customer account and complete the critical workflow. A successful restore command is not evidence that the application works.
  6. Reconcile what happened outside the database. Compare restored records with the systems that remained live: payment-provider transactions and settlements, refunds, deliveries, inventory movements, subscriptions, email or SMS actions, and partner systems. Use provider references and server-side verification where available. For example, Paystack's Verify Transaction API checks a transaction by reference; inspect the transaction status in the data object, not merely the API response status. Do not blindly replay old webhooks or jobs. Resolve exceptions with idempotency controls so a payment, refund, delivery or message is not applied twice.
  7. Measure the result against the targets. Record the time the incident began, the recovery point selected, the latest business event present after restore, the moment the critical customer workflow became usable, and the moment normal operations resumed. Calculate actual data loss and actual recovery time. Report the service-restoration time separately from the time required to reconcile all external records. If either target was missed, record it as missed; do not turn an estimate into a success claim.
  8. Make a return-to-service decision. The incident lead should confirm that the recovered database is the single source of truth, access controls work, files are available, critical workflows pass, payment and other external exceptions are understood, and queued jobs cannot create duplicates. Keep the old system read-only or otherwise fenced off before routing traffic to the recovered one. Re-enable workers and integrations in stages, monitor errors and reconciliation differences, and keep a clear rollback or containment decision.
  9. Tell customers what is true. Explain which actions are unavailable, whether a submitted action is saved or still being checked, and when the next update will arrive. Do not tell a customer that a payment failed, order disappeared or refund completed until the relevant system has been checked. Give support a named escalation owner and a list of unresolved cases.
  10. Close the drill with evidence. Save the timeline, restore point, validation results, measured RTO/RPO, unresolved gaps, owners and next actions. Rewrite the runbook where an instruction was missing or ambiguous. The next drill should test the gap you found, not merely repeat the easiest step.

Actual recovery point
The newest point in time for which the recovered system contains a complete set of committed business events. Compare the full set of events before the incident with the restored records and authoritative external systems; a later confirmed event does not prove that earlier events are present.

Actual data-loss gap
List every committed event that is missing after recovery, including the oldest missing event and its time. If gaps remain, report them separately instead of using the latest recovered timestamp to claim zero data loss.

Actual recovery time
Elapsed time from the agreed incident start to the point the defined critical customer workflow is usable again. Track full operational recovery separately if reconciliation or manual work continues afterwards.

Provider features are not interchangeable

Supabase: its database-backup guide (updated 12 November 2026) says database backups exclude Storage API objects and custom-role passwords; reset custom-role passwords after a restore. Its restore-to-new-project feature is labelled beta and requires a paid plan with physical backups enabled. Supabase calls it a database-only clone, but it also transfers Auth user records, database roles and selected configuration. Storage object bytes (only metadata is copied), Edge Functions, Auth settings and API keys, Realtime settings, integrations and other project settings still need manual reconfiguration. There is a further safety trap: the binary restore starts copied pg_net and pg_cron jobs as soon as it completes, with no pre-restore pause. If those jobs can call production endpoints, isolate outbound access before the restore. If you cannot, use a logical CLI restore and account for its trade-off: it lets you inspect or remove jobs first, but does not carry over the encryption root key, so Vault secrets and encrypted columns need separate handling. These are Supabase-specific behaviours. Read the database-backup guide, restore-to-new-project guide and Supabase's pg_cron isolation guidance.

Neon: its current instant-restore guide says restores work on both root and child branches. When restoring from another branch, however, the source branch must be a root branch. Restoring a root branch from another branch changes the target into a child, which removes access to snapshots and backup schedules on that target. A restore overwrites the branch's database timeline—including all databases on the branch—and is not a merge; Object Storage and Functions are outside that database timeline. Confirm the branch type, history window and overwrite scope before choosing a restore path. See Neon's instant-restore documentation.

Vercel: an application rollback is not a database restore. Vercel says rollback can leave configuration stale, does not roll back environment-variable changes, and restores cron definitions associated with the target deployment. After a rollback, Vercel turns off automatic production-domain assignment, so later pushes will not replace the rollback until you undo it. External databases and APIs keep their own state. Check the domain, cron and environment-variable consequences before confirming a rollback. See Vercel's Instant Rollback guide.

Payments: after restoring an application database, compare internal orders with the provider's transaction records before fulfilment or retry. Paystack's server-side verification guide says to fulfil only when data.status is success and data.amount matches the expected amount in kobo; the top-level API response status only tells you whether the API call succeeded. Its current guide also says transaction webhooks are sent only for successful transactions. Check the provider's current behaviour and your own transaction records before replaying anything. See Paystack's verification documentation.

AWS Well-Architected guidance recommends restoring backups to a new location and checking that data is accessible, intact and within the workload's RTO/RPO. It specifically warns against restoring a backup without querying or retrieving data to prove it is usable. See AWS REL09-BP04: periodic recovery testing and AWS REL13-BP03: test the recovery implementation. These are engineering recommendations, not a universal legal requirement.

08 — PUT IT TO WORK

Build a one-page recovery plan.

  1. Critical workflows: identify the customer actions the business must restore first.
  2. Recovery sources: record database, file-storage, code and configuration backup coverage.
  3. Targets: set a business-approved RPO and RTO for each critical workflow.
  4. Isolated restore: document how to restore without overwriting the only surviving copy or triggering live side effects.
  5. Dependencies: include identity, secrets, permissions, domains, jobs, queues and external providers.
  6. Validation: write the exact records, files and customer workflows that must pass.
  7. Reconciliation: name the external systems that must be checked before normal operations resume.
  8. Customer communication: prepare a truthful status update and escalation route.
  9. Ownership: name the incident lead, technical owner, business approver and backup contact.
  10. Evidence: record actual recovery time, data-loss interval, gaps and the next drill date.

Use the Production Recovery Drill Worksheet (CSV) to record each scenario and its result.

! Reality check

A quarterly restoration drill may be a reasonable starting point for a small, low-risk application. Systems with faster-changing data or greater consequences of failure may need more frequent tests. Choose a schedule that fits your risk; this is not a universal standard.

THE TAKEAWAY

A backup is not proof that your business can recover. Know how much data you can afford to lose, understand what your provider protects, keep important copies outside the primary failure point, test restoration safely, and verify that the recovered system agrees with the real world.

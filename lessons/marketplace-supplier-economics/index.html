A marketplace can have customers, transactions, and revenue—and still have a business-model problem. Sometimes the warning comes from the people who make the service possible: suppliers who stop participating because the economics no longer work for them.

01 — THE LAGOS CASE

The transaction is not the whole business.

In October 2026, drivers represented by the Lagos State Council of the Amalgamated Union of App-Based Transporters of Nigeria (AUATON) began industrial action involving Bolt and inDrive. The union cited fares, commissions, deductions, welfare, safety, and the rising costs of operating vehicles. Reporting published on October 13 said the union had extended its shutdown through October 14. At the time of publication, I could not verify a settlement or independently establish the scale of disruption.

The platforms' positions were not identical. Bolt expressed willingness to engage with driver representatives. inDrive cited its stated 6.1% commission excluding VAT, fare negotiation between drivers and passengers, and the ability to skip ride requests without penalty. Those are the company's stated features—not independent proof that drivers' overall economics are sustainable.

The dispute is revealing because both sides can evaluate the same transaction differently. A platform might see a completed trip: a passenger found a ride, a driver accepted a request, payment was processed, and the marketplace generated activity.

A driver has a different calculation. The fare must be weighed against fuel, vehicle maintenance, tyres, insurance, financing or rental obligations, mobile data, time spent waiting, and the cost of using a vehicle that depreciates with every kilometre.

The platform measures a transaction. The driver experiences an operating business.

!
Keep the claims in proportion

The union's statements explain its grievances; platform statements explain the companies' positions. Neither, by itself, establishes representative net earnings, the number of drivers participating, or the overall level of service disruption.

A trip can be completed without being sufficiently profitable for the driver. A platform can collect revenue without proving that its suppliers earn sustainable returns. Those are risks to measure, not outcomes to assume.

02 — THE THREE-ECONOMICS TEST

Marketplace liquidity is not marketplace health.

A marketplace does more than match buyers and sellers. It shapes prices, fees, incentives, risk, information, and how easily participants can leave. A useful health check looks at three connected economic systems.

01 · CUSTOMER VALUE

Can customers reliably get what they came for?

Track repeat usage, fulfilment, waiting time, cancellations, complaints, and customer retention—not just sign-ups or first purchases.

02 · SUPPLIER VIABILITY

Can suppliers keep participating on worthwhile terms?

Measure net earnings per active hour, cost per completed job, utilisation, unpaid waiting time, supplier retention, and the costs included in each calculation.

03 · PLATFORM SUSTAINABILITY

Can the company earn enough to operate and improve?

Track contribution margin per transaction after relevant variable costs, acquisition and incentive spend, support costs, retention, and cash burn.

These systems affect one another. Lower prices may attract customers but reduce supplier earnings. Higher commissions may improve platform revenue per transaction but weaken supplier retention. Higher supplier payments may improve availability but expose a platform whose margins were already too thin.

There is no universal answer to these trade-offs. The founder's job is to understand them before making changes—not to assume that improving one metric improves the business.

03 — LOOK BEYOND THE COMMISSION RATE

One visible percentage cannot explain the whole business.

AUATON has called for a commission cap of no more than 10%. inDrive, meanwhile, cited a 6.1% commission excluding VAT. It is tempting to treat this as a debate about finding the correct percentage. But a commission rate alone cannot tell us whether a marketplace is economically healthy.

Consider an illustrative transaction worth ₦5,000. At a hypothetical 10% commission, the platform collects ₦500 and ₦4,500 remains before other relevant costs or deductions. This is arithmetic for illustration, not a claim about either platform's actual fare or commission.

How much does the supplier spend to complete the transaction? How long does the full job take, including travel to the pickup point and waiting? Does the supplier have financing or rental obligations? Is demand consistent enough to keep the asset productively employed? Does the platform provide enough incremental business to justify its fee?

The platform has its own calculation: payment processing, support, fraud prevention, incentives, technology, local operations, and the cost of acquiring and retaining customers. How much contribution remains after those costs?

A lower commission does not automatically guarantee supplier profitability. A higher commission does not automatically make a platform exploitative. The question is whether the value created and costs borne by each participant produce an arrangement that can endure.

✳
Measure the whole transaction

Track supplier net economics and platform contribution alongside customer outcomes. A headline rate is one input—not the conclusion.

04 — MEASURE USEFUL SUPPLY

Registered suppliers are not the same as available supply.

A marketplace might have thousands of drivers, merchants, technicians, or freelancers registered. That number says little about how many are active, available at the right time, accepting work, or returning consistently.

01Registered

Who has signed up?

02Active

Who has recently completed meaningful work?

03Available

Who can fulfil demand now, in the right place?

04Viable and retained

Who can and wants to keep participating over time?

A platform can recruit new suppliers while existing participants become less active. Its headline supply count may remain stable even as fulfilment becomes more difficult.

For ride-hailing, investigate availability by time and location, acceptance rates, cancellations, hours online, and completed trips per active hour. Compare those measures with driver-reported costs and net earnings.

For an African small-business marketplace, the equivalent might be merchants who consistently fulfil orders, remain profitable after fees and logistics, and continue restocking. For a freelance marketplace, it might be qualified professionals who respond to briefs, win paid work, and return for more.

Your real supply is not everyone who has joined. It is everyone who can and wants to keep delivering value under your current model.

05 — DESIGN FOR THE MARKET YOU SERVE

Localisation includes the economics.

In Lagos, drivers may need to account for fuel, traffic, vehicle repairs, financing, insurance, and mobile data. The relative weight of these expenses varies by vehicle, route, driver arrangement, and demand pattern. The union's claims identify categories worth investigating; they do not establish a verified average cost or net income for every driver.

If a business depends on imported equipment or spare parts, exchange-rate movements may affect supplier costs. In heavy traffic, time spent completing a job can matter as much as distance. If suppliers rely on financed assets, an idle day may still leave them with a repayment obligation.

Founders expanding across African markets should not assume a model that works in one city will work unchanged in another. Purchasing power, infrastructure, regulation, transport costs, payment behaviour, and supplier financing can differ substantially.

Localisation is not just about currency, language, or payment methods. It also means understanding the economics that determine whether participants can sustainably serve your customers.

06 — THE FOUNDER CHECK

Five questions to ask before a marketplace breaks.

01 · TAKE-HOME ECONOMICS

What does a typical supplier actually take home? Measure earnings after relevant operating costs. Segment by location, time, supplier type, and tenure. State which expenses are included and which remain unknown.

02 · PRODUCTIVE SUPPLY

How much registered supply is genuinely productive? Track active and available suppliers alongside completed work, fulfilment, and retention. If recruitment rises while productivity or retention deteriorates, investigate why.

03 · COST SHOCKS

What happens when supplier costs rise? Model scenarios involving fuel, equipment, financing, materials, or other major expenses. Explore pricing, utilisation, demand density, and operational efficiency.

04 · VALUE EXCHANGE

Can suppliers understand the value exchange? Make prices, commissions, deductions, and incentives understandable. Transparency will not resolve every disagreement, but opaque calculations make it harder to diagnose one.

05 · VOICE AND APPEALS

Can participants challenge decisions without losing access arbitrarily? Define how suspensions, payment issues, disputes, and appeals are handled. A marketplace needs protection against abuse and processes participants can understand.

These questions will not eliminate conflict. They help founders detect economic stress before it becomes an operational crisis.

THE TAKEAWAY

A marketplace designs the terms of participation.

The Lagos ride-hailing dispute is a reminder that digital marketplaces are not just apps connecting people. They are businesses that distribute value, costs, risk, and decision-making power among participants.

The technology can make a transaction possible. It cannot, on its own, make that transaction worthwhile for everyone involved.

Build dashboards that measure supplier viability alongside customer retention and platform margins. Make transaction economics understandable. Pay attention to the people whose work makes the product real. Treat persistent supplier dissatisfaction as a reason to examine the model, not merely a communications problem to manage.

✳
Remember this

A marketplace doesn't just connect supply and demand. It designs the economics under which both sides agree to keep showing up.

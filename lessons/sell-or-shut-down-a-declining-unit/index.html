FOUNDER FIELD NOTE / STRATEGY & UNIT ECONOMICS

Sell it or shut it down?

In October 2026, Onafriq shut down Baxi, its Nigerian agent-banking business, after trying — and failing — to sell it. The audited numbers tell the real story: revenue down 56%, goodwill written to zero, and a market where PoS terminals doubled while transactions barely moved. Here is how to read a declining unit, decide sell vs. shut vs. keep, and wind down cleanly.

On 16 October 2026, Onafriq — the pan-African payments company formerly known as MFS Africa — shut down Baxi, its Nigerian agent-banking business, five years after acquiring it. Months before the closure, it had been negotiating to sell the unit instead. The sale never closed. If you run a business with more than one line, this is the case study to keep: the numbers that forced the decision were visible long before the announcement, and the choice was never really “save or close” — it was sell, shut, or keep funding the bleed.

01 — WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED

The sale that never closed, then the shutdown.

Strip away the commentary and the sequence is short. In October 2021, MFS Africa announced the acquisition of Capricorn Digital, the company behind Baxi — then one of Nigeria's largest independent agent networks, with more than 90,000 agents and over $1.6 billion in transactions processed that year. The deal completed in March 2022 after Central Bank of Nigeria approval. The strategy was to connect Baxi's physical distribution to Onafriq's cross-border payments infrastructure.

THE NUMBERS THAT FORCED THE DECISION

  • Revenue collapse. Agency services revenue fell 56% to $13.4 million in 2024, from $30.4 million the year before — figures from Onafriq's audited UK financial statements, as reported by TechCabal.
  • Margin compression. Gross profit fell even faster — down 65%, from $13.4 million to $4.7 million. For every $100 of revenue, the unit kept about $35 in 2024, down from $44 the year before. Costs were cut (from $17 million to $8.7 million) and it still was not enough.
  • The honest write-down. Onafriq wrote off $2.4 million of goodwill tied to the Nigerian payments business in 2023, then the remaining $4.56 million in 2024 — goodwill written fully to zero. In accounting terms: the company no longer expected the business's future earnings to justify what it had paid.
  • Debt-funded float. By July 2026, the Nigerian subsidiary owed ₦13.8 billion to Zenith Bank and ₦6.1 billion to Stanbic IBTC in overdrafts — ₦19.9 billion (about $15 million) in total, per the same filings. The business was funding agent float and short-term agent credit from borrowed money.
  • The failed exit. During 2026, Onafriq Nigeria Payments entered a non-binding memorandum of terms with a purchaser for 100% of the agency business. As of 29 July 2026, when the accounts were approved, the sale was still unresolved — no agreed structure, timing, or price. By October, the agents — over 460,000 as of 2025 — were told to move their funds elsewhere within 30 days.

Notice what Onafriq kept: its Nigerian payments network and card-issuing services. Those were the parts of the group that grew in 2024 — money transfer fee revenue up 20% to $43.9 million, card fee revenue up 22%. The company did not retreat from Nigeria. It killed the unit and kept the platform.

02 — THE VALUE TRAP

The market looked healthy. Per-terminal, it was collapsing.

This is the part founders should study hardest, because the trap is general. According to Nigeria's payments switch NIBSS, the value of PoS transactions rose 79% to ₦18.78 trillion in the first quarter of 2026. Deployed PoS terminals more than doubled — from 2.45 million at the end of 2023 to 5.56 million at the end of 2024. On those two numbers, the market looks like a boom.

But transaction value and transaction volume are different things, and only one of them pays commissions. In 2024, PoS transaction volumes rose just 8% — from 1.4 billion to 1.5 billion — while deployed terminals rose 127%. The implied transactions per year-end terminal fell from about 571 in 2023 to about 270 in 2024: a 53% collapse. (Figures as reported by TechCabal from NIBSS data.)

!
Value lies when prices move; volume per unit tells the truth

Inflation and naira depreciation push up the average ticket size, so transaction value can grow while the number of transactions — the thing an agent network earns commissions on — barely moves. If you run a network business, the metric that matters is volume per unit of capacity: transactions per terminal, per agent, per outlet. When capacity doubles and volume grows 8%, every operator's economics deteriorate, even the good ones.

The mechanism is not mysterious. Terminals, agents, float credit, and the overdrafts funding them are fixed costs that do not scale down when volume stalls. More devices chasing the same transactions means thinner commissions per operator. And a structural shift was undercutting the model itself: account-to-account (A2A) transfers were displacing the cash-based transactions agents exist to serve — one of the causes Onafriq itself cited, alongside inflation, naira depreciation, and rising costs. A competitor at Moniepoint's scale — over one million active PoS terminals in Nigeria and six million businesses served in 2025 — made the smaller network's position harder still.

The lesson: a declining unit rarely announces itself with a single bad quarter. It shows up as a widening gap between the headline metric (value, total users, total terminals) and the per-unit metric (volume per terminal, margin per ₦100 of revenue). Watch the per-unit line. It is where the decision lives.

03 — SELL, SHUT, OR KEEP

Three options, each with a price and a clock.

Onafriq tried them in order: keep (fund the unit through 2024's collapse), sell (the 2026 MOU), shut (October). The framework below is what that sequence teaches — applied while the clock is still running, not after.

THE THREE OPTIONS, STATED PLAINLY

  • Sell — while there is something durable to sell. A buyer pays for future cash flows, not past effort. What was saleable here: 460,000+ agents, brand recognition, licences, and a physical footprint. But a sale needs the numbers to be defensible — revenue down 56% and goodwill at zero make a buyer discount brutally or walk. The non-binding MOU that sat unresolved for months is the tell: when the economics are visibly deteriorating, “non-binding” stays non-binding. The sale window closes before the shutdown decision arrives. If you might sell a unit, start the process while the trend is still arguable, not after the write-downs.
  • Shut — when the wind-down costs less than the bleed. A shutdown has a real, calculable cost: agent and customer fund migration, staff exits, creditor settlements (those ₦19.9 billion overdrafts do not disappear), licence and contract unwinds, and the management time all of it consumes. Compare that total, honestly, against another 12 months of operating losses plus the opportunity cost of leadership attention. Onafriq's shutdown also bought focus: the payments network and card issuance that were growing no longer share management bandwidth with a unit losing money.
  • Keep — only on three conditions, all three. The decline is cyclical, not structural (a bad year, not A2A transfers eating your category). You can fund the turnaround without starving the healthy units. And the unit is strategically load-bearing — it feeds customers, data, or distribution to the rest of the business. Baxi failed all three tests: the headwind was structural, the float was debt-funded, and the cross-border platform no longer needed the agent network to work.

One more discipline: separate the unit's P&L from the group's story. Onafriq's group had growing lines — money transfer fees, card fees — which can camouflage a dying unit for quarters. Run each unit on its own economics, with its own trend lines, reviewed on a fixed cadence. A unit that needs the group's growth to look acceptable is already a candidate for this framework.

04 — READ THE NUMBERS BEFORE THE FUNERAL

The dashboard that would have forced the conversation earlier.

Every number in section 01 was knowable before October 2026. Here is what to track monthly for any unit, so the sell/shut/keep decision happens on evidence rather than exhaustion:

  • Revenue trend, then margin trend. Revenue falling is a warning; gross profit falling faster than revenue is the verdict. Baxi's margin per ₦100 of revenue dropped from ₦44 to ₦35 in one year — the business was shrinking and getting less efficient as it shrank.
  • Goodwill and impairments. Write-downs are management admitting, in audited ink, that the future will not justify the past price. Treat any impairment as a mandatory strategy review, not an accounting footnote.
  • How the float is funded. If working capital — agent float, short-term credit — is increasingly funded by bank overdrafts rather than operating cash flow, the unit is borrowing to stand still. ₦19.9 billion in overdrafts was the balance sheet screaming.
  • Volume per unit of capacity. Transactions per terminal, orders per outlet, revenue per agent. When capacity grows faster than volume, competition is intensifying and your share of each transaction is thinning — regardless of what total market value says.
  • Value vs. volume divergence. In inflationary markets, value can grow while volume stalls. Always read both, and price your decisions on volume.
  • Structural vs. cyclical check. Ask: what would have to be true for this unit to recover? If the answer requires a technology trend to reverse (A2A transfers going away) or a competitor at 10x your scale to stumble, you are hoping, not planning.

05 — WIND DOWN WITHOUT BURNING TRUST

The shutdown is an operation, not an announcement.

Onafriq's wind-down had the right shape: agents were transferred to another principal bank and given 30 days to move their funds to a principal bank of their choice. Agent money moved first, and the company kept operating its other Nigerian services, so the shutdown read as a unit exit rather than a company collapse. The CEO's statement was candid without being defensive: “We entered Nigeria with conviction and commitment, but did not achieve the outcome we set out for.”

If you ever have to do this, run it as a checklist, in this order:

  • Other people's money first. Agent balances, customer funds, escrowed amounts — a migration path with a named destination and a deadline, before anything else.
  • Staff and creditors. Exits handled cleanly; overdrafts and payables settled or restructured with the lenders named, not hidden.
  • Contracts and licences. Principal-bank relationships, terminal leases, agent agreements — unwound or transferred, with the paperwork to prove it.
  • One honest statement. Say what happened, what you are keeping, and what happens to everyone affected. Candour in the announcement is what lets you keep the relationships — the agents, the banks, the regulators — you will need for whatever you build next.
  • Protect the healthy units. Ring-fence the shutdown operationally and narratively so it does not contaminate the parts of the business that are growing.
THE TAKEAWAY

A declining unit is a decision with a clock on it. Measure per-unit economics, price the sale window honestly — it closes before the shutdown decision arrives — and never let the announcement be the first time you planned the exit.

The framework is drawn from this case plus general unit-economics reasoning — apply it to your own numbers, not to Baxi's headlines.

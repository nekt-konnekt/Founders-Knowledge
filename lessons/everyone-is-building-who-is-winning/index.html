FOUNDER FIELD NOTE / AI, DISTRIBUTION & CAPITAL

Everyone is building. Who is actually winning?

AI has made it easier to build a product. It hasn't made it easier to build a business. The next bottleneck is getting people to choose you.

AI is making it cheaper to turn an idea into software. But when more people can build, the scarce advantage shifts: knowing what deserves to be built, reaching the right customers, and earning their trust.

01 — THE FIRST BOTTLENECK IS MOVING

The launch is becoming less impressive.

Open GitHub. Browse a product-launch platform. Spend a few minutes on social media. Someone has launched an AI agent, built a SaaS product over a weekend, or turned a business problem into a working application with an AI coding assistant.

GitHub recorded more than 36 million new developer accounts in 2025 and more than 230 new repositories per minute. Those figures measure developer and repository activity, not startup launches. They are still a useful signal of the scale of software creation. Read GitHub's Octoverse reports ↗

For founders, this is good news. More people can test ideas without first raising money or assembling an engineering team. A working prototype can be within reach much earlier.

But the existence of a product tells us less about the strength of the business behind it than it once did. A polished dashboard does not prove customers need it. A successful deployment does not prove that the business can acquire customers profitably. Launch-day attention does not prove repeat use.

✳
Separate product progress from business progress

Shipping proves that you made something work. Demand, retention, and payment help show whether it matters to a market.

02 — CAPITAL IS STILL SELECTIVE

The money has not become unlimited.

It is tempting to assume that more products will eventually mean more funded startups. Investment does not work that way.

Africa: The Big Deal reported approximately $3.2 billion in African startup funding during 2025, excluding exits. Almost 500 ventures raised at least $100,000, and eight companies announced rounds exceeding $100 million. The four largest startup markets—Egypt, Kenya, Nigeria and South Africa—captured 82% of tracked funding. Explore Africa: The Big Deal's reporting ↗

These figures describe a market with meaningful capital, but uneven access. A large total can coexist with difficult fundraising conditions for early-stage founders. The numbers do not tell us that every promising founder is being overlooked; they do show why a headline funding total should not be mistaken for broad access to capital.

This is where the unicorn conversation becomes interesting. Large startups can attract large cheques because investors believe they can deliver returns at scale. Their funding may reflect genuine demand, execution, infrastructure needs, or the cost of entering a market. We should not assume that successful companies are undeserving simply because they have already raised money.

The harder question is whether the ecosystem is getting better at discovering promising companies early—or increasingly relying on a small group of already-visible businesses to represent the success of an entire market.

!
Concentration is a question to investigate, not proof of wrongdoing

To establish that large rounds crowd out smaller founders, we would need evidence about investor mandates, deal allocation, stage-specific supply, and viable companies that could not get financed.

03 — THE BOTTLENECK IS MOVING

Distribution is the harder problem.

Imagine two founders building software for small businesses. One spends three months developing a broad platform with multiple dashboards, integrations, and AI features. The other talks to 20 business owners, finds a repetitive problem, builds a narrow solution, and gets five customers to pay while learning what they actually need.

These are illustrative examples, not a benchmark. The first founder may have built more software. The second has started gathering evidence about a business.

Distribution is how customers discover a product, assess it, trust the seller, pay, and decide whether to return or recommend it. It is not just a marketing department's job. It is a business capability.

In many African markets, distribution can depend on trusted relationships, trade groups, professional associations, schools, churches, local resellers, referrals, payment habits, language, logistics, and the way a customer already works. Digital advertising and online content can help, but they are only some of the routes to market.

A founder who understands how a specific group makes decisions may have a stronger starting position than a competitor with a larger advertising budget and a more sophisticated product.

04 — START WITH THE MARKET YOU CAN REACH

Africa's opportunity is not to build more copies.

AI tools make it easier to reproduce familiar software models. That can be useful: proven approaches can give founders a starting point. But copying a product is not the same as understanding the market it serves.

Consider problems that remain expensive, inconvenient, or poorly served in your own environment. How does a school manage attendance and communicate with parents? How does a distributor track inventory across informal retail networks? How does a small business reconcile payments across several channels? How does an association manage members and dues without buying an enterprise system it cannot afford?

These are examples of questions to investigate, not proof that a market is waiting. Talk to the people who experience the problem. Learn what they do today, what the problem costs, who decides to buy, and what would make them change.

The goal is not to avoid global opportunities. It is to avoid confusing global attention with local demand.

05 — CHANGE WHAT COUNTS AS PROGRESS

How founders can respond.

Start with a market you can reach. Choose a specific group of customers you can speak with, observe, and serve. Access to those customers gives you a way to test assumptions before committing months to development.

Validate behaviour, not compliments. Ask about a recent real problem. Look for repeated workarounds, existing spending, a pilot, a referral to a decision-maker, or a willingness to pay. No single signal proves a market, but actions are usually more informative than praise.

Build distribution into the plan. Identify how your customers already discover solutions and decide whom to trust. Partnerships, referrals, community relationships, and existing workflows may be as important as the software itself.

Use AI to learn faster, not just to produce more. Prototype alternatives, analyse feedback, automate a tedious task, or reduce delivery costs. The goal is not to launch the greatest number of products; it is to discover which problems can support a sustainable business.

Choose financing that fits. Venture capital can suit companies pursuing large markets that need rapid scaling. Other businesses may initially grow through customer revenue, service contracts, or strategic partnerships. Funding is a tool, not a universal definition of success.

Measure what survives launch. Track repeat use, retention, revenue quality, margins, referrals, and the cost of acquiring customers. Those measures tell you more about business health than launch-day attention alone.

PUT IT TO WORK

Your next step: run a distribution test.

Before adding another feature, write down the following:

  1. Customer: Which specific group has the problem?
  2. Problem: What are they doing today, and what does it cost them?
  3. Channel: Where do they already seek help or buy similar solutions?
  4. Commitment: What concrete action would show genuine interest—a paid pilot, a repeat session, a referral, or time spent testing?
  5. Decision: What result would make you change the product, channel, price, or target customer?
! Keep the evidence honest

A handful of conversations can reveal assumptions worth testing, but they are not statistical proof. Record what you observed separately from what you infer, and keep looking for evidence that could change your mind.

THE TAKEAWAY

When software becomes easier to reproduce, knowing what deserves to be built—and earning the right to grow—becomes more valuable.

EVIDENCE & LIMITS

What this is based on.

This is an editorial analysis, not a count of daily startup launches. Developer accounts and repository creation are activity indicators, not startup counts. African funding totals cover tracked transactions and do not measure every source of business finance.

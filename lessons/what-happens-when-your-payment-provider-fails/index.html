01 — UNDERSTAND THE FAILURE

A payment error is not always a failed payment.

Your customer is ready to pay. They enter their details, confirm the transaction, and wait. Then an error appears. Was the payment declined? Was the money deducted? Should they try again?

A payment can depend on several systems working together: your application, payment gateway, processing partner, bank, card network, or transfer infrastructure. A problem in one part can disrupt the entire experience.

In October 2026, Paystack's public status history recorded disruptions affecting Nigerian card processing and Ghanaian bank transfers. These incidents were marked resolved. They are useful examples of why payment resilience matters—not evidence that every provider is unreliable.

Three situations matter:

AConfirmed failure

The provider confirms the transaction was unsuccessful. Explain the result and offer an appropriate next step.

BStill processing

The provider has not confirmed the final outcome. Keep the transaction pending until its state can be established.

CUnknown outcome

Your application timed out or lost its connection. The payment may have succeeded even if your app never received confirmation.

The lesson: an error message is not always proof that a transaction failed.

02 — BUILD FOR THE FULL LIFECYCLE

Do not build only for the happy path.

Many founders test one successful checkout, see the confirmation screen, and move on. A reliable payment flow must also handle delayed notifications, incomplete transactions, repeated requests, and recovery.

  • Create a unique reference for every payment attempt.
  • Record the amount, currency, customer, order, and transaction status.
  • Verify payments through the provider's supported server-side verification mechanism.
  • Validate payment notifications securely and handle repeated notifications without fulfilling an order twice.
  • Reconcile transactions that remain pending or have an unknown outcome.

For providers that support idempotency, use it according to their documentation. Idempotency can help prevent repeated requests from creating duplicate operations, but it does not replace verification or reconciliation.

!
Do not trust the success page alone

Do not mark an order as paid solely because the browser reached a success URL. Confirm the payment using a trusted server-side process before granting paid access or fulfilling the order.

03 — CHOOSE THE RIGHT RECOVERY

Match your response to the actual state.

  • Confirmed failure: explain the result and offer an appropriate retry or alternative method.
  • Pending transaction: show that confirmation is pending and provide a way to check the status.
  • Unknown outcome: check the existing transaction before initiating another charge.
  • Payment succeeded but the order did not update: reconcile the payment and complete the order safely.
  • Duplicate notification: process it safely so the same order is not fulfilled twice.

Imagine a customer paying ₦25,000 for a service. Their bank debits the money, but your application displays an error. If your only instruction is “Try again,” the customer may pay twice.

04 — SHOULD YOU ADD A SECOND PROVIDER?

Redundancy is useful only when you can operate it.

A second provider may give customers another way to pay, but it also introduces integration work, reconciliation complexity, support requirements, and new failure modes. Two providers may even share banks, processors, card networks, or other dependencies.

01Low transaction volume

Start with clear status handling, verification, and a manual reconciliation procedure.

02Growing transaction volume

Add monitoring, automated reconciliation, and tested recovery workflows.

03Business-critical payments

Evaluate alternative providers, shared dependencies, failover behaviour, and the cost of maintaining redundancy.

Before integrating a second provider, ask:

  • How costly is payment failure for this business?
  • Is the failure isolated to a provider, payment method, or market?
  • Can I independently verify that the alternative method is available and operational?
  • Can the team reconcile payments across both providers and prevent duplicate fulfilment?

Do not buy complexity before you understand the problem it is supposed to solve.

05 — YOUR INCIDENT CHECKLIST

Prepare before the dashboard fills with errors.

Before an incident
  • Subscribe to your provider's status notifications.
  • Document transaction verification, webhook, and refund procedures.
  • Make sure payment references can be traced to orders.
  • Test failed, pending, delayed, and repeated notifications.
  • Assign responsibility for customer communication and reconciliation.
During an incident
  • Check the official provider status page.
  • Identify affected methods, markets, and transactions.
  • Avoid blindly repeating transactions with unknown outcomes.
  • Communicate clearly and keep a record of unresolved cases.
  • Use an alternative only if it is supported, available, and operational.
After an incident
  • Reconcile pending and apparently failed payments.
  • Find successful payments that did not trigger order fulfilment.
  • Check for duplicate charges or duplicate orders.
  • Process eligible refunds and review the customer impact.
  • Improve the incident procedure based on what actually happened.

06 — MEASURE WHAT MATTERS

A green dashboard can still hide a customer problem.

Monitor a few useful indicators and break them down by provider, payment method, and market where possible:

  • Payment success rate: what proportion of initiated transactions succeed?
  • Pending transaction age: how long do transactions remain unresolved?
  • Confirmation delay: how long before successful payments update orders?
  • Duplicate-payment incidents: how often are customers charged more than once for one intended purchase?
  • Reconciliation backlog: how many transactions need investigation?
  • Customer impact: how many customers contact support about payment problems?

A general success rate can conceal a problem affecting only one card scheme or country. Monitoring should help you locate the source of a problem, not merely tell you that something went wrong.

YOUR NEXT STEP

Answer three questions before the next incident.

Write down how your team will establish what happened to the customer's money, what should happen to the order, and what to do if neither answer can be confirmed immediately.

List at home or list abroad?

In October 2026, MNT-Halan listed on the Egyptian Exchange, Airtel Money on the London Stock Exchange, and OPay filed to list in New York. The headlines say “raised millions”. The offer documents tell a more useful story: what was actually sold, to whom, and at what price.

October 2026 gave African fintech its clearest public exit data in years. Three companies, three venues, three different books. If you read only the headlines, you will conclude that a listing equals a fundraise and that a foreign exchange is the prize. Both conclusions are wrong in at least two of the three cases — and the corrections are exactly what a founder planning five years ahead needs to understand.

01 — THE HEADLINES, STATED PLAINLY

What actually happened this month.

Strip away the commentary and three distinct events remain. The details below come from the deal advisers' announcements, company statements reported by reputable outlets, and — in OPay's case — the company's own regulatory filing as summarised by the press.

THREE LISTINGS, THREE MECHANICS

  • MNT-Halan — Egyptian Exchange (EGX), ticker HALN.CA. Trading began 20 October 2026. The offer was 320 million existing shares — 20% of issued capital — at EGP 24.50 per share: 272 million placed privately with institutions and 48 million offered to the Egyptian public. Institutional demand was 11.2 times the shares on offer (19.4 times excluding cornerstone investors), from more than 300 investors across more than 10 countries; retail demand was 43.6 times, with more than 41,500 subscription orders. (Gibson Dunn, deal adviser)
  • Airtel Money — London Stock Exchange (LSE), ticker AMC. Conditional trading began 9 October 2026 at £1.96 per share, implying about £5.3 billion (roughly $7 billion); admission to the Official List was expected on 14 October. The offer was 270 million existing shares sold by minority shareholders, plus up to 27 million more from Mastercard Asia/Pacific if demand required — up to £582 million in total, about 11% of the company's capital. The IFC took 34,285,714 shares (£67.2 million) as a cornerstone investor. (The Hindu BusinessLine; Devdiscourse)
  • OPay — NYSE filing, not yet a listing. The company's F-1 was made public on 9 October 2026 for a listing under the symbol “OPAY”. The price range, share count and listing date are not set, and the listing still needs NYSE approval. The filing discloses 50.1 million monthly and 26.8 million daily users as of 31 July 2026; revenue of $205.7 million in 2024 rising to $536.3 million in 2025; a $50.8 million loss in 2024 turning into a $72.5 million profit in 2025; and $467.1 million in revenue with $90.9 million profit in the first half of 2026. Nigeria produced 88.1% of 2025 revenue. Stanbic Africa Holdings agreed to buy up to $200 million of shares in a separate private sale. (TechCity, summarising the SEC filing)

Notice what the three have in common: all are fintechs with enormous African user bases, and all three chose public markets as the next step. Notice what differs: the venue, the offer structure, and — most importantly — whose shares were being sold.

02 — READ THE OFFER, NOT THE HEADLINE

A listing is not automatically a fundraise.

The most consequential sentence in the MNT-Halan announcement is easy to miss: the shares were sold by MNT Investments B.V., the selling shareholder — not by the company. This was a secondary sale: existing shareholders cashing out, not the company issuing new shares to fund growth. The roughly EGP 7.84 billion involved (about $150 million at the conversion used by Tech In Africa) went to the seller, not into MNT-Halan's accounts.

The same is true of Airtel Money. Its IPO was “the sale of 270 million existing shares by minority shareholders,” with the explicit note that it was a secondary offering — no new capital was to be raised by Airtel Money itself.

So when a headline says “MNT-Halan raises $150 million in IPO,” read it as shorthand for “early shareholders sold $150 million of stock.” That is a real achievement — it means there was a liquid market willing to buy — but it is a different achievement from raising growth capital. Confusing the two is how founders end up planning a listing to fund expansion and discovering, too late, that their offer structure delivers liquidity to investors instead.

!
The primary piece, if you need one, must be built in deliberately

MNT-Halan's transaction does include a provision for a closed subscription by MNT Investments B.V. of up to EGP 4 billion in new shares at the offer price after the offering completes (Gibson Dunn). That is the part that would put fresh capital into the company — and as of this writing, it is an announced provision, not a confirmed completed subscription. If your listing must fund growth, the primary component has to be designed into the offer from the start; it does not happen by accident.

OPay's case adds a second correction: a filed F-1 is not a listing. The price range and share count are blank and NYSE approval is still pending. Filings get announced as if the listing happened; the actual pricing — where the market says what it will pay — is still to come.

03 — HOME OR ABROAD: THE VENUE DECISION

Choose the venue for the book you can build.

The two completed listings are a natural A/B test of venue strategy, and the results cut against the reflex that a foreign exchange is always the prize.

MNT-Halan listed where its customers live. The company combines its app with more than 1,200 physical distribution points across 25 Egyptian governorates (Gibson Dunn) — which means the brand was already known to the people being asked to buy shares. A 43.6-times retail oversubscription is what it looks like when a customer base converts into an investor base. Home-market familiarity did real work in that book.

Airtel Money went to London and priced at about $7 billion — below the $8–9 billion range reported when its intention to float was confirmed (FinanceTracked, reported). That gap is the cost of accessing deeper institutional pools: foreign institutions price African risk harder than a home crowd that uses the product. The offer still cleared, with a £67.2 million cornerstone from the IFC and eligibility for FTSE UK indices on the table — but the discount is part of the trade, not a footnote to it.

VENUE TRADE-OFFS, STATED PLAINLY

  • Home exchange: investors already know the brand; retail demand can be extraordinary; currency matches operations; listing and ongoing disclosure costs are typically lower. The constraint is a smaller institutional pool and less hard-currency liquidity for foreign investors.
  • Foreign exchange: deeper institutional pools, hard-currency investors, index eligibility (Airtel Money's expected FTSE inclusion), and a global valuation benchmark. The price is a harder bargain on valuation, heavier disclosure and ongoing compliance, and a retail book that is foreign too — Airtel Money's retail offer was UK-only via RetailBook, minimum £250 per investor (Business Post).

The decision rule: pick the venue where your natural book already lives. If the people who trust the business — customers, local banks, regional institutions — can buy shares at home, a home listing can clear enormous demand without the foreign discount. If you need deep institutional pools and hard-currency capital, go abroad with eyes open and price for it. Prestige is not a strategy; a clearing book is.

04 — THE BOOK IS THE VERDICT

Oversubscription is the evidence; valuation is the opinion.

Founders tend to fixate on the valuation number. The professionals running these offers fixate on the book — who committed, in what size, and at what multiple of the shares on offer. The book is the evidence that demand is real; the valuation is one reading of it.

Both October listings show how a book is built in layers. First the anchors: Commercial International Bank and Redwheel together took more than a third of MNT-Halan's offer as cornerstone investors (Tech In Africa); the IFC's £67.2 million anchored Airtel Money's. Anchors de-risk the offer for everyone behind them. Then the institutions: 11.2 times overall for MNT-Halan, 19.4 times excluding the cornerstones (Gibson Dunn) — demand beyond the anchors, which is the number that actually proves breadth. Then retail: 43.6 times, 41,500+ orders.

There is a private-markets mirror of this sequence that any founder can use long before an IPO: secure the anchor investor first, let their commitment pull in the rest of the round, and treat broad participation as validation rather than the starting point. The mechanics of a book build do not change just because the round is private.

One caution on reading debut-day moves: Tech In Africa reports MNT-Halan's shares rose as much as 18% on the first session, from EGP 24.50 to EGP 28.91; one outlet reports Airtel Money slipped slightly below its £1.96 offer price in early conditional trading (business-news-today). Neither is independently verified here, and neither predicts year one. A pop means the offer was priced to clear with room to spare — which is good pricing discipline, not a valuation verdict. Judge the listing by the book it built and the price discipline it showed.

05 — WHAT TO DO YEARS BEFORE A LISTING

The listing is the exam; the preparation is the syllabus.

None of October's three stories started in October. OPay's F-1 discloses years of audited-style numbers — revenue tripling from 2024, the swing from loss to profit — because public investors buy a track record, not a narrative. If a listing is anywhere in your five-year plan, the work starts now:

  • Decide what the listing is for. Liquidity for early backers, primary growth capital, or both. The offer structure — secondary, primary, or a mix like MNT-Halan's EGP 4 billion provision — follows from that decision.
  • Get auditable early. Clean, audited financials across several years cannot be manufactured in a quarter. Start the discipline while you are private.
  • Build relationships with your natural book. Local banks, development finance institutions (the IFC anchored Airtel Money), and the customers who could become retail investors. MNT-Halan's 1,200 distribution points were a business asset that doubled as an investor-relations asset.
  • Keep the cap table clean and governance real. Exchanges, cornerstone investors and regulators diligence this; surprises here delay or kill listings.
  • Study your peers' public documents. MNT-Halan's FRA prospectus, Airtel Money's LSE prospectus and OPay's F-1 are public. Read the risk sections first — they show what sophisticated investors actually worry about, which is precisely what you should be fixing now.
  • Price to clear, not to impress. A cleared book at a deliberate price beats a failed book at an ambitious one. The discount is a cost of the venue; budget for it.
THE TAKEAWAY

A listing sells shares to investors. Whether it is a triumph depends on whose shares, to whom, and at what price. Build the book first — the venue follows.

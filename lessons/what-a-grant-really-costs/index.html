What a Grant Really Costs: The Hidden Work Behind Free Money

On 10 November 2026, eight young Nigerian founders stood on a stage in Abuja and each took home ₦5 million. The money came from the NiYA–Cascador Founders Programme, run by the Federal Ministry of Youth Development through the Nigerian Youth Academy, known as NiYA, and Cascador, a platform that supports growth-stage founders. The funding was non-dilutive. That is a formal way of saying the founders gave up no shares in their companies. More than 19,000 people had applied. Seventeen were picked. Eight got ₦5 million each, and the other nine got ₦1 million each. The headlines called it free money. It is not. A grant is a contract with work attached. The cheque is the smallest part of the deal. This lesson prices the rest of it, so you can decide before you apply whether the money is worth the work.

01 — THE FREE-MONEY TRAP

“No shares” is not the same as “no cost”.

Here is how the trap works. A founder hears about a grant. The grant asks for no shares. The founder compares it with raising money from investors, who do take shares, and the grant looks free by comparison. That comparison is the mistake. Shares are only one kind of price. A grant charges in a different currency: your time, your calendar, and your freedom to change your mind. None of those show up in the headline. All of them show up in your week.

The trap has a second half. Grants feel safe because the money does not have to be paid back. That is true, and it matters. But “does not have to be paid back” is not the same as “comes with nothing attached”. The funder is not giving you money out of kindness. A government ministry, a development agency, a foundation — each one has a goal of its own, and your company is the way it reaches that goal. The money is payment for work that serves the funder's goal. The work is real. The question is never “is the money free?” The question is “is the work worth the money?”

Think of it like a customer who pays you in advance. The cash arrives first, which feels good. But the cash is not yours yet. It becomes yours when you deliver what you promised. A grant works the same way, except the customer is a funder, the promise is written in an agreement, and the delivery is measured in reports, attendance, and milestones. If you would not take a customer who pays ₦5 million and then demands six months of your time, do not take a grant that does the same thing.

✳
The mistake

Comparing a grant with equity and calling it free. Shares are one price. Hours, deadlines and lost freedom are the others — and a grant charges all three.

02 — WHAT THE FUNDER IS ACTUALLY BUYING

The funder is buying a result, not funding your company.

Every grant has a buyer behind it, and the buyer wants something specific. A youth ministry wants young people trained, businesses started, and numbers it can report. A development agency wants a solution deployed in real fields, with farmers using it. A foundation wants a problem measured and reduced. Your company is the tool that delivers that result. The money pays for the delivery.

This changes how you should read a grant announcement. The announcement tells you the size of the cheque. It rarely tells you the size of the job. The job is in the agreement — the document you sign after you win. The agreement says what you must deliver, when, and how the funder will check. It says what the money can and cannot be spent on. It says what happens if you miss a milestone. Most founders read the announcement carefully and skim the agreement. That is backwards. The announcement is advertising. The agreement is the deal.

There is a simple test for this. Before you apply, ask: if the funder's goal disappeared tomorrow, would I still do this work? If the answer is yes — the work is what your company needs anyway — the grant is paying for work you would do for free. That is a good grant. If the answer is no — the work only makes sense because the money is attached — the grant is hiring you to do someone else's job. That can still be worth it, if the pay is good. But you should know which one it is before you sign, not six months in.

One more thing the funder is buying: proof. Funders report to their own bosses — a minister, a board, a foreign government. Your reports, your photos, your attendance, your numbers become their evidence. That is why reporting is not a small extra. It is the product. A founder who treats reporting as paperwork will resent every hour of it. A founder who understands that reporting is the delivery will price it in from the start.

03 — THE FIVE HIDDEN COSTS

Five ways a grant charges you. None of them is shares.

First, the application. A serious grant application takes days, not hours. There is a form, a business plan, financial records, sometimes a video pitch. For the NiYA–Cascador programme, more than 19,000 people applied and 17 were picked. That is a selection rate below one in a thousand. The odds do not make applying wrong. They make the cost real: the hours you spend applying are hours you do not spend selling, building, or serving customers. And the odds mean you should expect to apply several times before one lands. Price the applications that fail, not just the one that wins.

Second, the reporting. After you win, you report. Progress reports, financial reports, receipts, photos, attendance records. Some funders want monthly reports. Some want quarterly reports plus a final report. Each report takes hours — collecting numbers, writing them up, getting them approved. The reporting is not optional. It is how the funder knows the money did what it was meant to do. A founder who wins ₦5 million and then spends two days a month reporting has given the funder roughly a quarter of a working week, every week, for as long as the grant runs.

Third, the restrictions. Grant money usually comes with rules about what it can buy. It may pay for equipment but not salaries. It may pay for a specific project but not for your rent. It may need the funder's approval before you spend above a certain amount. These rules are reasonable from the funder's side — the money is public or donor money, and it must be accounted for. But they mean the cash is not as flexible as your own revenue. A naira of grant money buys less freedom than a naira a customer paid you. If your urgent need is salaries and the grant only pays for equipment, the grant does not solve your problem, whatever its size.

Fourth, the calendar. Grants run on the funder's dates, not yours. A four-week programme means four weeks in the funder's rooms, on the funder's schedule. A twelve-week accelerator means twelve weeks of sessions, mentors, and a demo day. A six-month deployment means six months of fieldwork with check-ins along the way. Those dates are fixed. Your product launch, your biggest customer, your hiring plan — all of them wait. A founder with a small team cannot be in two places at once. The calendar is a cost, and it is the one founders forget most, because it does not look like money.

Fifth, the lost freedom. This is the quietest cost and the largest. Once you sign, your plan belongs partly to the funder. If the market changes and you need to pivot — to change what you sell, or who you sell it to — the agreement may not allow it, or may need the funder's permission. A founder who takes a grant to build one thing and then discovers customers want another thing faces a hard choice: keep building the funded thing, or lose the funding. Equity investors create the same problem, but founders expect it from investors. From a grant, it feels like a surprise. It should not be. The agreement says what you must deliver. If your company needs the freedom to change direction, a grant that locks your direction is expensive at any size.

✳
The five costs, in one line each

Application hours. Reporting hours. Spending restrictions. Fixed calendar dates. Lost freedom to change direction. Add them up before you apply.

04 — BUILD THE GRANT-COST SHEET

One page. Every cost in hours, then in naira.

The grant-cost sheet is a one-page table you fill in before you apply. It has five rows, one for each hidden cost, and three columns: what the work is, how many hours it will take, and who will do it. Then you turn the hours into naira. That last step is the one that makes the decision honest.

To turn hours into naira, you need the value of one hour of your time. There is no official number for this. Use a simple rule: what would you pay someone else to do an hour of your work, or what does an hour of your time earn when you spend it selling? Pick one number and use it for every grant you consider. The number does not need to be exact. It needs to be consistent, so you can compare one grant with another.

Here is a worked example. The numbers are illustrative — made up to show the method, not taken from any real grant. A founder values her hour at ₦10,000. She applies for a ₦5 million grant. The application takes 30 hours: ₦300,000 of her time. The programme takes four weeks, two days a week: 64 hours, or ₦640,000. Reporting takes one day a month for six months: 48 hours, or ₦480,000. Travel and waiting time add 20 hours: ₦200,000. The total is 162 hours, worth ₦1,620,000. The grant is ₦5 million, so the work costs about a third of the money in her own time — before any restrictions or lost freedom. That is a grant worth taking, if the work is useful anyway. But if the same grant took 400 hours, the work would cost ₦4 million of her time for ₦5 million of cash, and the restrictions would have to be very light for it to make sense.

Three rules make the sheet work.

Rule one: count the hours you will actually spend, not the hours you hope to spend. Applications always take longer than the form suggests. Reports always take longer than the template suggests. Add a third to your first estimate. If the grant still looks good after that, it is good.

Rule two: count everyone's hours, not just yours. If your co-founder spends a week on the application and your operations person spends a day a month on reports, those hours count too. A grant that costs your whole team is more expensive than a grant that costs only you.

Rule three: write down what the money cannot buy. Read the agreement — or, before you apply, the programme's published terms — and list the restrictions. If the money cannot pay for the thing you most need, write that in bold at the top of the sheet. A ₦5 million grant that cannot pay salaries does not solve a salary problem.

✳
The sheet, in short

Five rows: application, reporting, restrictions, calendar, lost freedom. Three columns: the work, the hours, who does it. Turn the hours into naira at one honest hourly rate. Compare the total with the cheque.

05 — THREE GRANTS, THREE DIFFERENT PRICES

The same word — “grant” — can mean three different deals.

First, NiYA–Cascador: cash, plus a programme. Eight founders received ₦5 million each, and the other nine in the cohort received ₦1 million each. The money is non-dilutive — no shares change hands. But the programme is a four-week commitment with business training, regulatory guidance, mentorship, and a pitch day in Abuja. The winners also receive a business management software system — an enterprise resource planning tool, which is software that organises a company's records and operations in one place — and alumni status with priority for future opportunities. That is a real package: cash, training, tools, and a relationship with a government programme. The price is four weeks of the founder's calendar, the pitch preparation, and whatever reporting the agreement requires. The public announcements do not publish the reporting terms, so a founder cannot price that part from the announcement alone — the agreement is where the number lives. For a founder whose business needs structure and training, the programme itself is worth something. For a founder who only needs cash, four weeks is a high price for it.

Second, NIMASA's Blue Economy Accelerator: no cash, but a pipeline. The Nigerian Maritime Administration and Safety Agency, known as NIMASA, opened applications in October 2026 for its Blue Economy Accelerator Programme. Applying was free. The programme runs a national hackathon — a short, intense event where teams build and present solutions — from 8 to 10 December 2026, then a twelve-week accelerator in the first quarter of 2027, and a demo day, where founders present to investors, in the second quarter of 2027. But this is an accelerator, not a cash grant. No fixed prize money has been announced. The value on offer is mentorship, venture development, industry partners, and investor access. The price is months of calendar: the hackathon, twelve weeks of programme, and the demo day, plus the application itself. A founder who reads “free accelerator” and hears “free money” has misread the deal. The money here is not cash. It is time, access, and credibility — valuable, but only if the founder's business is in the maritime or blue economy sector, which is the programme's focus, and only if the founder can afford the months.

Third, Farmerlifeline: cash tied to a job. Farmerlifeline Technologies, a Kenyan agritech company — agritech means technology for farming — won the third Innovate Africa Challenge, run by Smart Africa with the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, known as the FAO, and Norway's development agency, Norad. The prize is a $50,000 implementation grant: money to put the company's solar-powered cameras, which use artificial intelligence to spot crop pests and disease early, into real fields. The grant funds a six-month deployment. It is not general cash for the company. It is payment for six months of fieldwork, with the funders watching adoption rates, treatment outcomes, and operating costs. For Farmerlifeline, the work is the company's core work — deploying the product is what the company needs to do anyway. That is the best kind of grant: the funder is paying for the work you would do regardless. The price is still real — six months of the team's time, measured and reported — but the work and the company's plan are the same thing.

Notice the pattern. Three grants, three different deals. One pays cash and asks for a programme. One asks for months and pays in access, not cash. One pays cash for a specific six-month job. The word “grant” hides all of that. The cost sheet reveals it. Before you apply to any of them, the sheet would tell you: what you get, what you give, and whether the two match.

06 — WHAT COULD CHANGE THE ANSWER

Five limits on everything above.

First, the reporting terms are not public for every grant. The NiYA–Cascador announcements say the funding is non-dilutive and name the amounts. They do not publish the reporting schedule or the spending rules. Those live in the agreement each winner signs. This lesson cannot price what it cannot see. That is exactly why the sheet's third rule — read the agreement before you decide — matters. If a funder will not show you the agreement before you apply, treat that as information: the price is unknown, and unknown prices should make you careful.

Second, the NIMASA programme has no announced prize money. It is possible that funding opportunities appear later, through investors at the demo day or partners in the programme. None of that is promised. Judge the programme on what is announced — mentorship, venture development, and access — not on money that might appear.

Third, the Farmerlifeline figures come from reporting, not from the funder. The $50,000, the six-month deployment, and the 547 applications are reported by technology publications covering the challenge. Smart Africa, the organiser, has not published a detailed breakdown in the sources consulted. The shape of the deal is clear. The fine print is not.

Fourth, the hourly rate is yours to set, and it changes the answer. The worked example used ₦10,000 an hour because it is a round number, not because it is the right number for any real founder. A founder whose hour is worth ₦2,000 will find almost any grant cheap. A founder whose hour is worth ₦50,000 will find most grants expensive. The sheet does not decide for you. It shows you the trade in your own numbers.

Fifth, a grant can still be the right choice when the work matches your plan. Nothing in this lesson says grants are bad. A grant that pays for work you would do anyway, on a calendar you can afford, with reporting you can handle, is good money. The danger is not the grant. The danger is signing without pricing the work, and discovering the price six months later, when the calendar is already spent.

07 — THE NEXT STEP

Price it before you apply.

  • Take the next grant you are considering — or the one already sitting in your inbox — and fill in the grant-cost sheet before you start the application. Five rows: application, reporting, restrictions, calendar, lost freedom. Hours, then naira.
  • Ask the one question that matters most: would I do this work if there were no money attached? If the answer is yes, the grant is paying for your own plan. If the answer is no, compare the hours with the cheque honestly.
  • Read the agreement, or the published terms, before you apply — not after you win. If the reporting schedule or spending rules are not public, ask for them. A funder that will not show the terms is telling you the price is hidden.
  • Set your rule in advance: if the priced work costs more than half the cheque, do not apply. If the work matches your plan and costs less than a third, apply with confidence. Between the two, decide on the work itself — is it work your company needs?
  • This week, list every grant, prize, or programme you are chasing or considering. Price each one on the sheet. Drop any where the work costs more than the money. That list, priced, is your grant strategy.

One priced grant teaches the habit. The habit is what stops the next “free” cheque from costing you a quarter of your year.

THE TAKEAWAY

Free money is a contract. Price the work before you take the money.

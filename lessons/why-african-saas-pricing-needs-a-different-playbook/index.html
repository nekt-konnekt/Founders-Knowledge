A founder builds one SaaS product, opens sales in Lagos, Nairobi and Johannesburg, and publishes one dollar price converted into three currencies. The spreadsheet looks international. The offer may still be wrong in all three markets. The buyer, alternative, payment route, tax treatment, procurement process and cost of supporting the account can differ. African SaaS does not need one “Africa price”. It needs a repeatable way to decide what each buyer should pay in each market.

01 — THE ONE-CONTINENT MISTAKE

Country matters. But country is not the customer.

A country-level price list is better than pretending every market has the same currency and payment infrastructure. It is still too broad to be your whole pricing strategy.

Consider two businesses in the same city. One is a two-person consultancy that needs invoices and expense records. The other is a distributor with four branches, several stock controllers, approval workflows and hundreds of daily transactions. They may both be registered in Nigeria and both use accounting software. Their workload, buyer, urgency, implementation burden and ability to pay are not the same.

Now compare the distributor with a similar business in Kenya. The workflow may be close, but the local compliance requirement, payment method, currency exposure and vendor alternatives can differ. A country label tells you where to investigate; it does not tell you the final price.

✳
Use a country × buyer × workflow view

Start with the buyer and the job. Then localise the price, billing terms, payment route and support promise for the market in which that buyer operates.

Purchasing-power-parity figures and national income statistics can help describe broad economic conditions. They cannot tell you what a clinic owner will pay to reduce missed appointments, what a distributor will pay to reduce stock leakage, or whether a finance manager can approve a new software vendor this quarter. Use macro data as context, not as a pricing formula.

This article is the market-pricing layer of the existing Pricing Is a Product Decision and Choosing a Pricing Model lessons. It does not repeat the choice between subscriptions, freemium and usage-based billing. It asks how to set a defensible price for a defined buyer across different markets.

02 — WHAT PUBLIC PRICES SHOW

Local price lists are evidence of localisation—not proof of willingness to pay.

One useful way to study a market is to inspect what established software vendors actually publish. Zoho Books provides a concrete example. On 18 November 2026, its public Standard plan pages showed the following list prices:

Nigeria₦4,320₦3,600e-Invoicing
KenyaKSh 999KSh 849eTIMS-compliant invoicing and VAT3 report
South AfricaR99R82.50VAT returns and audit reports

The listed Standard plan includes three users in each market. Prices are shown per organisation per month and exclude local taxes. These are published list prices captured on 17 November 2026—not negotiated contract prices or a measure of actual willingness to pay.

The lesson is not that one country is “cheap” and another is “expensive”. Do not compare the nominal amounts without a dated exchange rate, tax treatment and a check that the feature scope is equivalent. The useful observation is that one vendor maintains country-specific currency prices and adapts parts of the product to local compliance workflows.

Payment collection is also market-specific. Paystack's transaction-fee page, updated 15 September 2026, lists Nigeria local transactions at 1.5% plus ₦100, capped at ₦2,000; Ghana at 1.95%; Kenya at 2.9% for cards or 1.5% for M-PESA; and South Africa at 2.9% plus R1 for cards or 2% for EFT, with the South African fees shown as VAT-exclusive. Those are one provider's published rates, not a market average or a universal processor cost. They demonstrate why your calculator must use the actual payment method and provider you will offer.

!
Do not reverse-engineer demand from a competitor's price

A public price tells you what a vendor is asking. It does not reveal conversion, discounting, churn, negotiated enterprise terms, collection failure or the customer's actual budget. Treat it as a reference point to investigate—not a number to copy.

03 — MAP THE BUYER

Find the person who feels the problem and the person who approves the spend.

Before calculating a price, write down five things about one narrow customer segment. “Small businesses in Ghana” is not a segment. “Independent pharmacies with two to five outlets that need stock visibility across branches” is a much better starting point.

The five-part buyer map

  1. Job: what recurring task or loss does the product improve? Be specific enough to observe it.
  2. Beneficiary: who gets the time, revenue, risk reduction or control? The daily user may not be the person who pays.
  3. Budget owner: who can approve the purchase, from which budget, and on what schedule?
  4. Current alternative: what does the buyer use today—paper, WhatsApp, a spreadsheet, an employee, an accountant, another SaaS product or doing nothing?
  5. Cost to serve: what onboarding, migration, training, integration, support and compliance work will this account require?

Interview about past behaviour, not hypothetical enthusiasm. Ask what the buyer paid for the current workaround, when they last approved a similar purchase, who signed off, what delayed it, and what happened when the current process failed. If they say the product is valuable, ask what they would stop paying for or which budget would fund it.

Record the answer by account, not just by individual. In B2B research, three employees from one company can explain three perspectives, but they are still one purchasing account. Do not turn a handful of conversations into a market-wide willingness-to-pay claim.

Finally, choose a billing unit that follows the value or workload. Charge per organisation when the main value is a shared business workflow; per location when branches create distinct operational work; per active user when each seat adds meaningful value or cost; and by usage only when the unit is predictable enough for a buyer to budget. A per-seat price that discourages the customer's staff from using the product can undermine the adoption you need.

04 — BUILD A PRICE CORRIDOR

Your price needs a floor and a ceiling.

Cost-plus pricing alone ignores customer value. Value-based pricing alone can ignore the cost of delivering the promise. A useful starting method is to calculate both and test the space between them.

The price corridor: define the buyer, calculate the cost floor, estimate the value ceiling, then test a real offer.

First, calculate the floor.

For one account and one billing period, include the costs that rise when you add or serve that account:

  • Hosting, storage, messaging, AI/API calls and other usage-linked vendor charges.
  • Payment processing, payout and collection costs for the method customers actually use.
  • Onboarding, migration and support time. Amortise one-off work over a realistic contract period instead of pretending it is free.
  • Partner commissions, refunds, failed collections and other variable delivery costs.
  • The local-currency impact of foreign-currency costs. Add a deliberate FX buffer to the exposed portion, not to every cost indiscriminately.

If your target contribution margin is measured against the list price, a simplified monthly price floor is:

Price floor = (risk-adjusted delivery cost + fixed payment fee) ÷ (1 − percentage payment fee − target contribution margin)

Enter the fee and margin as decimals in the formula—for example, 1.5% as 0.015 and 60% as 0.60. Use the actual fee schedule for the provider and payment method. If fees are capped, tiered or passed to the customer, calculate the real net receipt instead of forcing this simplified formula.

Then estimate the ceiling.

Estimate the economic value the buyer can actually realise in a month: labour time saved, avoidable loss reduced, revenue recovered, delays removed or a cost replaced. Remove double counting. Multiply the conservative value estimate by the share you can credibly capture:

Value ceiling = verified monthly customer value × defensible capture share

The capture share is a pricing hypothesis, not a universal percentage. Validate it through paid offers, renewals and the buyer's alternatives.

A worked example (all figures illustrative).

Imagine an inventory SaaS product sold to a four-branch Nigerian distributor. The team estimates monthly per-account delivery costs of ₦1,200 in local costs, ₦1,500 in FX-exposed costs, ₦1,000 in support and onboarding, and no additional variable cost in this simplified example. It adds a 10% buffer to the FX-exposed cost, giving risk-adjusted monthly cost of ₦3,850.

Assume one monthly Paystack local payment at the published Nigeria rate of 1.5% plus ₦100, and choose a 60% contribution-margin target for this example. The floor is:

(₦3,850 + ₦100) ÷ (1 − 0.015 − 0.60) = approximately ₦10,260 per month.

Now suppose customer interviews and operating records support a conservative monthly value estimate of ₦60,000. If the business believes it can capture 20% of that value, the provisional ceiling is ₦12,000. The first price corridor is therefore roughly ₦10,260–₦12,000. A test price of ₦11,000 leaves about ₦10,735 after the assumed payment fee and ₦6,885 after the illustrated delivery costs—about 62.6% contribution against the list price.

That is not a Nigerian SaaS benchmark. It is a transparent example of how to combine observed customer value with your own delivery economics. Replace every assumption with real account data before using the result.

Notice what happens if the team offers a 10% annual prepayment discount: ₦11,000 × 12 × 90% = ₦118,800 for the year. If the customer makes one annual Paystack local payment, the 1.5% fee plus ₦100 fixed fee leaves ₦116,918. Annual delivery cost is ₦3,850 × 12 = ₦46,200, so contribution is ₦70,718, or about 59.53% of the discounted gross annual price. That is just below the 60% target. The net monthly equivalent after the payment fee is about ₦9,743, but compare annual contribution margin with the discounted annual price—not that net monthly figure with the undiscounted monthly price floor. Annual cash upfront may still help working capital, but it does not make the discount free. Recalculate payment fees, annual delivery costs and service obligations before offering it.

If your floor is above the value ceiling, do not automatically cut the price. You may be serving the wrong segment, carrying too much manual support, packaging the product poorly, or promising an expensive level of service to a buyer who cannot fund it. Change the cost structure, scope, buyer or delivery model—or decide that the segment is not viable.

05 — PAYMENT AND PROCUREMENT

The price on the page is not the amount that reaches your bank.

Two customers can accept the same price and create different collection costs. One pays by card immediately. Another requests an invoice and bank transfer. A third needs a purchase order, vendor onboarding, a tax invoice and approval from a finance team. Those are different commercial journeys.

Before publishing a market price, decide:

  • Currency: what currency will the customer see, be charged in and receive an invoice in? Do not promise a local-currency charge if the payment route settles differently.
  • Collection: which local methods are available and what do they cost? Model card, transfer, direct debit or mobile-money fees separately where they differ.
  • Tax display: is the published amount tax-inclusive or tax-exclusive? Which entity invoices the buyer? Confirm local tax treatment with a qualified adviser.
  • Billing terms: does the segment buy monthly, annually, by invoice, by purchase order or after a pilot? A buyer's procurement process can be a larger obstacle than the nominal price.
  • Support promise: is onboarding self-serve, assisted or hands-on? Price the time and service level instead of hiding unlimited support inside the cheapest plan.

For a product team, keep one underlying product and plan definition, then maintain a versioned price book by market. A practical record contains product_plan_id, country, currency, billing interval, amount, tax display, allowed payment methods, effective date and review date. Do not create a different product SKU for every currency just to store a different amount. Preserve the agreed price for existing contracts and communicate changes before renewal.

Currency risk deserves its own operating discipline. If some delivery costs are in dollars and customers pay in local currency, set a review cadence and a trigger for revisiting the price. Do not reprice every customer every time an exchange-rate screen moves. The separate Price the FX Risk lesson covers the exposure map and practical hedges; this guide uses the resulting cost buffer in the price calculation.

06 — TEST THE PRICE

Ask for money, then watch what happens after payment.

A survey answer such as “I would pay ₦10,000” is weak evidence. A signed paid pilot, a completed payment, successful use and a renewal are progressively stronger signals. None alone proves a whole market, but each tells you more than a compliment.

  1. Hold the segment constant. Offer the same outcome, scope, onboarding and service promise to comparable buyers.
  2. Change one pricing variable. Test price, billing interval or packaging separately. If you change all three, you will not know what caused the result.
  3. Record the whole funnel. Track qualified offers, proposals, approvals, payments, activation, support hours, refunds, renewals and reasons for rejection.
  4. Separate price objections from other friction. “Too expensive” can mean the value is unclear, the wrong person saw the offer, the budget is unavailable this month, or the procurement process is unfinished.
  5. Review by account cohort and market. A small pilot is useful for learning, not for claiming a representative market conversion rate.

Do not quietly give every buyer a different discount and then report the published list price as realised revenue. Record list price, discount, reason, approver, net collected amount and renewal terms. The price customers actually pay is the price your economics must survive.

07 — A 30-DAY PRICING EXERCISE

Build a price you can defend in four weeks.

WEEK 1Choose one buyer

Pick one country, one buyer segment and one recurring job. Map the user, beneficiary, budget owner, current alternative and procurement route.

WEEK 2Calculate the corridor

Use actual vendor bills, support time, payment fees and a measured customer-value estimate. Record what is still unknown.

WEEK 3Make paid offers

Present one clear scope and price to qualified buyers. Ask for a paid pilot or a real purchase commitment with honest delivery and refund terms.

WEEK 4Review the economics

Compare paid conversion, net collection, activation, support burden, refunds and renewal intent. Decide whether to keep the price, change the package or change the segment.

Repeat the exercise for the next country only when you have a credible buyer route and enough evidence to justify the work. Expanding a price book across ten countries before you understand the first buyer is not localisation. It is multiplying assumptions.

08 — THE DECISION CHECKLIST

Before you publish a market price

  • Can we name the buyer segment more precisely than “SMEs” or “Africa”?
  • Do we know who uses, benefits, approves and pays?
  • Have we documented the customer's current alternative and its real cost?
  • Does the price cover delivery, support, collection and FX exposure?
  • Is the customer's measurable value comfortably above the price?
  • Can the customer pay, receive an invoice and complete procurement in the way they already work?
  • Have we tested a real paid offer and recorded the net amount collected?
  • Do we know when the price book will be reviewed and how existing customers will be treated?
✳
The takeaway

Do not export one price across a continent and call it scale. Build one product, understand each buyer, calculate each market's cost to serve, and test a local price that the customer can justify and your business can sustain.

THE TAKEAWAY

African SaaS pricing is not a discount formula. It is a country-by-buyer decision backed by value, cost, collection and renewal evidence.

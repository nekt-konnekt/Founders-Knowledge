Free access is a strategy, not a universal prerequisite for validation.

“

Many founders assume users must try a product, experience its value, and only then be asked to pay. Sometimes that is the right path. But it is not a rule. Pricing can be part of validating a product from the beginning—especially when a specific customer already understands the problem and the cost of leaving it unsolved.

01 — QUESTION THE DEFAULT

Does a user need to experience the product before paying?

The familiar approach is simple: let people use the product for free, help them experience the value, and convert them into paying customers later. It assumes that product experience is the main barrier to purchase.

But customers do not always need hands-on access to understand a product's potential. They may recognize the value from a credible demonstration, a clear explanation of the outcome, a successful pilot, or the cost of their current workaround.

AValue discovered through use

A habit tracker or creative tool may need time for users to discover whether it fits their routines. A free tier or time-limited trial can reduce adoption uncertainty.

BValue understood before use

A specialized tool that saves a professional hours of repetitive work may be worth paying for after a credible demonstration, without requiring a lengthy free experience.

Some products sit between these cases. Buyers may understand the problem but still need proof of reliability, privacy, or performance before committing. The task is to identify the uncertainty blocking the purchase—not automatically remove the price.

✳
Free access is one possible answer

It can reduce uncertainty, encourage discovery, or help a product spread. It is not a universal requirement for validating value.

02 — KNOW WHAT YOU ARE TESTING

What does charging from launch day validate?

Charging early can reveal whether the right customers will exchange money for the outcome you promise. But payment is one signal in a wider validation process; it does not prove every part of the product is right.

  • Problem validation: does the problem matter enough for someone to seek a solution?
  • Value validation: does the proposed solution improve on current alternatives, including doing nothing?
  • Willingness to pay: will the intended customer pay this price for the expected outcome?
  • Product validation: can the product reliably deliver that outcome and earn continued use or payment?

A purchase provides stronger evidence of willingness to pay than a compliment, survey answer, or free sign-up. Repeat purchases, retention, and successful outcomes provide stronger evidence that value lasts.

And a failed sale is not a verdict on the product. Price, positioning, trust, timing, customer segment, purchasing authority, and reach can all affect the result. Treat the outcome as evidence to investigate, not an instant conclusion.

!
A payment validates a purchasing decision

Repeated payment and successful outcomes provide stronger evidence of lasting value. Pricing cannot replace product quality or customer learning.

A niche product can be commercially meaningful with a relatively small audience if it solves an important problem for customers willing and able to pay. The question is not only how many people will use it, but how many of the right people will pay enough to sustain its delivery.

03 — PRODUCT DESIGN

How pricing changes the product itself

Pricing is not a number added after development. It influences who the product serves, what it includes, the standard customers expect, and whether the business can sustain the promise.

  • Who it serves: a free product can attract curious users who may never pay. A price can filter for committed buyers, but it can also exclude people who genuinely need the solution.
  • What gets built: pricing forces decisions about the core promise, premium capabilities, usage limits, and what should remain accessible to everyone.
  • What customers expect: paying customers may expect reliable service, support, privacy, security, and clear accountability.
  • What the business can sustain: hosting, AI processing, storage, payment fees, onboarding, and support all affect the economics of serving each customer.

A low price that attracts many customers can still damage the business if every additional user costs more to serve than the revenue they bring. That is not merely a financial issue; it may indicate that the product's delivery model and pricing do not fit.

The price must make sense for the customer, the product experience, and the business model simultaneously.

04 — TEST EARLY, LEARN HONESTLY

How can you test willingness to pay?

A founder can test commercial demand before every feature is complete, provided the offer is honest about what exists, what is promised, and when delivery will happen.

01Paid launch or pilot

Sell the initial product or a limited engagement to a small group and observe whether customers pay, use it, and achieve the intended outcome.

02Credible prototype or demo

Explain the offer and ask for a real commitment. Make clear when the product is not yet available and handle pre-orders and refunds responsibly.

03Trial followed by payment

Use a trial when actual use is necessary to resolve the customer's uncertainty. Measure whether people reach the key outcome before the trial ends.

04Transparent price experiments

Compare offers with relevant customer groups. Change one important variable at a time and avoid misleading buyers with hidden or arbitrary terms.

Freemium can also be right when a free tier drives discovery, collaboration, or future upgrades. The point is not to avoid free access; it is to choose it deliberately.

05 — TRY THIS

Before deciding to launch free

Write short answers to these questions before choosing a pricing approach:

  • Who has the problem most urgently?
  • What does the problem currently cost them in money, time, risk, or missed opportunity?
  • What proof do they need before paying?
  • Can a demonstration or pilot provide that proof, or must they use the product themselves?
  • What must the product deliver—and what will it cost us—to justify the price?

Reality check: early payment is useful evidence, not a shortcut to certainty. A small number of purchases cannot establish the size of a market, and a failed sale does not tell you which assumption was wrong without further investigation.

The takeaway

Pricing is part of product design and a way to test commercial demand—not a monetization decision you must postpone until after launch.

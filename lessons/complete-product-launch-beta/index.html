Do not confuse finishing the build with proving the product can deliver value.

“

Founders can spend months building a product without knowing whether they are building something people need. The goal is not to ship something perfect. It is to deliver the promised outcome responsibly, then learn what the market and real usage reveal.

01 — DEFINE COMPLETE

A complete product delivers its core promise.

A complete product is not one with every feature the founder imagined. It is one that can reliably deliver the specific outcome it promises to its intended user.

Imagine an invoice tool. A freelancer can enter client details, calculate the total correctly, create an invoice, and download or share it. AI suggestions, multiple themes, team collaboration, and advanced analytics might improve the product, but they are not necessarily needed to deliver its first useful outcome.

✳
Small can still be complete

A product can have a narrow scope and fulfil that scope well. A feature-rich product can still be incomplete if its main workflow is unreliable or does not solve the user's problem.

Five dimensions to review

  • Functional: can users complete the main task?
  • Reliable: does the workflow work consistently enough for its intended use?
  • Usable: can intended users understand what to do?
  • Trustworthy: are privacy, security, data integrity, and important failure cases handled?
  • Valuable: does the product solve a problem that matters to the intended user?

Technical completeness and commercial validation are different. You can finish building a product before you know whether customers want it, will return to it, or will pay for it.

02 — KNOW THE STAGES

Prototype, MVP, beta, and launch are not synonyms.

01Prototype

Test whether an idea or workflow could work. It may be a mock-up or a limited technical experiment.

02Minimum viable product

Deliver the smallest useful version that lets real users experience the core benefit and helps test key assumptions.

03Beta

Let selected external users test the product in real conditions. Observe usability, reliability, and whether the product creates value.

04Public launch

Open access more broadly when the core promise works, important risks are addressed, and support and measurement are ready.

These are useful distinctions, not mandatory gates. A public launch can still be an experiment, and potential users can test a prototype before an MVP exists. Choose the sequence that resolves your biggest uncertainties responsibly.

03 — USE BETA ON PURPOSE

Why do founders skip beta?

Some want immediate traction. Some fear exposing unfinished work. Others confuse adding features with progress, trust only internal testing, or see recruiting testers as extra overhead. Skipping beta is not always wrong: a low-risk product that is easy to update may be suitable for a controlled public release.

  • Perfectionism: the definition of “ready” keeps moving.
  • Fear of feedback: public release feels like a verdict instead of a learning step.
  • Builder blind spots: developers know the intended workflow; new users do not.
  • No learning goal: the founder has not decided what beta should answer.
  • Time constraints: a separate testing phase can be costly when the team is small.

A beta without a purpose becomes a waiting room. A beta with clear questions becomes an experiment. Decide in advance what you need to learn and what evidence would make you fix, launch, or rethink the product.

How many testers?

There is no magic number. As a practical starting point, five to ten representative users can help expose major usability problems; a larger group can reveal more varied workflows. These are working ranges, not scientific thresholds. Recruit people who resemble the intended customers, not only friends who want to encourage you.

04 — MEASURE BEHAVIOUR

Do not ask only whether people like it.

People can praise a product without using it. Measure actions related to the value you promise, and choose a cadence that fits how often the problem occurs.

  • Activation: can new users reach the core benefit?
  • Retention: do users return when the problem arises again?
  • Reliability: do essential workflows succeed without serious failures?
  • Friction: where do people hesitate, abandon tasks, or ask for help?
  • Willingness to pay: will the intended buyer commit to a purchase or paid pilot?

A product used daily needs different retention measures from one used quarterly. Five carefully observed users can reveal a serious usability issue, while fifty sign-ups may reveal little about willingness to pay if no one is asked to commit.

05 — DIAGNOSE BEFORE YOU REBUILD

If nobody comes, investigate the bottleneck.

NO VISITORS

Potential issue: distribution.

Are the right people aware of the product? Does your channel reach them?

VISITS, BUT NO SIGN-UPS

Potential issue: relevance or trust.

Do visitors understand the value, and does the offer match the reason they arrived?

SIGN-UPS, BUT NO MEANINGFUL USE

Potential issue: onboarding or core value.

Can users reach the promised benefit without help?

FIRST USE, THEN SILENCE

Potential issue: recurring value or usage frequency.

Does the product solve a recurring problem, or did it fail to deliver a useful result?

REPEAT USE, BUT NO REVENUE

Potential issue: buyer, pricing, or monetization.

Who benefits, who pays, and is the value worth the price?

These are diagnostic starting points, not certain explanations. A few observations cannot prove product-market fit. The lesson is to avoid treating every market problem as a product-development problem.

06 — READINESS CHECKLIST

Before you open the doors wider.

  • The intended user and core problem are clearly defined.
  • A user can complete the main workflow from beginning to end.
  • Critical defects, data-loss risks, and serious security issues are addressed.
  • Representative users can understand the essential workflow.
  • You can observe meaningful usage and identify failures.
  • You have a practical way to reach the first relevant users.
  • You can collect feedback and act on what you learn.
  • You have defined the launch objective and the next decision it will inform.

Not every optional feature must be complete. But the acceptable risk depends on the product. A minor visual issue in a personal reading app is different from data loss, incorrect payments, or a failure that could cause serious harm.

!
Do not lower the bar for safety

Early launch is not an excuse for careless engineering. Resolve serious risks before exposing users to them, especially when sensitive data, money, or health is involved.

YOUR NEXT STEP

Build enough to learn. Then make the next decision.

Write down the core promise, the biggest unresolved risk, the smallest group of relevant users who can help you test it, and the evidence that would make you launch, fix, or rethink.

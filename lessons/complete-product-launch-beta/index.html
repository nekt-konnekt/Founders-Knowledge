Before launch, founders face a different set of questions: What makes a product complete? When is it ready? Must it go through beta? These are not just questions about code. They are decisions about value, risk, and what you still need to learn before putting a product in front of more people.

01 — DEFINE COMPLETE

What is a complete product?

A complete product is not necessarily a product with every feature you imagined. It is one that can reliably deliver the specific outcome it promises to its intended user.

Imagine a product that promises to help freelancers generate professional invoices.

✓Complete enough to launch

A freelancer can create an invoice, enter the client's details, calculate the total correctly, download or share it, and return to use the product again.

+Not required for the first launch

AI-generated invoices, team collaboration, advanced analytics, ten themes, accounting integrations, and a mobile app.

The first group delivers the promised outcome. The second group may improve the product, but it is not necessarily required to deliver that outcome.

✳
Small can still be complete

A product can be small and complete. It can also be enormous and still fail to deliver its central promise.

Five dimensions of product completeness

  • Functional: can users complete the main task?
  • Reliable: does it work consistently enough for its intended use?
  • Usable: can the intended users understand how to use it?
  • Trustworthy: are privacy, security, data integrity, and important failure cases handled appropriately?
  • Valuable: does it solve a real problem well enough that someone would want to use it?

Notice the last dimension. A product can pass every technical test and still fail as a business.

One more distinction: a product can be technically complete without being commercially validated. You can finish building something before discovering whether the market wants it.

02 — WHEN TO LAUNCH

When is the perfect time to launch?

The honest answer is that there is no universally perfect launch date. There is, however, a point at which waiting to build more becomes less useful than putting the product in front of real users.

01Build — too early for broad release

The core workflow is broken, essential functionality is missing, or users cannot safely complete the promised task.

02Beta — ready for controlled learning

The main workflow works, but you still need to test usability, reliability, demand, or assumptions with a limited group of users.

03Launch — ready for wider access

The product delivers its core promise, known risks are acceptable, support is manageable, and you can observe how people use it.

You do not need to wait until every possible feature exists. You do need to distinguish between an unfinished enhancement and a failure that prevents the product from doing its job.

For example, a note-taking app missing collaborative editing might be launchable. A note-taking app that regularly deletes users' notes is not ready for a broad release.

!
Ask the responsible question

The launch question is not, “Have I finished building?” It is, “Can I responsibly put this product in front of the people it is intended to serve?”

03 — USE BETA ON PURPOSE

Why do founders skip the beta stage?

There is no single reason, and not every founder who skips beta is making a mistake. But several patterns explain why it happens.

  • They confuse building with progress. Adding another feature feels productive. Finding out that customers do not want the product feels uncomfortable. Building can become a way to postpone market feedback.
  • Perfectionism. The founder wants the branding, dashboard, onboarding, animations, and every workflow to feel finished before anybody sees it. The definition of “ready” keeps changing.
  • Fear of public failure. A small beta makes it easier to admit that something needs fixing. A public launch can feel like a verdict on the founder's ability, even though it is only one step in the learning process.
  • They believe internal testing is enough. Developers know how the product is supposed to work. New users do not. Real users bring different devices, expectations, habits, and mistakes that internal testing may not reveal.
  • They want immediate traction. Some founders expect a launch to generate users, revenue, publicity, and validation simultaneously. They may prefer a public release because it creates a visible milestone.
  • A beta seems like extra work. Recruiting testers, collecting feedback, fixing issues, and managing another release can be demanding, especially for a solo founder.

If the product is low-risk and easy to update, a controlled public launch may be more efficient. The deeper issue is not skipping a formal beta; it is skipping learning. Some founders have not defined what they need to learn before scaling.

✳
A beta without a purpose becomes a waiting room.

A beta with clear questions becomes an experiment. Decide what you need to learn and what evidence would make you fix, launch, or rethink the product.

04 — KNOW THE STAGES

What is the difference between a prototype, MVP, beta, and launch?

These terms are often mixed together, creating unnecessary confusion.

01Prototype

Question: Can this idea work?
A rough model, mock-up, or technical experiment. It helps test an idea before committing to a full product.

02MVP

Question: Can we deliver the core value?
The minimum version that lets real users experience the essential benefit and helps the founder test important assumptions.

03Beta

Question: Does it work for real people in real conditions?
A testing phase with selected external users. It reveals usability issues, unexpected behaviour, reliability problems, and whether users actually get value.

04Public launch

Question: Can we open it to a wider market?
Broader availability, supported by appropriate reliability, customer support, onboarding, and distribution.

These are not four mandatory, strictly sequential steps. An MVP can enter beta; a public launch can still be an experiment; and a prototype can be tested with potential customers before an MVP exists. The right sequence depends on the risks you need to resolve.

05 — CHOOSE TESTERS

How many people should participate in a beta?

There is no magic number. The number should reflect what you are trying to learn, how diverse your users are, and how much support you can provide.

Private, hands-on beta — 5–10 users
Illustrative starting point for observing workflows and discovering major confusion.

Expanded beta — 10–50 users
Illustrative starting point for testing more use cases and gathering broader behavioural evidence.

Open beta — anyone who wants to join
Useful for learning from wider usage and real-world variation.

These are practical starting ranges, not scientific thresholds or universal standards.

Five people can expose a serious usability problem. Fifty people can still tell you very little about willingness to pay if you never ask for payment or observe purchasing behaviour.

Choose participants who represent your intended customers—not simply friends who want to support you.

06 — MEASURE BEHAVIOUR

What should founders measure during beta?

Do not ask only, “Do you like the product?” People can praise your product without using it. Instead, measure the behaviours that reveal whether it is working.

  • Activation: can a new user reach the product's core benefit without excessive assistance?
  • Retention: does the user return when the problem arises again? Measure this at a cadence that fits the product.
  • Reliability: do essential workflows succeed? Are there crashes, lost data, failed transactions, or other serious problems?
  • User friction: where do users hesitate, misunderstand the interface, abandon tasks, or ask for help?
  • Willingness to pay: will the intended customer pay, commit to a paid pilot, or take another meaningful step toward purchasing?

Your measurements should follow the product's promise. A product used daily requires different retention measures from one used for an annual task.

For an early-stage product, a small number of carefully observed users can be more informative than a large number of anonymous sign-ups.

PART 1 TAKEAWAY

Build enough to test the promise.

A product does not need every feature to be complete enough to test. It needs to deliver its central outcome, work reliably enough for the context, and let you learn from the people it is meant to serve.

Use the readiness checklist in Part 2 to decide what to do when you launch, how to interpret what happens, and when to improve, reposition, or change direction.

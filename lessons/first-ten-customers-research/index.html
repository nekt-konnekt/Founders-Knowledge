Your First Ten Customers Are a Research Project, Not a Sales Target

Your first ten customers are worth more as evidence than as income. Treat them as a research project and they will tell you what to build next.

Ten customers will pay you a little. They will teach you a lot — if you ask before you sell.

“

You have ten customers. Your product works, people pay for it, and the number on your dashboard says ten. The natural next move feels obvious: get to a hundred. More adverts, more outreach, more sales calls. But there is a cheaper move first, and most founders skip it. Ask those ten people what they actually do with your product, what nearly stopped them from buying it, and what they would miss if it disappeared tomorrow. Their answers are worth more than the money they pay you. Ten customers will pay you a little. They will teach you a lot. This lesson shows you how to collect that teaching before you spend money chasing the next ninety.

01 — THE SALES-TARGET TRAP

Ten customers is a milestone. It is also a test you have not taken yet.

Here is how the trap works. A founder gets ten people to pay. Ten feels like proof. Proof that the idea works, proof that the market is real, proof that it is time to grow. So the founder turns the ten into a target: ten becomes a hundred, a hundred becomes a thousand. All the energy goes into reaching more people with the same product.

The mistake is in what ten proves. Ten paying customers prove that ten people found the product useful enough to pay for it once. That is real, and it matters. But it does not prove you know why they paid. It does not prove you know which part of the product they value. It does not prove the next ninety people will want the same thing, or pay for the same reason. Ten customers can all be buying for different reasons. If you scale before you know the reason, you scale a guess.

Think of it like a cook who sells ten plates of food on the first day. The plates sold out, so the cook buys ten times more ingredients for the second day. But the cook never asked which dish people liked, or whether they came back because of the food, the price, or the location. On the second day, the wrong dish sells out and the right one goes cold. The sales number was real. The understanding behind it was missing. Early customers work the same way. The number tells you something happened. It does not tell you what happened.

There is a second half to the trap. Money from ten customers is small money. If each customer pays ₦10,000 a month, ten customers pay ₦100,000 a month. That is real income, but it will not fund a team, and it will not carry a company. The learning from those same ten customers, on the other hand, can change what you build, who you sell to, and what you charge. One good interview can save you months of building the wrong feature. The cash from ten customers is a small reward. The knowledge is the real payment. Founders who treat ten as a sales target collect the small reward and throw the big one away.

✳
The mistake

Counting ten customers as proof the product is right, then spending money to reach more people with a product you do not yet understand. Ten proves ten people paid. It does not prove why.

02 — WHAT TEN CUSTOMERS CAN TEACH YOU

Ten people cannot give you statistics. They can give you patterns.

Ten is a small number, and small numbers cannot prove much. If three of your ten customers like a feature, you cannot say that thirty percent of the market likes it. The sample is too small for that kind of claim, and anyone who quotes a percentage from ten people is dressing a guess up as a fact. But ten people can do something else, and it is more useful at your stage: they can show you a pattern.

A pattern is the same thing said three or more times by different people, in their own words. If one customer says your sign-up is confusing, that is one person's opinion. If four customers say it, in different words, you have found something real. The pattern is the signal. The count is not a statistic. It is a reason to act.

There are three things ten customers can teach you, and each one changes a decision.

First, which problem is real. You built the product for a problem you imagined. Your customers live with the real version of that problem every day. They will tell you which part of it hurts most, and which part you misunderstood. The problem you thought you were solving is often the second or third problem on their list. The first one is the one they would pay more to solve. You cannot learn that from a sales number. You can only learn it by asking.

Second, what the product gets wrong. Customers find the rough edges you cannot see. The step they skip. The button they never press. The feature they use in a way you never intended. One customer using your product the wrong way is a curiosity. Four customers using it the same wrong way is a design lesson: the product should probably work the way they are already using it. Your first ten are the cheapest testers you will ever have, because they are already paying you.

Third, the words they use. This is the one founders undervalue most. The exact words a customer uses to describe their problem are the words your next hundred customers will recognise. If your website says “streamline your workflow” and your customers say “I was tired of chasing people on WhatsApp for payment”, your website is speaking a different language from your buyers. The words in your interviews are your future sales copy. Write them down exactly as spoken. Do not tidy them up.

Notice what all three have in common. None of them needs a hundred customers. All of them need a conversation. The research project is not a survey sent to a thousand strangers. It is ten honest conversations with people who already paid.

03 — THE THREE QUESTIONS

Three questions. Asked the same way, to every customer.

You do not need a long questionnaire. A long questionnaire gets short answers. Three questions, asked in a real conversation, will give you more than thirty questions on a form. Ask them the same way to every customer, so the answers can be compared. Write the answers down in the customer's own words, not in your summary.

Question one: “What were you doing before you found us?” This question finds the real alternative. Customers rarely switch from nothing. They switch from a spreadsheet, a notebook, a WhatsApp group, a person they pay, or a rival product. That is what you are competing with, not the rival you imagine. If five customers say they were doing the job on WhatsApp, your product's real job is to be better than WhatsApp, not better than the big company in your market. The answer also tells you how painful the old way was. If the old way was free and easy, your product must be clearly better, not slightly better.

Question two: “What almost stopped you from buying?” This question finds the friction — the thing that nearly cost you the sale. It might be the price. It might be a step in the sign-up. It might be that the customer did not understand what the product did until someone explained it. It might be trust: the customer was not sure a small new company would still be there next month. Whatever it is, it is the same thing that is stopping the people who did not buy. Your ten customers got past it. The next ninety might not. Fix the thing that almost stopped them, and you fix it for everyone who comes next.

Question three: “What would you miss most if this disappeared tomorrow?” This question finds the one thing that matters. Customers will name one feature, one result, or one feeling. It is rarely the feature you are proudest of. It is usually the small, boring thing that saves them time every day. That answer is your product's core — the part you must never break, never hide behind a paywall, and never remove in a redesign. If three customers name the same thing, that thing is your product. Everything else is decoration.

Three rules make the questions work.

Rule one: ask about what they did, not what they think. “Would you pay more for this?” gets a polite yes from almost everyone, and it means nothing. “What did you do last time this problem came up?” gets a true story. Behaviour is evidence. Opinion is decoration. Keep asking for the last time, the last payment, the last complaint — the specific moment, not the general feeling.

Rule two: do not pitch during the interview. The moment you explain your product, defend a price, or describe a coming feature, the customer stops telling you the truth and starts being polite. Your job in the interview is to listen and write. If the customer asks about a feature, say you will come back to it after the questions. The interview is research, not a sales call. Mixing the two ruins both.

Rule three: write down their exact words. “The customer likes the dashboard” is your summary, and it is useless. “I check the dashboard every morning before I open my shop” is evidence. It tells you when, how often, and why. Summaries lose the detail that makes the answer useful. Exact words keep it.

✳
The three questions, in one line each

What were you doing before us? What almost stopped you from buying? What would you miss if we disappeared tomorrow? Ask about what they did, not what they think. Listen. Write their exact words.

04 — BUILD THE TEN-CUSTOMER LOG

One page. Ten rows. Patterns you can count.

The log is a simple table. Ten rows, one for each customer. Four columns: the customer's name, what they did before you, what almost stopped them, and what they would miss. That is the whole tool. Fill it in after each interview, in the customer's own words, while the conversation is fresh. A log filled from memory a week later is a log full of your own opinions.

Once you have five or six rows, read down each column and count. Count the same answer, said in different words. “I was using a notebook”, “I wrote it in a book”, and “I kept it on paper” are the same answer. “The price scared me”, “I thought it would cost more”, and “I waited until month-end” are all about price, but they are three different problems: the price itself, the fear of hidden costs, and the timing of the payment. Count carefully. The grouping is the thinking.

The log gives you a decision rule. If three or more customers say the same thing, act on it. Three out of ten is not a statistic. It is a pattern strong enough to change a plan. If three customers nearly did not buy because the sign-up asked for too much information, shorten the sign-up. If three customers say they would miss the same small feature, protect that feature and put it at the front of your sales message. If three customers came from the same old method — the same spreadsheet, the same WhatsApp group — that method is your real competition, and your product should be built to beat it, not to beat the rival you were watching.

The log also tells you when to stop listening and start building. Ten interviews are enough. Twenty interviews will give you more of the same patterns, not new ones. Founders who keep interviewing instead of acting are hiding from the decision. The log is a research project, not a research career. Ten rows, patterns counted, one change made. Then do the next ten.

One more use for the log: it settles arguments inside the team. When a co-founder wants to build a new feature and you want to fix the sign-up, the log decides. Whichever one three or more customers named wins. The log does not make the decision for you. It makes the decision honest.

05 — TWO WORKED EXAMPLES

What the log changes: two small stories.

Both examples below are illustrative — made-up stories built to show the method working, not reports of real companies. The numbers are round and simple for the same reason.

First, the invoicing tool that learned its real job. A founder in Lagos builds a simple invoicing tool for small shops. Ten shop owners pay ₦5,000 a month. The founder assumes the product's value is the invoice itself — a clean, professional bill. She interviews the ten. Six of them were writing invoices in notebooks before. Four of them nearly did not buy because they thought the tool would need a computer, and they only have a phone. And when she asks what they would miss, seven of them say the same thing: the reminder. The tool sends a polite message to customers who have not paid, and that message collects money the shop owners used to chase by hand. The invoice was never the product. The reminder was. She changes the website to lead with the reminder, makes the phone sign-up the first thing a visitor sees, and stops building the fancy invoice templates she had planned. The log did not give her more customers. It told her which customers she already had, and what they were actually buying.

Second, the delivery app that learned to wait. A founder builds a food delivery app for one neighbourhood. Ten restaurants sign up. He assumes the problem is speed — faster delivery wins. He interviews the ten. Eight of them were taking orders on WhatsApp before. Five of them nearly did not sign up because they did not trust a new app to handle their regular customers. And when he asks what they would miss, six of them name the same thing: the order list. The app keeps a clean list of every order, so nothing gets lost in a chat. Speed never comes up. Not once. He stops spending money on faster riders and spends it on the order list instead: a printed copy for the kitchen, a clear record for the owner, and a message that tells the restaurant the order is confirmed. The restaurants stay. The log told him his product was a record-keeping tool that happened to deliver food, not a delivery company that happened to keep records. That is a different business, with different spending.

Notice the pattern in both stories. In each one, the founder's assumption was reasonable, and wrong in the detail that mattered. The log did not need a hundred customers to correct it. It needed ten honest conversations and a table.

06 — WHAT THE LOG CANNOT TELL YOU

Five limits on everything above.

First, ten customers are not the market. Your first ten are the easiest people to reach: friends of friends, people from your network, early believers. They are kinder than the market, and more forgiving. The patterns in the log are real, but they describe your first ten, not the next thousand. Treat the log as the best evidence you have, not as the whole truth.

Second, paying customers and free users are different people. A person who pays ₦5,000 a month has a different relationship with your product from a person who uses it free. Free users will praise a feature they would never pay for. If your ten are a mix of paying and free, keep their rows separate in the log. The paying customers' answers count more for pricing and for what to protect. The free users' answers count more for what stops people from paying at all.

Third, early customers flatter you. People who know you, or like you, will soften bad news. “It is fine” often means “I did not want to say”. The three questions help, because they ask about behaviour — what the customer did — rather than asking for a judgement. But if all ten interviews are with people who know you personally, find three strangers among your next ten, and ask them the same questions. Strangers are less polite, which is what you need.

Fourth, the log cannot tell you what to charge. Customers will tell you what they value and what nearly stopped them. They will not tell you the right price. Asking “would you pay more?” gets a polite answer, not a true one. Pricing is a separate decision, with its own method. The log can tell you that price nearly stopped five people — which means the price, or the way you explain it, needs work. It cannot tell you the number.

Fifth, the funding climate does not change the method, but it raises the stakes. African startups raised $3.02 billion in the first ten months of 2026, up 14.2% on the same period in 2025, with October alone bringing $540 million across 73 disclosed deals — but the money is concentrating in fewer, bigger cheques, and early-stage founders feel that first. New early-stage funds are still opening — First Circle Capital closed a $26 million fund in November 2026 to back pre-seed and seed fintechs — yet the bar for a first cheque keeps rising. A founder who can show what ten real customers taught them, in their own words, walks into that conversation with something better than a pitch: evidence. The log is not a substitute for revenue. It is the cheapest proof you can carry that you understand the people you serve.

07 — THE NEXT STEP

Interview two this week.

  • Pick two of your customers — the ones who use the product most, or the ones you understand least — and ask for twenty minutes each. Say it is research, not a sales call. Most paying customers will say yes.
  • Ask the three questions, in order: what were you doing before us? What almost stopped you from buying? What would you miss if we disappeared tomorrow? Write down their exact words.
  • Start the log today, even with two rows. Two rows will not show a pattern yet, but they will show you whether your questions work — and they will make the next eight interviews easier.
  • When you reach ten rows, count the patterns. If three or more customers say the same thing, act on it before you spend money on growth. One change from the log beats ten new adverts for a product you do not yet understand.
  • Set your rule in advance: no new feature, no new market, no new sales push until the log has ten rows and one clear pattern. The research comes first. The sales target comes second.

Ten interviews will not make your company. They will make your next decision honest. That is what the first ten customers are for.

THE TAKEAWAY

Ten customers will pay you a little. They will teach you a lot — if you ask before you sell.

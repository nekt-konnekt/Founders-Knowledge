01 — CHECKOUT IS NOT THE FINISH LINE

A customer can pay successfully and still have a bad experience.

Imagine a customer in Lagos pays ₦25,000 for a service. Their bank sends a debit alert. Your payment provider reports a successful transaction. Your order table still says awaiting payment. Finance cannot find the money in the bank yet. The customer asks support: “Have you received my payment?”

There are several different questions hiding inside that one message:

01Did the customer pay?

Was the transaction verified with the provider for the expected reference, amount and currency?

02Did the business fulfil?

Was the product, service, booking, credit or access actually delivered?

03Did the business receive cash?

Was the transaction included in a settlement and credited to the right destination?

A fourth question appears when something goes wrong: what does the business still owe the customer? That may be a refund, a corrected order, evidence for a dispute, or a clear explanation of an unresolved payment.

The mistake is to represent all of this with one field called paid. That field cannot tell support whether the customer was debited, finance whether the settlement arrived, or engineering what should happen next.

!
Do not make the customer become your reconciliation system.

“Send us a screenshot” can help support investigate, but it is not a substitute for checking the provider, matching the order and keeping an exception queue your team owns.

02 — KEEP FOUR RECORDS SEPARATE

One purchase creates more than one business record.

Start with four records that answer different questions. They can live in one database or several services; the important part is that they remain distinguishable and link to one another.

1. The order — what did we promise?

Store your internal order ID, customer or account, items, agreed amount, currency, tax treatment, fulfilment rule and current order state. The order exists before the payment attempt and remains the business record even if the customer tries to pay more than once.

2. The transaction — what did the provider process?

Store the provider name, provider reference, transaction ID, payment method, amount, currency, raw provider status, verified status and timestamps. One order can have several attempts. Never assume one order ID equals one provider transaction.

3. The settlement — what did the provider send to us?

Store the settlement or payout ID, included transactions, currency, gross amount, fees, deductions, net amount, destination, expected date and actual status. One settlement can contain many transactions; a settlement is not a renamed transaction.

4. The refund or dispute — what happened after the original payment?

Keep a separate refund record or dispute case with its own reference, amount, reason, status, evidence, deadline, owner and outcome. Do not erase or rewrite the original successful transaction when money is refunded or challenged.

03 — STATES ARE NOT INTERCHANGEABLE

“Success” means different things at different points in the journey.

Give each record its own state machine. Your application can map provider-specific values into a small internal vocabulary, but preserve the original provider status and payload reference so that support and finance can investigate what actually happened.

Order state

awaiting_payment → paid → fulfilment_pending → fulfilled. Other outcomes may include cancelled, refund_pending, refunded or disputed. A paid order can still be waiting for fulfilment.

Transaction state

Your internal categories might be pending, succeeded, failed, reversed and unknown. Do not invent a universal provider status list. Paystack, for example, documents values including abandoned, ongoing, pending, processing, queued, reversed and success. Its verification guide also warns that the API response's status is not the transaction status; the transaction status is in data.status. [Paystack: verify payments](https://paystack.com/docs/payments/verify-payments/).

Settlement state

Use separate states such as expected, processing, settled, failed and flagged, mapped to the provider's actual vocabulary. Paystack's Settlement API exposes settlement statuses such as success, processing, pending and failed. Flutterwave documents flagged settlements that are not released to the merchant. [Paystack Settlement API](https://paystack.com/docs/api/settlement/) · [Flutterwave settlements](https://developer.flutterwave.com/docs/payment-operations/settlements).

Refund state

Track requested, processing, needs_attention, processed and failed where your provider supports them. Do not tell a customer that the money has reached their bank merely because your dashboard says a refund was processed.

Dispute state

Track when a case was opened, when evidence is due, who owns the response, when it was submitted, and whether it was accepted, declined, reopened or closed. A dispute is a time-sensitive case, not just a negative transaction status.

Why preserve the raw provider status? Because providers do not use the same words for the same point in a process. Flutterwave's refund documentation, for example, describes completed as initiated and pending disbursement, while channel-specific states such as completed-bank-transfer and completed-momo indicate a successful refund. [Flutterwave: refunds](https://developer.flutterwave.com/docs/payment-operations/refunds).

↔
A browser success page is not a financial record.

Use it to guide the customer back into the product. Make payment decisions from authenticated provider events and server-side verification, not from a query parameter or a message supplied by the browser.

04 — RECONCILE THE MONEY

Match the order, the provider transaction and the settlement batch.

The minimum useful reconciliation chain has three identifiers:

  • Your order ID: the business obligation and what the customer bought.
  • The provider reference or transaction ID: the payment attempt and verified transaction.
  • The settlement ID: the payout batch in which the provider says the funds were included.

Join them with amount and currency. Add transaction time, payment method, provider fee, deductions, settlement date and bank credit as supporting fields. Do not match by amount alone: many customers can pay the same price.

A fictional settlement example

Three orders are paid in naira:

  • Order A: ₦25,000
  • Order B: ₦40,000
  • Order C: ₦15,000

Gross verified transactions: ₦80,000. Suppose the provider's illustrative fees total ₦1,600. The expected net settlement is ₦78,400 before other adjustments.

The bank statement shows ₦77,900. Do not change the three transactions to “failed”. Open a ₦500 settlement variance, compare the provider's settlement detail with its fee and deduction lines, assign an owner and resolve the difference.

All amounts and fees in this example are invented for teaching. They are not a provider tariff, market benchmark or claim about actual settlement behaviour.

Settlement batching is why this distinction matters. Flutterwave says a single settlement can contain several transactions, with transactions in the same batch sharing a currency; multiple settlements can occur on the same day for different currencies. [Flutterwave: settlements](https://developer.flutterwave.com/docs/payment-operations/settlements).

Run reconciliation at a frequency that matches transaction volume and cash risk. A small shop may start with a daily provider export and bank statement. A higher-volume platform should automate matching and leave a human-readable queue for exceptions.

Put these exceptions in a queue
  • Provider says success, but no matching order exists.
  • Order says paid, but no verified successful transaction exists.
  • Amount or currency differs from the order.
  • One provider reference appears against more than one order.
  • Successful transactions are missing from an expected settlement.
  • Settlement is flagged, failed, short or credited to an unexpected destination.
  • Refund or dispute adjustments do not match the provider statement.

05 — REFUNDS AND DISPUTES

A refund request is not the same as a refund received.

Write a refund policy before the first difficult case. State who can approve a refund, what qualifies, whether partial refunds are possible, how the customer is updated, and how long the provider says the process may take. The exact rights and legal requirements depend on the product, market and applicable rules; a provider's dashboard status is not a substitute for legal advice.

A safe operational sequence is:

  1. Find the original order and verified transaction.
  2. Check the policy, reason, amount and any previous refund.
  3. Approve or reject the request and record who made the decision.
  4. Submit the refund using the provider's supported process and store the refund reference.
  5. Listen for the provider's refund event or check the refund endpoint until the state is resolved.
  6. Update the customer with the actual provider state and the provider's stated time window.
  7. Reconcile the refund against the original transaction and later settlement or account adjustment.

Paystack's documentation says a refund marked processed may still take up to 10 business days for the customer to receive the funds. Flutterwave's published guidance gives different indicative windows by channel: 3–15 days for cards, 3–5 days for mobile money and up to 24 hours for bank-account refunds. These are provider- and method-specific statements, checked on 21 November 2026—not a universal promise. [Paystack refunds](https://paystack.com/docs/payments/refunds/) · [Flutterwave refunds](https://developer.flutterwave.com/docs/payment-operations/refunds).

Keep the original payment intact. A refund is a separate event with its own reference and amount. This gives finance a traceable history and prevents the original transaction from appearing as if it never happened.

Disputes need an owner and a clock.

When a chargeback or dispute arrives, preserve the order terms shown at purchase, invoice or receipt, delivery evidence, relevant customer messages, cancellation/refund policy and a concise event timeline. Use only the personal data needed to answer the case; do not collect or retain card secrets.

Provider deadlines can be much shorter than a founder expects. Paystack's support guidance, edited 16 September 2026, says its response window is 16 hours for Nigeria-based businesses and 48 hours for businesses in Ghana, South Africa and Kenya, counted on business days. It explains that the 72-hour CBN timeline referenced in its guidance is directed at banks, while the merchant receives a shorter operational deadline. Treat these as Paystack's current process—not a universal rule for every processor or a legal deadline for every merchant. Check the live dispute case and your provider's terms. [Paystack: resolving chargebacks](https://support.paystack.com/en/articles/2125698) · [weekends and public holidays](https://support.paystack.com/en/articles/2125378).

Put dispute notifications somewhere a responsible person checks every working day. A mailbox no one owns is not a dispute process.

06 — TELL CUSTOMERS WHAT YOU KNOW

Give a clear update without promising an outcome you cannot see.

Payment is still pending

“We have received your payment attempt and are checking its status with our payment provider. Please do not pay again while we confirm the result. We will update you by [time].”

Payment is verified, but fulfilment is delayed

“Your payment is confirmed. Your order is still being prepared. We have assigned the issue to our team and will update you by [time]. You do not need to make another payment.”

Refund has been submitted but is not complete

“We have submitted a refund of [amount] to the payment provider. Its current status is [provider status]. The provider's published processing window is [window for this method]. We will contact you if the status changes or more information is needed.”

A dispute has been opened

“We have received a payment dispute relating to order [reference]. Our team is reviewing the order and delivery records. If we need more information from you, we will contact you through [support channel].”

Only fill in a time or status when your system can support it. If you cannot yet confirm a payment, say that. A precise, honest update is better than “payment successful” copied from a browser redirect.

07 — BUILD THE OPERATING SYSTEM

Make the process reliable before transaction volume makes it expensive.

You do not need a large payments platform to start. You do need clear records, safe event handling and an owner for unresolved money.

1. Verify from your server

Check the provider reference, final transaction state, expected amount, currency and the order it belongs to. Flutterwave explicitly recommends checking reference, successful status, currency and amount before giving value. Paystack's verify guide explains its own transaction statuses and response fields. Implement the current instructions for the provider and payment method you use. [Flutterwave verification](https://developer.flutterwave.com/docs/transaction-verification) · [Paystack verification](https://paystack.com/docs/payments/verify-payments/).

2. Authenticate and safely process webhooks

Verify the provider's signature or secret before trusting an event. Paystack's current webhook guide describes HMAC-SHA512 verification over the raw request body with its signing secret. Flutterwave documents a configured secret hash sent in the verif-hash header. These are different schemes; do not copy one provider's implementation into another integration. [Paystack webhooks](https://paystack.com/docs/v2/developer-tools/integration/webhooks) · [Flutterwave webhooks](https://developer.flutterwave.com/docs/webhooks).

3. Make event handling idempotent

Store the provider event ID or a stable deduplication key. A repeated webhook must not create a second fulfilment, refund, credit or ledger posting. Acknowledge valid webhooks quickly, then queue slower work. Provider retry schedules differ: Paystack documents its live retry schedule; Flutterwave says retries must be enabled and then describes three retries at 30-minute intervals. Do not assume the provider will retry forever.

4. Keep an append-only history

Record each verified payment, fee, settlement, refund, reversal and dispute adjustment as its own event or ledger entry. Correct mistakes with a reversing or correcting entry. Do not silently overwrite the original transaction or delete the evidence needed to explain the customer's account.

5. Give each exception an owner

Engineering owns correct state transitions and safe retries. Finance or operations owns settlement matching and variances. Support owns clear customer updates. A named manager owns refund approval rules and dispute escalation. In a small team, one person may hold several roles, but every open case still needs one accountable owner and a due time.

For a first implementation, keep the records explicit: orders, payment_attempts, provider_events, settlements, refunds, disputes and ledger_entries. Link them with stable internal IDs and provider references. Store only the provider data you need, protect secrets, and restrict access to customer information.

08 — RUN THE DAILY CHECKLIST

Close the loop every day, not only when a customer complains.

Start with a short daily reconciliation. Increase frequency as volume, value or risk increases.

Daily payment operations
  • Find successful provider transactions with no matching order.
  • Find orders marked paid without a verified successful transaction.
  • Review pending or unknown transactions older than your operating threshold.
  • Match transactions to settlement batches by reference, amount and currency.
  • Investigate settlement variances, flagged batches and unexpected deductions.
  • Review refunds waiting for action, provider confirmation or customer receipt.
  • Review open disputes and the nearest evidence deadline.
  • Send customers the updates promised by your team.
  • Assign every unresolved item an owner, next action and due time.

Track a small set of operating measures: unmatched successful transactions, paid orders not fulfilled, settlement variance and age, refunds by state and age, disputes approaching deadline, and payment-related support contacts. A payment success rate alone cannot show whether customers received what they paid for or whether the business received the expected cash.

YOUR NEXT STEP

Trace one real purchase from order to bank statement.

Choose a recent customer payment. Find its order ID, provider reference, verified transaction, fulfilment record and settlement batch. If it was refunded or disputed, trace that record too. Write down who owns the next action at every point. If you cannot complete the chain, that missing link is your first reliability improvement.

This guide describes a general operating model. Status names, webhook authentication, retries, settlement batching, refund windows and chargeback deadlines differ by provider, country, payment method and account configuration.

LESSON / MONEY & FUNDRAISING

Three Numbers That Tell the Truth

Revenue can grow while a business quietly loses money on every sale. Contribution margin, CAC payback and burn multiple — computed weekly, on a napkin — tell a small team whether growth is building the business or burning it, before the bank balance does.

Picture a founder with a graph she is proud of: revenue doubled in a quarter, customers doubled with it, and the team celebrated both. Then she opens the bank account and the balance has fallen. Nothing was stolen, nobody lied, and the graph was accurate. The business was simply selling something that cost more to deliver and acquire than customers paid for it — and selling more of it, faster, only drained the account faster. Revenue told her the business was growing. It did not tell her whether growth was building the business or burning it. Three numbers do, and a team of three can compute all of them in an hour a week.

01 — THE VANITY TRAP

“Revenue is up” can describe a dying business perfectly accurately.

Early teams reach for the numbers that are easiest to count: revenue, signups, headcount, downloads. Each of them can rise while the business gets worse. Revenue rises when you discount hard enough. Signups rise when you run a giveaway. Headcount rises when a round closes. None of them asks the only question that decides survival: for each new customer, does the money that comes in exceed, over a reasonable time, the money that went out to win and serve them?

The trap is not that founders ignore numbers. It is that they watch the wrong ones, and the wrong ones flatter. A team that reports “revenue up 40%” in a pitch meeting sounds like it is winning; a team that reports “revenue up 40%, and each new customer costs us ₦190,000 to win and contributes ₦38,000 a month, so they pay for themselves in five months” sounds like it understands its business. Investors in a choosier market — as this desk's reading of the Q3 funding numbers showed, with capital concentrating in fewer, bigger deals — ask the second version of the question, whether or not the founder volunteers it. So does the bank balance, eventually, without waiting to be asked.

The three numbers in this lesson are not a finance department. They are a smoke alarm. Each one catches a different way a business can quietly lose money: selling at a loss (contribution margin), buying customers who never repay their cost (CAC payback), and growing in a way that consumes more cash than the growth is worth (burn multiple). Together they cover the unit, the customer, and the company. If all three are healthy, growth is probably real. If any one is bad, you know where to look first — which is the whole point of a dashboard small enough to actually use.

One warning before the maths: these numbers are only as honest as their inputs, and the most common dishonesty is accidental. Founders leave costs out — the founder's own unpaid time, the support hours, the payment fees, the refunds — because those costs do not sit in a neat invoice. The rule for this lesson is simple: if a cost would disappear if you stopped serving that customer, it belongs in the number. If it would remain — office rent, the founders' salaries at their current level — it does not, yet. That boundary is what makes the numbers computable for a small team, and it is also their main limit, which section 05 returns to.

02 — CONTRIBUTION MARGIN

What one sale actually leaves behind, after the costs of making that sale.

Contribution margin is the money left from one sale after subtracting every cost that varies with that sale: the payment fee, the delivery or hosting cost, the support time, the refunds and chargebacks, the commission. It is not revenue, and it is not profit — profit also subtracts the fixed costs, like rent and salaries, that do not change with one more sale. Contribution margin answers a narrower, more urgent question: before fixed costs, does selling one more unit help or hurt?

Contribution margin = price − variable costs of serving that sale. Expressed as a percentage of price, it tells you how much of each naira of revenue is available to pay for everything else — acquisition, salaries, rent — and eventually to keep.

Take an illustrative example, with every figure invented to show the mechanism. A small team sells a shop-management tool at ₦50,000 a month per customer. Serving each customer costs ₦12,000 a month in hosting, payment fees, onboarding and support time. Contribution per customer per month is ₦38,000, a margin of 76%. That 76% is the fuel for everything else the business does. Now change one input: suppose the team discounts to ₦30,000 a month to win customers faster, while support costs stay at ₦12,000. Contribution falls to ₦18,000 — a 60% margin — and every later calculation in this lesson gets roughly twice as hard. Discounting feels like a growth lever; through the contribution lens, it is a margin decision first.

✳
The commerce version (illustrative)

For a business that sells goods rather than subscriptions, the same question applies per order. An order priced at ₦8,000 with ₦6,400 of variable costs — stock, delivery, payment fees, packaging, expected returns — leaves ₦1,600 of contribution, a 20% margin. At that margin, a customer must order many times before the business recovers any acquisition cost at all — which is why section 03, not this one, decides whether the commerce model works. The number to watch is the same: what does one sale leave behind?

Three mistakes recur. First, counting revenue as margin. A team that treats a ₦50,000 subscription as ₦50,000 of fuel is overstating its fuel by the variable costs — in the example above, by nearly a quarter. Second, forgetting the variable costs that arrive later: refunds in month two, a support ticket that takes a founder's afternoon, a delivery partner's price rise. Estimate them honestly, even roughly, rather than leaving them at zero. Third, averaging across products or customers that have nothing in common. A high-margin enterprise customer and a loss-making small customer do not average into a healthy business; they average into a number that hides the loss-making segment. Compute contribution per segment — per price tier, per channel, per product — and the number starts telling the truth.

If contribution margin is negative, stop and fix it before anything else in this lesson matters. No acquisition strategy rescues a sale that loses money, and no funding round fixes it either — it only finances more loss-making sales. The fixes are pricing, packaging, or cost: charge more, charge for the expensive part separately, or stop serving the segment whose variable costs exceed its price. That is a hard conversation, which is exactly why the number exists: it forces the conversation before the bank balance does.

03 — CAC PAYBACK

How many months before a new customer has paid for themselves?

Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is everything spent to win a new customer — advertising, sales salaries and commissions, agency fees, events, tools — divided by the number of new customers won in the same period. CAC payback is how long that customer's contribution takes to repay it. It is the speedometer of growth: it tells you how fast cash comes back, and therefore how much cash growth consumes before it starts funding itself.

CAC payback (months) = CAC ÷ monthly contribution per customer. The denominator is contribution, not revenue — the point section 02 exists to make. Dividing by revenue instead of contribution is the single most common error here, and it always flatters the answer.

Continue the illustrative example. The team's fully loaded CAC is ₦190,000 per new customer — ads, a salesperson's time, onboarding. Monthly contribution per customer is ₦38,000. Payback is ₦190,000 ÷ ₦38,000 = 5 months. For five months, that customer is a hole in the cash position; from month six, they are funding the next customer. Now compare the flattering version: dividing the same CAC by the ₦50,000 monthly price gives 3.8 months. The business would plan its cash as if customers paid back in under four months, when the truth is five — and across a hundred customers, that gap is the difference between a planned raise and an emergency one.

Payback has no universal healthy number, and any article that gives you one without asking about retention is selling a benchmark, not a judgement. The right comparison is against how long customers actually stay. A 12-month payback is comfortable if customers stay for three years and dangerous if the average customer leaves after eight months — because a customer who leaves at month eight never pays back a 12-month acquisition cost, however good the product felt in month three. So compute payback alongside retention, by cohort and by channel: the channel with the cheapest CAC is not the best channel if its customers churn in half the time.

◈
The blended-CAC trap

One average CAC hides the channel that is destroying value. A team acquiring half its customers through referrals at ₦40,000 each and half through paid ads at ₦340,000 each reports a blended CAC of ₦190,000 and a tidy 5-month payback. The paid half, on the same ₦38,000 monthly contribution, actually takes nearly 9 months to pay back — and if paid customers churn faster than referred ones, they may never pay back at all. Report payback by channel, or the average will spend your money for you.

For businesses without subscriptions, adapt the question rather than forcing the formula: how many repeat orders, or how much total contribution, must a customer generate before their acquisition cost is repaid — and do customers actually reach that number? A commerce business with ₦1,600 of contribution per order and a ₦4,000 acquisition cost needs each customer to order at least three times just to break even on acquisition. If the average customer orders twice, the business is not acquiring customers; it is subsidising them. That sentence, computed from real numbers, is worth more than any growth chart.

When payback is too long, there are only three honest levers: lower CAC (a cheaper channel, a better conversion rate, a product people refer), raise contribution (price, or lower variable costs), or improve retention so customers live long enough to pay back. Discounting to win customers faster usually pulls two levers the wrong way at once — it lowers contribution and attracts the customers least likely to stay. That is why discount-led growth so often shows up as revenue up, cash down.

04 — BURN MULTIPLE

How many naira of cash does each new naira of recurring revenue cost?

Contribution margin judges one sale. CAC payback judges one customer. Burn multiple judges the whole company: for a given period, divide net burn — cash out minus cash in, excluding money raised — by net new annual recurring revenue (ARR) added in that period. For businesses without recurring revenue, use net new annualised revenue instead, and say so; the question is the same even if the label changes.

Burn multiple = net burn ÷ net new ARR. A multiple of 1.5 means the company burned ₦1.50 for every ₦1 of new recurring revenue it added. A multiple below 1 means it added more recurring revenue than it burned — growth funding itself. A negative multiple means the company is not burning at all: it is profitable, and the ratio stops being useful, which is a good problem.

The bands most often quoted come from David Sacks of Craft Ventures, who popularised the metric for venture-backed software companies: below 1 is exceptional, 1 to 1.5 strong, 1.5 to 2 acceptable, 2 to 3 suspect, above 3 poor. Treat those bands as a practitioner's reference for venture-stage software, not as a law and not as an African benchmark — no published Nigerian benchmark for this metric was used in this lesson, and a commerce or marketplace business will read differently from a software one. What matters more than the band is the trend: a multiple drifting from 1.2 to 2.4 across three quarters says the company is getting less efficient as it grows, even if revenue is accelerating. That drift is the early warning.

✳
Illustrative burn multiple (figures invented)

In one quarter, a team burns ₦15 million net and adds ₦10 million of net new ARR (new and expansion revenue minus churn and contraction). Its burn multiple is 1.5 — strong by the Sacks bands, and, more usefully, a number it can compare with last quarter's. If next quarter it burns ₦18 million to add the same ₦10 million, the multiple rises to 1.8: growth got 20% more expensive. The question for the founders is not whether 1.8 is “good” in the abstract, but what changed — a pricier channel, a hiring wave ahead of revenue, churn eating the new sales — and whether that change is temporary or structural.

Burn multiple also catches a gaming trick that the other two numbers miss: a company can improve its multiple by cutting the spending that creates new revenue — pausing marketing, freezing hiring — and reporting a prettier ratio while its growth stalls. A falling burn multiple alongside falling new revenue is not efficiency; it is retreat. Read the multiple together with the absolute new revenue, never alone. And remember what it excludes: it says nothing about runway. A company with a beautiful 0.8 multiple and two months of cash left still dies in two months. Runway — cash divided by monthly net burn — remains the number that sets the deadline; burn multiple says whether the spending inside that runway is buying anything durable.

05 — THE WEEKLY DASHBOARD

Three numbers, one page, every Monday — and what each bad reading means.

A dashboard only works if someone actually opens it. The version in this lesson fits on one page, updates weekly, and has exactly three lines plus runway. Update it every Monday from last week's or last month's real figures — monthly figures are fine for burn multiple, which is too noisy to read weekly, while contribution and payback can be refreshed from the latest cohort data. The discipline is the schedule, not the software: a spreadsheet updated every Monday beats a finance tool nobody opens.

Contribution margin per segment — if it falls, the problem is pricing or the cost of serving. Check for discounting, a new expensive customer segment, or a supplier or partner price rise that nobody passed on. CAC payback by channel — if it lengthens, the problem is acquisition or retention. Check whether a channel's customers churn faster than assumed, or whether CAC quietly grew because salaries and tools were left out of it. Burn multiple, quarterly trend — if it rises, the problem is company-wide efficiency. Check whether hiring ran ahead of revenue, or whether churn is eating new sales faster than the sales team can replace them. Runway — cash divided by monthly net burn — sits underneath all three as the deadline: it tells you how many months you have to fix whatever the other three reveal.

Use the numbers as tripwires, not grades. A contribution margin below zero is a stop sign: fix pricing or cost before spending another naira on growth. A payback longer than the average customer's life is a stop sign for that channel: pause it, or change who it targets. A burn multiple above 2 that is rising for two quarters is a strategy conversation, not a cost-cutting exercise: the business may be buying growth its model cannot support, and the fix may be a different customer, a different price, or a slower pace — not a cheaper office. Conversely, a team with strong numbers on all three can spend with confidence, including on the hires and channels a cautious team would fear. That is the payoff of measurement: it does not only restrain spending, it licenses it.

Know the limits, because a dashboard believed too completely is its own trap. These numbers use contribution, not full profit: a business can pass all three tests and still lose money if its fixed costs — salaries, rent, compliance — exceed the contribution its customers generate. That is the next calculation, once these three are healthy: total monthly contribution minus fixed costs must be positive, or at least on a credible path to positive within the runway. The numbers also depend on honest variable costs, and on retention estimates that early teams often guess. Where a figure is a guess, label it a guess and revisit it monthly; a dashboard that admits its unknowns is more trustworthy than one that hides them behind decimals.

06 — THE MONDAY EXERCISE

Compute yours with last month's real numbers, before next week's spending decisions.

Set aside one hour, with the bank statement, the payment dashboard, and the ad and payroll figures — not the pitch deck. Work through four steps, in order, and write the answers down where the whole team can see them.

  • Contribution first. For your main product or segment, list the price and every cost that varies with one sale or one customer-month. Subtract. If you cannot list the variable costs, your first task is to find them — ask support how many hours a customer takes, check the payment fees, count the refunds.
  • Payback second. Add up everything spent on sales and marketing last month, divide by the new customers won, and divide that CAC by monthly contribution per customer. Then split it by channel, even roughly. One channel will surprise you; that channel is the lesson.
  • Burn multiple third. Take last quarter's net burn and divide it by the net new ARR (or annualised revenue) added in the same quarter. Compare it with the quarter before. Is growth getting cheaper or more expensive — and can you name what changed?
  • Runway last. Divide cash in the bank by last month's net burn. That is how long you have to fix whatever the first three numbers showed. If the answer is under six months, the dashboard stops being a weekly exercise and becomes the week's main agenda.

Then make one decision from what you found — one price change, one channel paused, one cost renegotiated, one segment stopped — and write it next to the numbers. A dashboard that produces no decision is decoration. A dashboard that produces one decision a week, honestly measured, is how a team too small for a finance department ends up with better numbers than teams that have one: because the people reading the numbers are the same people who have to act on them, and there is nowhere for an uncomfortable truth to hide.

✳
Your Monday-morning exercise

Open last month's figures and answer three questions in writing: What does one sale leave behind? How many months until a new customer pays for themselves? How much cash did each new naira of recurring revenue cost? If any answer surprises you, that surprise is the most valuable thing the dashboard will give you all quarter — act on it before the next spending decision, not after.

THE TAKEAWAY

Watch contribution margin, CAC payback and burn multiple every week, and let them — not revenue — decide whether growth is working. Growth that does not pay for itself is not progress; it is burn with good news attached.

◈
Evidence & limits

What is established: the definitions used here are the standard practitioner definitions — CAC payback as CAC divided by monthly contribution (gross margin per customer), and burn multiple as net burn divided by net new ARR, popularised by David Sacks of Craft Ventures building on Bessemer's efficiency score. The Sacks bands (below 1 exceptional, 1–1.5 strong, 1.5–2 acceptable, 2–3 suspect, above 3 poor) are a venture-software reference, cited below, not a measured African benchmark.

Illustration: every worked figure in this lesson — the ₦50,000 subscription, ₦12,000 variable costs, ₦190,000 CAC, ₦15 million quarterly burn, the commerce order at ₦8,000, and the referral/paid channel split — is invented to show the mechanism; no figure is a market rate or a benchmark. Not established: no published Nigerian CAC, payback or burn-multiple benchmark was used, so this piece states none; founders should compare their numbers against their own history and retention, not an imported average. The funding-concentration context is this desk's Q3 lesson, linked below. Not financial advice: these are measurement tools for a founder's own business, not investment recommendations.

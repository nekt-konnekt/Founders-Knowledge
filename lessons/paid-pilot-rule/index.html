LESSON / B2B SALES & DISTRIBUTION

The Paid Pilot Rule: Selling to Businesses That Have Never Heard of You

A paid pilot is a bounded test of business value, not free custom development. Scope it around one buyer, one workflow, one measurable result, and a decision date — so the first customer teaches you whether the business can work, not just whether you can keep building.

If a pilot has no buyer, price, success measure, or decision date, it is probably unpaid product development.

“

A founder can spend months building software for a school, distributor, clinic, logistics operator, or financial-services company and still not know whether the customer will buy it. The buyer asks for a trial because they need evidence. The founder agrees because they need a reference. Then the trial grows, custom requests multiply, nobody owns the decision, and the “pilot” becomes a second product built for free. A better pilot is a small commercial agreement: one customer, one real workflow, a measurable outcome, a price, a timebox, and a date when both sides decide what happens next.

01 — A PILOT IS NOT A DEMO

A demo shows what the product can do. A pilot tests whether it creates value in the buyer’s work.

A demo can be short and free. You control the data, the scenario, and the environment. A pilot enters the customer's real workflow, where incomplete records, staff habits, unreliable connectivity, existing tools, and approval processes can change the result. It should test a business assumption that matters enough for a buyer to spend money or commit scarce staff time.

That distinction matters for small teams. A founder needs to learn whether a customer will pay, whether implementation is manageable, and whether the product can deliver the promised outcome without endless founder intervention. A buyer needs to learn whether the change is worth the cost and disruption. A well-scoped pilot gives both sides evidence.

Free pilots are not always wrong. They can make sense when a strategically important design partner provides unusual access to users or data, when the product is not yet ready to charge, or when the founder deliberately funds a tightly limited experiment. But the reason should be explicit. “They might refer us later” is not a distribution plan, and “they are a big name” is not a success metric.

02 — CHOOSE ONE COSTLY WORKFLOW

Start with a repeated problem the buyer already recognises, not a list of features.

Choose a customer segment and talk to the person who feels the problem and the person who can approve spending. They may not be the same person. Ask about the last time the problem happened, how it is handled today, what the workaround consumes, what goes wrong, and what the business has already tried. Recent behaviour is more useful than a general statement that an idea sounds interesting.

For a school, the pilot might focus on reconciling daily attendance records across classes — not “digitising the whole school.” For a distributor, it might focus on resolving stock discrepancies at one depot — not replacing every spreadsheet and accounting system. For a clinic, it might focus on a specific appointment-reminder workflow — not promising to modernise the entire practice.

These are examples of how to narrow a test, not claims that these customers have bought such products. Your job is to discover which task is genuinely painful, who owns it, and whether fixing it matters enough to fund.

◈
Ask before you build

“When did this last happen? What did it cost you in time, money, risk, or lost sales? What do you do now? Who decides whether to change it? If we improve it, what would make you pay to keep the improvement?” If the answers stay vague, keep researching instead of turning guesses into features.

03 — DEFINE THE RESULT BEFORE BUILDING

Write the success test in a sentence that both sides can check.

A useful pilot brief names five things: the workflow being tested, the starting baseline, the target result, the person responsible on each side, and the time window. Use a measure the buyer already understands: time spent reconciling records, unresolved errors, missed appointments, days to collect an invoice, or orders that need manual correction. Do not choose a metric merely because your product can count it.

✳
Illustrative example — not a customer result

A school currently spends about 45 minutes each day reconciling attendance from paper registers. A four-week pilot might test whether a defined group of classes can complete that reconciliation in 15 minutes or less, while keeping a manual fallback and recording missing or corrected entries. The numbers are invented to demonstrate how to write a test; they are not a benchmark for Nigerian schools. The actual baseline and target must come from the customer.

Set a baseline before the product goes live. Agree how you will collect the measure and who can confirm it. Include guardrails: a faster process is not a success if it creates more errors, excludes staff who cannot use the tool, or requires a founder to fix every record manually.

Keep the scope narrow enough that a small team can support it. A pilot should test the most important uncertainty, not deliver a miniature version of the entire roadmap.

04 — PRICE THE COMMITMENT

Charge for the test, and be explicit about what the fee covers.

A paid pilot does not have to cost the same as a full annual contract. Its price should reflect the implementation work, support you will provide, direct operating costs, and the value of the test to the buyer. It should be large enough to represent a real commercial decision, not so small that you quietly subsidise weeks of custom work.

Write down what is included: setup, number of users or sites, data import, training, support hours, and any integration. Write down what is excluded: new modules, unlimited revisions, extra branches, and bespoke reports not required for the agreed test. If the buyer wants more, quote the change before doing it.

If a buyer refuses to pay, do not argue that every pilot must be paid. Ask what the refusal means. Is there no budget? Is the problem not urgent? Is procurement blocking the purchase? Does the buyer lack authority? A no-payment experiment can still be useful if it has a clear learning objective, strict limits, access to the people who will use the product, and a scheduled decision. If none of those commitments is available, politely stop.

Do not use a discount to hide uncertainty. If you offer to credit the pilot fee against a longer contract, write that term down. Make the full price and the conditions for moving to it visible before the pilot starts.

05 — THE ONE-PAGE PILOT AGREEMENT

Put the commercial and operational boundaries on paper.

Before work begins, make sure the buyer and your team agree on these points:

  • Buyer and owner: who approves spending, who uses the product, and who signs off on the result.
  • Scope: the exact workflow, site, users, integrations, deliverables, and exclusions.
  • Baseline and success measure: how the starting point is recorded, the target, the data source, and the person who confirms it.
  • Duration and fee: start date, end date, payment terms, support allowance, and what triggers additional charges.
  • Customer responsibilities: access, staff participation, data quality, approvals, and a named contact who can unblock the work.
  • Data and security: what information is accessed, where it is stored, who can see it, how long it is retained, and how it is returned or deleted.
  • Conversion and exit: the full-price offer, the decision date, the steps to go live, and what happens if either side stops.

If the pilot touches personal data in Nigeria, treat privacy and security as part of the product, not paperwork to postpone. Review the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 and applicable guidance, agree the parties’ responsibilities, and seek qualified local advice where needed. This article is practical business guidance, not legal advice.

06 — AGREE ON THE DECISION DATE

The pilot should end with a decision, not another vague extension.

Before kickoff, agree what happens at the review. There are three honest outcomes:

Buy: the agreed result was achieved, the buyer sees sufficient value, and the parties move to the contract already discussed.

Extend for a defined reason: a named external dependency prevented a fair test, and both sides agree on the additional evidence needed, the new end date, and any extra fee. An extension is not a reward for missing the target.

Stop: the result was not achieved, the problem was less important than expected, the buyer cannot support implementation, or the economics do not work. Record what was learned, close access safely, settle outstanding payments, and move on.

Do not treat a friendly comment, a request for more features, or a promise to revisit the budget next quarter as a sale. The evidence is a signed agreement, a payment, a named decision-maker, or another concrete commitment appropriate to the business. Interest is useful; it is not revenue.

07 — A PRACTICAL 30-DAY TEST

Use a month to test the offer, not to promise a universal sales cycle.

DAYS 1–7 · DISCOVER

Interview people in one defined customer group. Map the current workaround, buyer, cost of the problem, existing alternatives, and the evidence needed to justify a change.

DAYS 8–14 · SELL THE TEST

Send a one-page pilot proposal with the scope, measure, fee, responsibilities, and decision date. Ask for a clear yes, no, or a named blocker. Do not build a custom integration just to keep a conversation alive.

DAYS 15–28 · RUN A BOUNDED PILOT

If the buyer commits and the workflow allows it, deliver the agreed test. Record the baseline, support time, errors, result, and every request outside scope. If procurement or implementation takes longer, keep the decision date explicit rather than pretending every sale fits a month.

DAYS 29–30 · DECIDE AND LEARN

Review the evidence with the buyer. Convert to a contract, extend only for a named reason, or stop. Compare the revenue with implementation and support costs. Use the objections and outcomes to improve the next offer.

One paid pilot does not prove repeatable distribution or product-market fit. The next test is whether a similar customer will buy a similar offer without an entirely new product build or extraordinary founder effort. Repeatability begins when you can explain how the buyer was reached, why they bought, what it cost to serve them, and what made them stay.

THE TAKEAWAY

A pilot is a commercial test with a boundary and a decision date. Sell the test before you build around it, measure the result the buyer values, and stop when the evidence says stop. Do not confuse a busy pilot with a business.

Evidence & limits

What is established: this article's pilot structure is an original practical framework, not a claim that one duration, price, or conversion rate works for every B2B sale. The suggested 30-day sequence is a planning aid; public-sector procurement, enterprise security reviews, integrations, and complex workflows can take longer.

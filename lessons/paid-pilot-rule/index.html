The Paid Pilot Rule

A free pilot feels like progress until it eats your quarter. Charge for the pilot, name the person who can say yes, and set the date it ends in a contract or a clean no — or walk away.

The email every business-to-business founder wants arrives sooner than expected: a large company wants to try your product. Not buy it — try it. A pilot, they say. Three months, every branch, free — because if it works, they will roll it out everywhere. It feels like the door opening. For an unknown startup selling to businesses that have never heard of it, it is usually the door closing slowly: a quarter spent serving a customer who never had to decide, while the buyers who might actually have paid go uncalled.

01 — THE FREE-PILOT TRAP

A free pilot is not a sale. It is a favour with a roadmap attached.

What the buyer is really asking for is work without a buying decision attached: do the work, carry the risk, and let us decide later whether any of it was worth anything. There is no budget line, no commitment, and no cost to saying yes — and no cost to walking away. That asymmetry is the whole trap. It plays out the same way almost every time, through four mechanics worth naming before you accept one.

First, no budget means no priority. Inside the customer, free work queues behind paid work. Your champion cannot escalate something that costs nothing; when their boss asks what they are working on, your pilot is not on the list. Meetings slip, feedback waits, and your follow-ups start to feel like pestering — because, from inside their building, they are.

Second, no decision-maker. The person who asked for the pilot is rarely the person who can sign a contract. A free pilot never forces that introduction, because nobody needs approval to accept something free. You can spend a quarter impressing someone whose approval was never the approval you needed.

Third, scope grows. Without a price, every department adds a requirement. You came to test one workflow; you leave building their wishlist, still unpaid, because there was never a moment where anyone had to say what this was worth.

Fourth, no end. “Let us extend another month” costs the customer nothing, so the pilot never ends in a decision. It ends in silence, or in a new contact who has never heard of you, and the quarter is gone either way.

The real cost here is not your hours. It is the quarter. A three-month free pilot consumes the selling time that could have produced five real prospects. For a two-person team, that is not a delay. That is the runway.

✳
The test

If the pilot ended tomorrow with a no, would you have anything to show for it — a decision, a relationship with a buyer, a report someone paid for? If the answer is no, you are not running a pilot. You are donating a quarter.

02 — WHY PRICE CHANGES BEHAVIOUR

Price is a diagnostic, not just revenue.

Charging for a pilot is often read as confidence or greed. It is neither. It is an instrument: a price forces three revelations that a free pilot hides.

It reveals the budget — and therefore the buyer. Someone must approve spend, and that person is usually the decision-maker or sits next to them. The price buys you the introduction a free pilot never will: the meeting where the person who can say yes is in the room.

It creates attention. Paid work gets used. Users show up to sessions, managers check progress, the champion defends the project internally — because their budget is now attached to it. Free software gets installed and ignored, and then the pilot “fails” for reasons that had nothing to do with your product.

It tells the truth about the problem. A buyer who will not pay a small fee to solve a painful problem does not have a painful problem — or you have not found the person who feels the pain. The price tells you that in week one. A free pilot hides it for a quarter.

A paid pilot is also a rehearsal. Procurement, legal, security review, invoicing — the whole machinery of the real sale, exercised at small scale. If a customer cannot run a small purchase, they cannot run a big one. Better to learn that before you have built anything than after.

Price protects you, too. It caps scope — they bought this, not everything. It funds your time. And it gives you a clean exit line when the pilot ends: here is the report, here is what we learned, here is the decision we agreed to make.

03 — THE RULE

Price, date, decision-maker. All three, in writing, before work starts.

Here is the rule, stated so it can be checked: a pilot is real only if it has a price, an end date, and a named decision-maker with a decision date. All three, agreed in writing, before work starts. Missing any one, it is not a pilot. It is a favour, and favours do not close.

Put it on one page — a pilot term sheet, seven lines, sent before you write a line of custom code:

  • Scope: one workflow, one team, named users. Not “the company”.
  • Price: what it costs, and what it covers.
  • Dates: a start date and an end date. No open-ended pilots.
  • Success criteria: numbers agreed in advance — “stock-outs on tracked lines fall 20%”, not “the team likes it”.
  • Decision date: the meeting, on the calendar, before the pilot starts.
  • Decision-maker: a name and a title, not a department.
  • On yes, on no: what happens in each case — contract terms, and whether the fee is credited; on no, a clean exit, they keep the report, you keep the learning.

The term sheet is not bureaucracy. It is the pilot. Everything after it is execution. And it has a useful side effect: a buyer who will sign a one-page term sheet has already done the hardest part of buying — deciding, in writing, that this matters enough to decide about.

◈
The line

If the buyer will not name the decision-maker, you do not have a pilot. You have a pen pal.

04 — HOW TO PRICE IT

Never negotiate the price to zero. Negotiate the scope down.

Three anchors, in order. First, the cost floor: what your time for the pilot weeks actually costs. Never go below it. If you cannot name your cost, you cannot name your price — work that out before the negotiation, not during it.

Second, the value anchor: a small fraction of the first year's contract value. A single-digit percentage is a reasonable starting heuristic — a heuristic, not a market rate. Adjust it to your costs and their budget, and say so when you do.

Third, the credit: the pilot fee credited against year one if they sign. This removes the “we are paying twice” objection without removing the commitment, and it is the single most useful sentence in a pilot negotiation: “The fee comes off year one.”

Then the objection you will actually hear: “We never pay for pilots.” Two moves, in order. First, the smaller paid step: a fixed-fee diagnostic — two weeks, a report they keep, a price one manager can approve without a committee. It often converts, because it is the same machinery at smaller scale. Second, walk away, politely, and say what would change your mind: “Happy to run this when there is a budget line for it — here is the one-pager for when that exists.” Then spend the quarter elsewhere.

There is one exception, and it has a condition. A logo that unlocks a named set of next customers can be worth a free pilot — but only if the trade is explicit, written, and capped: a case study plus introductions to two named peers, delivered before you start, with a date. If they will not put the trade in writing, it is not a trade. It is the same trap with better lighting.

05 — THE MOOVN MIRROR

Even a global network stages trust.

On 19 October 2026 in Nairobi, Mastercard and Moovn Technologies signed a five-year strategic agreement: virtual and physical prepaid Mastercard cards, online and in-app acceptance through Mastercard Payment Gateway Services, Pay Local merchant payments, peer-to-peer transfers and eligible cross-border remittances through Mastercard Move, integrated into MoovnPay across Kenya and Tanzania and other African markets. Those are the reported facts, from Mastercard's own announcement.

But the deal did not start there. MoovnPay was selected for Mastercard's Start Path Emerging Fintech programme in September 2025, one of 11 fintechs. Since then the companies moved from early engagement to commercial rollout, including Moovn's launch of the gateway service in Kenya; the next phase covers the Shooga prepaid card portfolio and Pay Local expansion. Financial terms were not disclosed, and this lesson does not guess them.

Here is the read — and it is interpretation, not reporting. The shape of this deal is the shape of the rule. Programme, then gateway launch, then cards: stages, each with its own decision, over more than a year. Nobody handed Moovn a five-year deal on a cold call, and Moovn did not earn it by working free for a year. It entered a structured programme and passed through its gates. Your pilot is the same shape at your scale: a stage with a price, a date, and a decision.

Keep the parallel honest. Moovn is a payments company with an existing wallet, a merchant base and a corridor a network wanted; you are an unknown startup. The lesson is the structure of trust, not a promise that a programme will find you, and not a claim that Moovn ran anything like a paid pilot. What transfers is this: big partners do not hand out trust. They stage it. Build your stages.

06 — THE MONDAY EXERCISE

Run your next pilot request through the rule before you answer it.

Illustrative example. Amara runs a two-person team in Lagos selling a stock-tracking tool to independent pharmacies. A 40-store chain asks for a free three-month pilot across all stores. Every figure that follows is invented for teaching, not a market rate.

She counters with the rule. Four weeks, five stores — not three months, not forty. A pilot fee (illustrative: ₦500,000) credited against an annual contract (illustrative: ₦2.4 million). Success criteria agreed in advance: stock-outs on tracked lines down 20%, weekly counts completed by store staff without her team chasing. A decision meeting booked with the operations director, by name, in week five. On yes, rollout terms. On no, a report — and the chain's introduction to two other operators, agreed in writing before the pilot starts.

If the chain refuses all three terms — no price, no date, no decision-maker — she walks away. The pilot she turned down costs her nothing. The quarter she would have spent on it costs her everything, and she spends it instead on five prospects who will pay.

Before you say yes to any pilot, answer these five questions. If any answer is blank, the pilot is not ready:

  • What is the price?
  • What are the dates?
  • Who decides, by name?
  • What numbers decide yes or no?
  • What happens on no?
THE TAKEAWAY

A pilot without a price, a date, and a decision-maker is not a sales process. It is a favour — and favours do not close.

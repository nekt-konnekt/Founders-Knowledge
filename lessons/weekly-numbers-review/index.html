The 30-Minute Weekly Numbers Review: Five Numbers, One Page, Every Week

A monthly check finds problems after they are expensive. Five numbers, checked every week at the same time, find them while a phone call still fixes them.

A founder opens her banking app on the last day of the month. The balance is lower than she expected. A customer has not paid. A tool renewed early. One office stopped ordering and nobody noticed. 01 — THE MONTHLY CHECK IS TOO LATE

A month is four chances to miss a problem.

Most small teams check their numbers once a month — if they check at all. The monthly habit feels serious: a proper look, once a month, with the accountant or the spreadsheet. But a month is a long time for a small company. A customer who stops paying in week one has had four weeks to become a debt by the time the monthly check finds it. A cost that crept up in week two has had three more weeks to repeat. A product fault that started in week one has had a month to lose customers.

The mechanism is simple: the cost of a problem grows with time. A late payment is a phone call in week one and a lost customer in week five. A small cost overrun is a cancelled subscription in week one and a salary problem in week five. So the question is not how carefully you check. It is how soon you check. The gap between a problem starting and a founder seeing it is called detection time — and a monthly check makes that gap up to four weeks long. A weekly check makes it at most seven days.

That is the whole argument for this lesson. You do not need a better spreadsheet. You need a shorter gap between the problem and your eyes on it.

02 — WHY WEEKLY WORKS

Shorten the gap between a problem starting and you seeing it.

A weekly review works for three reasons. First, the fix is still small. In week one, a late payment needs a call. In week five, it needs a new customer to replace the money. A cost surprise in week one needs one cancellation. In week five, it needs a salary conversation. Small problems have small fixes; old problems have expensive ones.

Second, the cause is still findable. On a Monday, you can remember what happened on Tuesday: the driver was sick, the customer asked for more time, the tool sent its renewal notice. A month later, nobody remembers, and the review turns into guesswork. Weekly numbers are fresh enough to explain.

Third, the comparison is honest. A weekly review compares this week to last week — two real weeks, side by side. A monthly review compares this month to a plan written in January, when the company was different. A number that moved since last week is a question. A number that moved twice in a row is a problem. That is the whole test, and it only works when the numbers are close together in time.

✳
The rule

Check often enough that a problem cannot grow expensive between checks. For most small teams, that means weekly.

03 — THE FIVE NUMBERS

Five numbers, one page.

The review has five numbers. Five, not fifteen. A review with twenty numbers is a report nobody reads. Each number answers one question, and together they cover the whole company: the money, the customers, and whether the product works.

  • 1. Cash in the bank — and how many weeks it covers. The bank balance today, divided by what the company spends in an average week. That is your cover: how many weeks until the money runs out at the current pace. If the balance is ₦2,100,000 and the team spends ₦450,000 a week, the cover is just under five weeks. The balance alone tells you nothing; the cover tells you how much time you have.
  • 2. Money in this week, against what you expected. Cash that actually arrived — not invoices sent, not promises made. If ₦650,000 was expected and ₦480,000 arrived, the gap is ₦170,000, and it has a name on it: who did not pay, and how late are they? An invoice is not cash. Only money in the account counts.
  • 3. Money out this week, against the plan. What left the account, and was anything a surprise? A renewal that came early, a fee nobody flagged, a cost that arrived a month ahead of schedule. Surprises are the point of this number: planned costs are already in the cash sheet, so this number catches the ones that were not.
  • 4. Customers: won, lost, and gone quiet. New customers this week, customers who left, and customers who have gone quiet — no order, no login, no reply. Quiet is the early warning. A customer who leaves loudly is already gone; a customer who goes quiet can still be won back. Count all three every week, even when the numbers are small.
  • 5. The one number that says whether the product works. The founder picks one: orders delivered on time, active users, failed payments, support tickets, repeat orders. It changes as the company changes — a delivery business watches on-time deliveries, a software business watches active users — but there is only ever one. If everything is the key number, nothing is.

That is the whole page. Cash and cover. Money in. Money out. Customers. One product number. The cash-sheet lesson from this week is the tool behind number one: the 13-week sheet is where the cover number comes from, and the FX check from our pricing lesson is one line inside number three. This review is the ritual that holds those tools together.

04 — HOW TO RUN IT

Same time, same page, thirty minutes.

The ritual is fixed: same time, same page, every week. Monday morning, before the week starts — because a decision made on Monday can still land a payment or cut a cost this week. Friday is too late. The week is spent.

  • 1. Update the five numbers — 10 minutes. From the source, never from memory: the banking app for cash, the sales record for money in and out, the customer list for won, lost and quiet, the product for the one product number. If a number cannot be found in ten minutes, the company needs a better record, not a better review.
  • 2. Compare each number to last week — 5 minutes. Circle anything that moved the wrong way. A number that moved once is a question. A number that moved twice in a row is a problem. Do not circle everything — a review that flags every number flags nothing.
  • 3. Ask why, once per circled number — 10 minutes. Not a meeting. One sentence each. The customer paid late because their own customer paid them late. The tool renewed early because the annual plan started last November. The deliveries slipped because one driver was covering two routes. If you cannot answer why in one sentence, that is the first thing to find out.
  • 4. Decide one fix and write it down with a date — 5 minutes. One fix, not five. A call, a cancellation, a changed date, a split route. Write who does it and by when. A fix without a date is a worry, not a plan.

That is the whole ritual. Thirty minutes, four steps, one fix. One person can run it alone; if the team is two or more, both should see the same page. The point is a shared picture of the week, not a secret spreadsheet.

05 — APPLY IT

Run one real week through it.

None of those is visible in the balance. All three are visible in the review.

And put the tool's renewal date in the cash sheet, so it never surprises again. If the hotel pays its ₦170,000 next week, the cash becomes ₦2,270,000 and the cover moves from 4.7 weeks to 5.0 weeks — the point is not the half-week, it is that the fix is two phone calls made in week one, not a crisis in week five.

The line

06 — WHAT COULD CHANGE THE ANSWER

Four limits on everything above.

First, the review is only as good as the numbers. If the bank balance is a week old, the sales record is a guess, or the customer list is out of date, the review is fiction — neat, confident fiction. Update from the source every week, or do not run the review at all. A stale review gives false confidence, which is worse than no review.

Second, five numbers, not fifteen. If a number never changes a decision, drop it. If a number always looks fine, replace it with one that might not. The review earns its thirty minutes by changing what you do on Monday. A number that never changes anything is decoration.

Third, weekly is a default, not a law. A business that takes money every day — a shop, a food stall — may need a daily look at one number. A business that signs one deal a quarter may review fortnightly. The rule is the same either way: review often enough that a problem cannot grow expensive between reviews. Pick the rhythm that fits how fast your money moves, then keep it fixed.

Fourth, the review finds problems; it does not fix the business. If the numbers say the product loses money on every sale, the fix is the price or the cost — not a better review. For that, work the three numbers in our lesson on unit economics: contribution margin, CAC payback and burn multiple. And if the numbers say the money runs out in thirteen weeks, the answer is a plan, not a ritual: our lesson on building revenue-first is that plan.

07 — THE MONDAY EXERCISE

Run your first review this Monday, in 30 minutes.

  • Pick a time — Monday morning, same time every week — and put it in the calendar now, repeating. The fixed time is half the habit.
  • Take one page. Write the five numbers at the top: cash and cover, money in, money out, customers, your one product number.
  • Fill in this week's numbers from the source: the banking app, the sales record, the customer list, the product. Never from memory.
  • Circle anything that moved the wrong way since last week. Ask why, once each, in one sentence.
  • Decide one fix, write it down with a date and a name, and do it before Friday.
  • Put next Monday in the calendar now. The review only works if it repeats.

If nothing is circled, you have something just as useful: proof, in writing, that the week was healthy — and the freedom to spend Monday building instead of worrying.

THE TAKEAWAY

A monthly check tells you what went wrong. A weekly review tells you while you can still fix it.

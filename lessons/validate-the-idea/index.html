IDEA VALIDATION / PRACTICAL GUIDE

Validate the idea before you build.

An idea can sound convincing and still describe a problem people will not prioritize. Learn what to test before committing your time and money.

Validation is not asking people to approve your idea. It is learning whether the problem matters enough to change behaviour.

“

The most expensive assumption is often the one a founder never tests. Building can feel like progress because the work is visible. Validation is quieter: it checks whether the work is pointed at a problem worth solving.

01 — MAKE THE INVISIBLE VISIBLE

Every idea contains assumptions.

You may believe a group of people has a problem, that the problem is painful, that your proposed solution fits their lives, and that they will adopt or pay for it. These are separate beliefs, and each can be wrong.

Write down the assumptions behind your idea before you try to prove them. Circle the ones that would make the whole idea fail if they turned out to be false.

✳
Start with the riskiest assumption

Do not spend a week polishing what you can test with a short conversation or a simple experiment.

02 — UNDERSTAND THE REAL WORLD

Test the problem before pitching the solution.

Talk to people who plausibly experience the problem. Ask about a recent real situation rather than asking them to imagine a hypothetical future.

QUESTIONS THAT REVEAL BEHAVIOUR

  • When did this last happen?
  • What did you do to handle it?
  • What was frustrating, costly, risky, or slow?
  • How often does it happen, and what happens if you leave it unsolved?
  • Have you already spent time or money trying to fix it?

Listen for specifics. “That sounds useful” is weak evidence. A detailed account of repeated workarounds, missed outcomes, or existing spending gives you something more concrete to investigate.

03 — SEPARATE OPINION FROM EVIDENCE

Look for what people already do.

People can be sincere and still mispredict what they will use. Past behaviour is not perfect evidence of future behaviour, but it is usually more informative than compliments or promises.

01Weak signal

“I would use this.” A like, compliment, or enthusiastic conversation.

02Stronger signal

They share a real workflow, try a relevant prototype, introduce a decision-maker, or commit meaningful time or money.

What counts as a meaningful commitment depends on the audience and the problem. Do not confuse a single positive response with proof of a market.

04 — RUN A FIVE-CUSTOMER LEARNING ROUND

Five conversations are a starting point, not proof of a market.

Five interviews can expose weak assumptions before you spend weeks building. They cannot tell you what percentage of a market has a problem, and five polite yeses do not prove demand. Treat this as a first learning round: a way to discover what to investigate or test next.

Start with one narrow customer segment and one repeated job. Recruit five people who actually do, experience or decide about that job. Do not fill the list with friends, other founders or people who like your idea but do not live with the problem.

THE FIVE-CUSTOMER LEARNING LOOP

  1. Choose one job. Name the task, the person who performs it and the situation in which it happens. “Small businesses need better software” is too broad. “Independent pharmacies reorder fast-moving medicines when stock falls below a working level” is a job you can investigate.
  2. Recruit people who do that job. Keep the segment narrow enough that you can compare their routines. If the person using the product and the person approving the purchase are different, include both roles or run separate rounds.
  3. Replay the last real occurrence. Ask the participant to walk through the most recent time they did the task. Start with what happened, not with your feature idea.
  4. Trace the workaround and its cost. Find the tools, calls, people, waiting, duplicate entry, lost sales, mistakes or risk involved. Ask what they already spend in money and time.
  5. Choose the next test. Compare the evidence across interviews. Decide whether to narrow the segment, investigate a different problem, test a manual service, run a small pilot or stop.

Recruit where the work already happens. In an African market, a useful introduction may come from an existing customer, a supplier, a distributor, a trade association, a cooperative, a professional group or a local business cluster. You can ask a WhatsApp community administrator to share an opt-in invitation, or visit a workplace or market with permission. Do not scrape group members, send unsolicited bulk messages or treat membership as consent. A warm introduction is a route to a conversation, not proof that the person is a good participant.

If you reimburse transport or data, explain the amount in advance and pay for the participant's time, not for a positive answer. Ask permission before recording. Let people hide customer names, account details and other private information. Your notes should describe the workflow, not become a store of their sensitive business records.

OPEN WITH THE LAST TIME, NOT YOUR PITCH

“Thank you for speaking with me. I am trying to understand how you handle [specific task]. I am not selling anything today. I would like to hear about the last time you did it. There are no right answers, and I will not record or use your name without permission.”

Then ask one question at a time:

  1. When did you last do this? Can you walk me through what happened from the beginning?
  2. What started the task? Who else was involved?
  3. What did you use? What happened next?
  4. Where did you wait, call someone, copy information, travel or use another workaround?
  5. What happens when the task is late, incomplete or wrong?
  6. How often does this happen? What did the last instance cost in time, money, missed sales or risk?
  7. What have you already tried? What did you spend or give up to make it work?
  8. Who can approve a change, and who would pay for it?

Where possible, ask the person to show you the process on the device, paper record or workspace they actually use. Watch the handoffs and exceptions. Do not assume the manager's description is the same as the operator's experience.

Avoid questions such as “Would you use an app that does this?”, “Would you pay ₦10,000 for it?” or “Don't you find this process frustrating?” They invite the participant to agree with your idea or your wording. Ask what they did last time, what they already pay for, who made the decision and what they would have to stop doing to change.

01Weak evidence

A compliment, a like, “I would use it”, or a promise to try a feature that has not been built.

02Stronger evidence

A recent example, a visible workaround, an existing expense, a repeated delay, a named decision-maker or a concrete next step the participant is willing to take.

After each conversation, separate what you observed, what the participant reported, what you inferred and what remains unknown. Record the exact words that explain the problem. If two people describe different workflows, do not average the difference away; check whether they have different roles, business sizes or customer types.

After five interviews, do not write “60% of the market has this problem” because three people mentioned it. You do not have a representative sample. Ask instead: did the same job and costly workaround appear in this narrow segment? Can you see how people solve it today? Is there a buyer who can authorise a change? If the evidence is promising, test the smallest paid or manually delivered version before building the full feature. If the stories are vague or inconsistent, recruit more precisely or observe the work again.

Download the Five-Customer Interview Guide (CSV) to record the task, workaround, cost, role, evidence type and next test without collecting unnecessary personal information.

05 — DESIGN THE SMALLEST TEST

Run an experiment that can change your mind.

Choose the smallest ethical, low-cost test that distinguishes between competing explanations. You might interview a focused group, offer a manual service before automating it, show a prototype, invite a small pilot, or test whether people take a concrete next step.

WRITE YOUR TEST

We believe [specific audience] experiences [specific problem]. We will test it by [small experiment]. We will look for [observable behaviour]. If we see [predefined threshold], we will [next decision].

Decide in advance what result would support, weaken, or leave your belief unresolved. This reduces the temptation to reinterpret every outcome as success.

06 — LEARN WITHOUT FOOLING YOURSELF

Negative evidence is useful too.

If people do not act, investigate why. You may have the wrong audience, a low-priority problem, a weak offer, poor timing, or a test that makes it difficult to respond. One failed test does not automatically invalidate an idea; repeated evidence should still change your confidence.

Keep three lists: what you observed, what you infer from it, and what you still do not know. This prevents assumptions from quietly becoming “facts.”

!
Reality check

Validation reduces uncertainty; it does not eliminate it. Small samples, polite respondents, and unusual early adopters can mislead you.

PUT IT TO WORK

Your next step.

Before building the full version, complete this short plan:

  1. Name the specific people who experience the problem.
  2. Write the three assumptions most likely to make your idea fail.
  3. Speak with five relevant people about recent real experiences, not your pitch.
  4. Use the Five-Customer Interview Guide to record what they did, what it cost and what remains uncertain.
  5. Choose one behaviour that would count as stronger evidence than praise.
  6. Run the smallest test and decide what you will do with the result.
! Keep the scope honest

Five conversations are a starting point for learning, not a representative sample or statistical proof. If the people you interview have different jobs or the evidence remains unclear, run another targeted round before deciding what to build.

THE TAKEAWAY

Do not ask whether your idea sounds good. Find out whether the problem is real, important, and worth acting on.

◈
Evidence & limits

The five-customer learning loop and the recruitment suggestions are Founders Knowledge editorial methods, not a statistically validated sampling rule. Five interviews can reveal useful questions and workarounds; they cannot estimate how common a problem is across a market. GOV.UK and NCVO user-research guidance support recruiting current or likely users, obtaining consent, using open and neutral questions, and asking about real past behaviour. Those sources are methodological guidance, not evidence about African customer behaviour. Adapt the recruitment route to the actual segment and location.

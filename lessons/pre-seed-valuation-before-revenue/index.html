LESSON / MONEY & FUNDRAISING

What Is Your Startup Worth Before Revenue?

With no revenue, there is nothing to multiply — so founders reach for a number that sounds like confidence and investors hear a claim on their return. Your pre-seed valuation is set by evidence and alternatives, not ambition: what you have de-risked, what comparable founders actually raised, and what the next round must believe. Get it wrong in either direction and you pay for it later.

Ask a pre-seed founder what their company is worth and you will usually get one of two answers: a number borrowed from a louder founder's announcement, or a number reverse-engineered from how much money they want. Neither is a valuation. A valuation before revenue is a negotiation about risk — how much of the risk has already been retired, how much remains, and what return the person taking that risk needs if the bet pays off. Once you see it that way, the conversation changes: you stop defending a number and start presenting evidence.

01 — THE NUMBER IS NOT A MEASUREMENT

Before revenue, valuation is a negotiation about risk.

A revenue-stage company can be valued by a multiple: take a real number, multiply it by what comparable companies fetch, argue about the multiple. A pre-revenue company has no real number, so the usual machinery has nothing to grip. What replaces it is judgement, structured by methods and disciplined by alternatives — the investor's alternative is every other pre-seed deal they could fund, and yours is every other way of financing the next twelve months, including not raising at all.

That is why two honest, well-informed people can look at the same pre-seed startup and disagree by a factor of two without either of them being foolish. The disagreement is not about arithmetic; it is about how much risk they think remains and how much return that risk demands. A founder who treats the number as a verdict on their worth will defend it emotionally. A founder who treats it as a risk price will trade it intelligently — for a better partner, a cleaner structure, or a milestone that raises the next number.

The market you negotiate in is also choosier than the headlines suggest. Africa: The Big Deal's data for January to September 2026, as reported by BusinessDay and Radarr Africa, puts Nigeria at $635 million raised — the continent's top market — but four deals (Moove, Flutterwave, Jumia, Yellow Card) account for $440 million, about 69% of it. Across the continent, the count of ventures raising at least $100,000 fell to 269 from 332 a year earlier. The money is real, and it is concentrated. Your valuation conversation happens in a market where most early cheques are contested, not distributed — which makes a defensible number more valuable, not less.

✳
The one sentence to carry into the room

“Here is the risk we have already retired, here is the risk that remains, and here is the milestone this money buys.” If you cannot say that sentence, no method in section 03 will save the negotiation — because you are asking an investor to price something you have not described.

02 — WHAT INVESTORS ARE ACTUALLY PRICING

Five things move a pre-seed number. Everything else is decoration.

1 · The team, specifically. Not “a great team” — evidence that this team can survive the next eighteen months of this specific problem: domain experience, a builder who has shipped, a seller who has sold to this buyer. An investor is pricing execution risk, and the only evidence for it before revenue is what these people have already done under pressure.

2 · A product that exists. A prototype retires technology risk: the thing can be built. A prototype with real users retires more: somebody wants it. The gap between a pitch deck and a working product is the single largest valuation step most pre-seed founders can take, because it converts a claim (“we can build this”) into a fact (“we built this”).

3 · Access to a market. Distribution before revenue looks like letters of intent, a waitlist with named buyers, a channel partner who will actually take the call, or a founder who already sells into the target market. A large market nobody can reach is worth less than a small market one founder already stands inside.

4 · Traction that is not vanity. Ten paying pilot customers beat ten thousand free signups, because payment is the only traction signal that survives scrutiny. If you have no paying users, say so — and show the closest honest proxy: repeat usage, a waitlist that converted, a pilot that renewed. Investors discount any metric you clearly chose because it flatters you.

5 · The distance to the next round. A pre-seed cheque buys a milestone, and the milestone must be one a seed investor will pay for. “Build the product” is not a milestone; “ten paying clinics in Lagos, with churn under 5%, ready for a seed round” is. The closer and more credible the path to the next round, the less risk the pre-seed investor carries — and the more the number can bear.

Notice what is absent: the size of the total addressable market slide, the number of competitors you claim to have none of, and the founder's own salary expectations. A trillion-naira market does not raise your valuation if you cannot reach the first hundred customers. Investors price the next eighteen months, not the next decade.

03 — FOUR METHODS, USED HONESTLY

Four methods, each honest about what it cannot do.

No single method is correct. Experienced investors run two or three as cross-checks and negotiate where the ranges overlap. Use them the same way: if three methods point to roughly the same range, you have a negotiation position. If they point in wildly different directions, one of your inputs is fantasy — find it before an investor does.

The Berkus method — score what you have de-risked. Angel investor Dave Berkus's method assigns value to five risk-reducing elements: a sound idea, a working prototype, a quality management team, strategic relationships, and product rollout or early sales. In its original form each element is worth up to $500,000, capping the valuation at $2.5 million pre-money. Treat the dollar figures as the method's original US-era anchors, not as a Nigerian price list — there is no published, reliable median for Nigerian pre-seed valuations to anchor to, and importing a US ceiling as a local fact would be exactly the kind of invented precision this lesson warns against. Use Berkus as a checklist: every element you cannot score is a visible gap, and a gap is a discount the investor will apply whether or not you name it.

The Scorecard method — compare, then adjust. Start from the average valuation of recently funded startups in your region, stage and sector, then adjust up or down on weighted factors — team strength (around 25–30%), market opportunity (20–25%), product or technology, competitive environment, channels, and need for further funding. Its honesty depends entirely on the baseline: a baseline built from three cherry-picked announcements is worse than no baseline. Build yours from disclosed rounds you can verify — the fund's own announcement, the company's statement, a reputable tracker naming the deal — and write down the deals, not just the average. If you cannot find enough comparable local deals, say that: a thin market is itself information, and it argues for a range, not a point.

The venture capital method — work backwards from the exit. Start from a plausible exit value, apply the return the investor needs (pre-seed investors commonly need a multiple in the teens or higher, because most of their bets return nothing), and discount for the dilution between now and exit. This method is the most honest about the investor's actual maths — and the most dangerous in a founder's hands, because it invites inventing an exit. Use it to test your number, not to set it: if your proposed valuation requires an exit larger than any comparable company in your market has achieved, your number is a story about a market that does not exist yet.

Round arithmetic — the method that ends arguments. Post-money valuation equals the money raised divided by the percentage of the company sold: $150,000 for 10% implies a $1.5 million post-money valuation, whatever anyone's spreadsheet says. Every offer can be translated into this form, and every translation should be done out loud before signing. It is also the method that exposes the most common pre-seed confusion — pre-money versus post-money. A $2 million pre-money valuation on a $500,000 raise is a $2.5 million post-money valuation; the 20% the investor buys is measured against the post-money number. Founders who negotiate the pre-money figure and forget the money itself give away more than they agreed to.

Worked example (illustrative — every figure invented to show the method)

Imagine a two-founder Lagos team with a working prototype, no paying users yet, and a waitlist of 300 clinics. Berkus-style, they can score the idea, the prototype and the team, but not strategic relationships or sales — so the method already tells them their number should sit well below any ceiling. Scorecard-style, they find five verifiable local pre-seed rounds and take the median as a baseline, then adjust down for no revenue and up for a team that has sold into clinics before. The venture capital method tests the result: at their proposed number, does the investor's required return demand an exit this market has actually produced? If the three methods land in the same broad range, that range — not a single figure — is what they take into the room.

04 — THE POST-MONEY SAFE TRAP

The cheapest document in the room can be the most expensive.

Most pre-seed rounds in the Y Combinator tradition are done on a SAFE — a Simple Agreement for Future Equity, introduced by Y Combinator in 2013 as a faster, lower-cost alternative to a convertible note. A SAFE carries no interest rate and no maturity date; it is a contractual right to receive equity at a future priced round. Its most important term is the valuation cap: the maximum valuation at which the SAFE converts. If the priced round lands above the cap, the SAFE holder converts at the cap price and receives more shares per dollar than the new investors — compensation for taking the earliest risk.

The trap is in the word “post-money.” Since 2018, Y Combinator's standard SAFE has been a post-money instrument: the cap is measured after counting the SAFE money itself and every other SAFE converting alongside it. The consequence is precise and widely misunderstood. A $100,000 SAFE on a $2 million post-money cap buys 5% of the company, fixed at signing ($100,000 ÷ $2,000,000). Raise three such SAFEs and you have sold 15% before a single priced round closes — and every point of that dilution comes out of the founders, because the SAFE holders' percentages are fixed while the founders' are not. Under the old pre-money form, later SAFEs diluted earlier SAFE holders too; under the post-money form, they dilute only you.

That is not a reason to avoid SAFEs — the clarity is genuinely valuable, and the alternative instruments have their own costs. A convertible note carries interest and a maturity date after which the investor can demand repayment; a discount-only SAFE without a cap leaves the early investor's reward undefined. The reason to understand the post-money form is that it turns “just sign another small SAFE” into a cap-table decision. Before every SAFE, add up the percentages already promised on the same basis. If you cannot state your fully diluted ownership after the next two SAFEs, stop signing and do the arithmetic — a spreadsheet that takes ten minutes now replaces a negotiation you will lose later, when the priced-round investors look at a cap table and see founders who no longer control their company.

✳
The stacking test, run before every signature

Write down each outstanding SAFE as a percentage: amount ÷ its cap. Add them. That total is the ownership already sold, before the priced round, before the option pool, before any new money. If the total surprises you, it would have surprised you later at a much higher price.

05 — WHAT A TOO-HIGH NUMBER COSTS YOU

A high valuation is a promise the next round will collect on.

Founders chase high valuations for understandable reasons: less dilution now, a better headline, proof — to themselves and to others — that the company matters. But a pre-seed valuation is not a prize; it is the price at which the next investor must agree you are worth more. Set it above what the next milestone can support, and the costs arrive on a schedule.

First, the bridge. When the seed round cannot clear the pre-seed cap, the company raises a bridge — more money at the same or a lower number, usually with worse terms — to buy time to grow into a valuation it has not yet earned. Bridges are not fatal, but they are expensive in attention: every month spent negotiating survival is a month not spent building the milestone.

Second, the down round. A priced round below the SAFE cap converts the early investors at the round price, not the cap — the cap was a ceiling, not a floor — and the headline fall damages hiring, morale and the next negotiation. Worse, a down round often arrives with protective terms from the new money that reprice everyone else's position.

Third, the signalling tax. A high pre-seed number that the company visibly fails to grow into tells the next investor something the founder cannot unsay: this team prices itself above evidence. Seed investors talk to each other and to the pre-seed investors; a reputation for fantasy pricing is a cost paid in every future room.

The opposite error is real too. A number set far below what the evidence supports sells the company cheaply, leaves the founders demotivated and under-resourced, and — because the option pool and future hires price off the same cap table — can make it harder to hire the very people who would raise the next number. The goal is not the lowest or the highest number. It is the highest number the next milestone can credibly support, with room for the milestone to slip without breaking the story.

◈
The test for any number you are about to accept

Ask: “If we hit the milestone this money buys, but nothing more, can a seed investor look at us and pay more than this number without embarrassment?” If the answer needs a second milestone, a market shift or a miracle, the number is too high. If the answer is “obviously, and we left money on the table,” it may be too low. The right number makes the next round the natural next sentence, not a rescue.

06 — RUN THE CONVERSATION BACKWARDS

Start from the milestone, and let the number follow.

The practical way to run a pre-seed valuation conversation is backwards. First, name the milestone the money buys, in terms a seed investor would recognise: paying customers, a retention figure, a signed channel, a licence, a unit-cost figure — something checkable in twelve to eighteen months. Second, cost it honestly: salaries, the product work, the distribution experiments, and a buffer for the month everything slips. That cost is the raise. Third, decide what percentage of the company that raise should buy, given the risk that remains after the milestone — and let the valuation be the arithmetic that falls out: raise ÷ percentage = post-money valuation. Founders who start from the valuation and work backwards to the raise almost always end up raising too little at too high a price, the worst combination.

Then negotiate the range, not the point. Take the range your methods produced into the room and say which end you will accept and what would move you to the other end — a named investor, a faster close, a milestone commitment, help with the next round. A range signals that you understand this is a negotiation about risk; a single immovable figure signals that you are defending an identity. Investors would rather negotiate with the first founder.

Finally, ask the investor three questions before you accept their number, and listen to the answers as evidence about the partner, not just the price. What return do you need from this cheque, and over what horizon? What milestone would make you lead or follow our seed round? What would make you pass on that round even if we hit the milestone? The answers tell you whether their number is a bet on your company or a bet on a portfolio average — and whether the partner attached to the number is one you want in the room when the next negotiation starts.

Monday-morning checklist — before you name a number

☐ The milestone this money buys, stated so a seed investor could verify it. ☐ The honest cost of that milestone, with a buffer. ☐ The percentage you are willing to sell for that cost, and the post-money valuation it implies. ☐ Every outstanding SAFE restated as a percentage (amount ÷ cap) and totalled. ☐ Three verifiable comparable rounds, written down with their sources — or an honest note that you could not find them. ☐ The seed-round test: if we hit the milestone and nothing more, can the next investor pay more without embarrassment?

THE TAKEAWAY

Before revenue, you are not selling what you built — you are selling the risk you have retired and the milestone this money buys. Price that, in a range, and let the number follow the evidence.

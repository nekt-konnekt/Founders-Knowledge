Your First 100 Customers Won't Come From Your Product

“

Your product can be excellent and still have no customers. You can ship features, improve onboarding, add AI, polish the landing page and post every day. None of that answers the question that pays your bills: how does the right customer move from not knowing you to trusting you enough to pay, use the product and come back?

01 — COUNT THE RIGHT THING

A download is not a customer. A conversation is not a sale.

Before you plan to acquire 100 customers, decide what you will count. Founders often mix up attention, interest, trials, transactions and customers. That makes progress look larger than it is and makes it difficult to see where the business is actually stuck.

For a business-to-business product, count one paying organisation as one customer account. If a school pays for 40 staff accounts, you have one customer organisation and 40 users. For a consumer product, count a unique person who has completed a paid transaction. Track repeat purchases separately; do not count every order as a new customer.

01Evidence of interest

A view, download, signup, demo request, waitlist entry or friendly compliment. Useful signals, but not revenue.

02Evidence of a customer

Payment received, a defined service or product delivered, and a record of whether the customer reached first value.

A free pilot is not a paying customer. A paid pilot can count as a paying account when the money has arrived and the agreed work has been delivered, but track pilot-to-contract conversion separately. An unsigned letter of intent is not a sale. A partnership announcement is not a customer.

✳
Set the counting rule before the campaign.

Write down what counts as a new customer, what counts as activation, and what counts as repeat use. Use the same definitions in every weekly review.

02 — CHOOSE A NARROW GROUP

Do not start with a country, an industry or “everyone with a phone”.

“Small businesses in Nigeria” is not a customer segment. It is a huge collection of businesses with different jobs, budgets, buying habits and reasons to trust a supplier. A useful first segment is small enough that you can identify the buyers and understand how they make decisions.

Start with a group that shares five things:

THE FIRST-CUSTOMER SEGMENT TEST

  • The same job: they are trying to complete a similar task.
  • The same costly problem: the current workaround wastes money, time, stock, access or customer trust.
  • A reachable buyer: you can name the role or person who can approve a purchase.
  • A reason to act now: the problem happens often enough, or has become expensive enough, to matter.
  • A route you can repeat: you can find more people like them without relying on one lucky introduction.

For example, “schools” is broad. “Independent private secondary schools in one city that still reconcile staff attendance manually” is a more testable starting point. You can identify the proprietor, principal, bursar or administrator, observe the current process, and ask what happens when records are disputed. The example is a way to narrow the problem, not a claim that every school has the same need.

Do not choose a segment only because you know one person in it. Choose it because the problem repeats, the buyer can be reached, and the economics can work. If the first five conversations reveal five unrelated problems, your segment is probably still too broad.

03 — FIND THE TRUSTED ROUTE

The best route may already exist around the customer.

A channel is where a message or transaction happens: WhatsApp, Instagram, search, a marketplace, an agent, a shop or a sales visit. A route is the whole journey from a specific customer discovering you to trusting the offer, paying, receiving value and telling someone else.

AChannel thinking

“We should post on Instagram, join groups and run ads.” The activity is named, but the buyer and the next step are not.

BRoute thinking

“Reach school owners through a relevant network, demonstrate one workflow, agree a paid test and earn an introduction after it works.”

Ask where the customer already gathers, who they already trust, where the problem occurs and what proof they need before changing the way they work. The answer might be a supplier, trade association, professional group, local agent, accountant, community organiser, existing software provider, physical market or a well-run online community. It depends on the job you are solving.

There is a useful Nigerian example in the GSMA's 2023 report on e-commerce in Africa. It describes an Asaba business community formed around a WhatsApp group by a local resident. The administrator vetted local traders, connected them with online buyers, provided basic marketing training and organised in-person trade fairs. More than 60 per cent of the participating traders were women. The lesson is not that every founder should create a WhatsApp group. It is that the route combined local trust, access, education and physical interaction instead of relying on posts alone.

The same report found that social commerce was the most widely used e-commerce channel among surveyed MSMEs in all the markets studied except South Africa. It also found that exclusive reliance on social media fell as businesses got larger: 60 per cent of surveyed micro-enterprises relied on social media alone, compared with 33 per cent of medium-sized firms. These are survey findings from 2023, not current market shares or a rule for every business.

A group is not a list to blast. It has an administrator, norms and a reputation. Ask permission, contribute something useful, respect group rules and give people a clear way to opt out. Scraping contacts or sending repeated unsolicited messages can destroy the trust that made the route valuable in the first place.

◈
Map the route before buying reach.

Write down the buyer, the trusted introducer, the place the problem happens, the proof needed, the payment path and who supports the customer after the sale.

04 — BUILD THE PIPELINE

Turn introductions into a process you can inspect.

Early sales often live in the founder's head: a name in a phone, a voice note, a promise to call next week and a spreadsheet that is never updated. That may work for five customers. It becomes difficult to learn from when there are 30 conversations happening at different stages.

Keep one simple record. You do not need a complicated customer relationship management system. A spreadsheet is enough if every prospect has a next step and someone owns it.

THE MINIMUM CUSTOMER PIPELINE

  • Identified: the organisation or person fits your segment; you know why they might need the product.
  • Reached: you have had a two-way conversation, not just sent a message.
  • Qualified: the problem is real and recurring, the current workaround is understood, and the decision-maker or budget path is known.
  • Offered: the customer has received a specific scope, price, timeline and next step.
  • Paid: payment has been received. Record the amount and date.
  • Activated: the customer has completed the first meaningful job with the product.
  • Retained or referred: the customer repeats the behaviour, renews, or introduces another qualified buyer.

For a business sale, map the buying roles. Who uses the product every day? Who feels the problem? Who can champion a change? Who controls the budget? Who can block approval? In a small business, one person may fill every role. In a larger organisation, the user and the person who signs the contract may never be the same person.

For each prospect, record the segment, name, role, route of introduction, problem described, current workaround, economic cost if known, decision-maker, next action, date, expected value and reason won or lost. Do not write “interested” as a stage. Record what the person actually agreed to do and by when.

COPY THIS INTO YOUR WORKSHEET

“[Customer] is trying to [job]. Today they use [workaround]. This causes [cost or risk]. [Decision-maker] will consider [specific offer] if we can prove [success measure] by [date]. The next step is [action] on [date].”

05 — SELL A RESULT

Do not make the customer work out why your features matter.

A feature list describes what you built. A useful offer connects that capability to a job the customer already wants done. “We have an AI-powered dashboard” is not a reason to change a process. “Your team can see which orders are unpaid before the next delivery leaves” is closer to a business outcome.

In the first conversations, ask about recent behaviour rather than hypothetical enthusiasm. “Tell me about the last time this happened.” “What did you do next?” “How long did it take?” “Who had to approve it?” “What did the mistake cost?” “What have you already tried?” These questions reveal whether the problem exists outside the founder's imagination.

Then offer a small, specific next step. It could be a paid pilot, one location, one team, one transaction type or one workflow. State what is included, what is excluded, who must participate, how long it runs, what success means, what it costs and what decision happens at the end. A pilot without a decision date can become unpaid support with a new name. See The Paid Pilot Rule for the commercial boundary.

Do not promise a result you cannot control. If your software depends on a payment provider, network, delivery company or customer data, say so. Agree on what you will measure and what remains outside your control. A customer who understands the limits is more valuable than one who was sold an impossible promise.

Deliver the first useful outcome quickly. Watch the customer use the product. Notice where they hesitate, return to paper, ask for help or stop. The first sale proves willingness to pay; activation proves that the product can deliver the job. They are different tests.

06 — MEASURE WHAT CONVERTS

Count the movement between stages, not the noise around them.

Followers, group members, impressions, downloads and demo requests can help explain how people found you. They do not tell you whether the route is producing customers. Track the movement from one stage to the next, then calculate what it costs in cash and founder time.

Qualified opportunities×Close rate=New paying customers

Here is an illustrative high-touch B2B example, not an industry benchmark. If 25 per cent of qualified opportunities become paying accounts, 100 new accounts require 400 qualified opportunities. If 40 per cent of first conversations qualify, you need about 1,000 meaningful conversations. At ten conversations a week, one founder would need roughly 100 weeks to create those conversations.

The arithmetic is useful because it exposes the workload. If the plan depends on a founder personally having 1,000 conversations, the answer may be to improve qualification and conversion, ask existing customers for introductions, sell to a group at once, work with a trusted reseller or choose a segment where the same buyer can purchase repeatedly. Do not treat the example rates as targets; replace them with your own observed numbers.

Review these measures by route, not only across the whole company:

A WEEKLY ROUTE SCORECARD

  • Prospects identified and reached.
  • Two-way conversations and qualified opportunities.
  • Offers made, payments received and time to close.
  • Customers who reached first value and customers who repeated.
  • Introductions or referrals generated by existing customers.
  • Cash spent, founder hours, delivery cost and gross margin.

Include the founder's time in your acquisition cost. A channel that looks free because the founder spends every evening following up is not free. Compare the cost of acquiring a customer with the gross profit the customer can reasonably produce, not just with the first invoice.

07 — RUN THE 30-DAY SPRINT

Use one month to test a route, not to pretend you have a growth engine.

The first month should answer a narrow question: can you repeatedly reach a specific buyer, prove a real problem, make an offer and deliver value? Do not run five unrelated channels at once. Pick one primary route and one backup route so you can learn what caused the result.

YOUR FIRST-CUSTOMER WORKSHEET

Four weeks. One segment. One route.

  1. Week 1 — Define and list. Choose one narrow segment. Name the buyer and user. Write the costly job. Build a list of 30 relevant prospects and record how each can be reached.
  2. Week 2 — Talk and qualify. Have 10 to 15 meaningful conversations. Ask about recent behaviour and workarounds. Record the buyer, urgency, cost and next step. Do not count a message sent as a conversation.
  3. Week 3 — Make a specific offer. Offer a small paid test to the best-fit prospects. Set scope, price, timeline, success measure and decision date. Deliver the first value with close support.
  4. Week 4 — Review and repeat. Measure each stage, the founder hours and the delivery cost. Ask successful customers for one relevant introduction. Keep the route only if the evidence justifies another cycle.
✓ The Friday decision

Keep the route if the same kind of buyer responds to the same problem and moves toward payment. Change the segment if the problem is weak. Change the offer if people understand the pain but do not buy. Change the route if the right buyers never reach a real conversation.

What changes with the business model?

A high-value B2B product may need direct conversations, site visits, references, procurement and a paid pilot. A low-ticket consumer product may need retailers, agents, marketplaces, community recommendations or a convenient repeat-purchase path. A self-serve software product may use search, content and product-led onboarding, but the first customers still teach you which promise and workflow matter. A regulated product may need a licensed partner or a formal approval path before a sale can happen.

There is no single African distribution playbook. Markets, sectors, languages, payment habits, infrastructure and buying processes differ. The discipline is to observe the route your actual customer uses and design around it, rather than importing a channel because another startup made it look fashionable.

THE TAKEAWAY

Your first 100 customers are not a product milestone. They are evidence that you have built a repeatable route from trust to value to payment.

Evidence & limits

The Nigeria-specific Asaba example and cross-market channel findings below come from the GSMA's 2023 e-commerce study. Its 1,591 interviews were conducted with MSMEs in six African markets between February and April 2023. Local teams recruited businesses in urban and peri-urban areas using online searches, existing contacts and snowball sampling. It is useful evidence about observed routes and seller experience, not a representative census of every African business or a current measure of market share. The pipeline stages, sample calculations and school-software example in this article are editorial frameworks and illustrative assumptions, not measured industry benchmarks.

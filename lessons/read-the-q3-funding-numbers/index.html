LESSON / MONEY & FUNDRAISING

The Money Is Back, But It's Choosier.

Nigerian startups raised $635 million in the first nine months of 2026 — two and a half times last year's haul — but four deals took 69% of it, and the single biggest cheque belongs to a company now leaving Nigeria. Here is how to read the Q3 funding numbers like a founder planning a raise, not a spectator.

In the first nine months of 2026, Nigerian startups raised $635 million — two and a half times the haul in the same period last year, and the country's best nine-month run since 2022. If you are an early founder watching the news, the instinct is to treat that number as weather: the rain has returned, so this is the season to plant your seed round. But funding totals are not weather. They are a report card on someone else's past — and before you schedule your raise around them, you should know whose past it was.

01 — THE HEADLINE AND ITS HOLE

The rebound is real. So is the narrowing.

Start with what the data actually says, from the continent's most-cited funding tracker, Africa: The Big Deal, as covered when its Q3 review landed in early October. African startups raised $2.16 billion between January and September 2026 — a touch below the $2.24 billion raised in the same period of 2025. The composition is what moved: equity funding rose 23% to $1.5 billion, its strongest nine-month reading since 2022 and nearly 70% of the total, while debt fell by a third to $669 million. The third quarter was the engine: $580 million in equity, two and a half times Q3 2025 and the strongest third quarter for equity since 2021.

Now the hole in the headline. By the end of August, only 269 distinct ventures had raised at least $100,000 — down 19% from 332 a year earlier. In August alone, the five largest transactions took 84% of the month's total. Fewer recipients, bigger cheques. That is not a rounding error; it is the market telling you who it currently wants to fund.

✳
Two facts to hold at once

The equity rebound is real — even stripping out the single biggest deal of the quarter, Q3 equity would still have grown roughly 40% year on year, according to the tracker. And it is choosy — fewer companies are getting funded than last year. A founder who reads only the first half plans a raise in a market that no longer quite exists.

02 — THE MOOVE PARADOX

A raise validates the company, not your market.

The sharpest illustration is sitting inside Nigeria's headline number. Moove, the mobility-financing company, raised $250 million in August — a Series C led by Mubadala that valued the company at $2.1 billion, earmarked for its push into autonomous mobility infrastructure. It is the single largest contributor to Nigeria's $635 million nine-month total: roughly 39% of it.

Earlier this month, Moove announced plans to exit Nigeria. As RegTech Africa put it on 22 October: “A large funding round does not necessarily mean that a business will expand or maintain operations in every market where it has a presence.”

Moove is not alone in the concentration. Flutterwave's $100 million, Jumia's $50 million and Yellow Card's $40 million, plus Moove's $250 million, add up to $440 million — about 69% of Nigeria's total. Strip out those four deals and the remaining $195 million was shared across every other venture that raised at least $100,000 in the period.

The mechanism matters more than the anecdote. Funding totals count money raised by companies attributed to a market. They do not tell you how much of it will be spent there, how many local jobs it supports, or whether the raiser plans to stay. When you read a country's number as a country's opportunity, you are mistaking the photograph for the terrain.

03 — TWO TRACKERS, TWO TOTALS

Methodology is the story.

There is a second trap, and it is sneakier: the totals disagree with each other. Africa: The Big Deal says Kenya raised $201 million in the first nine months, down 73% year on year — its weakest showing since 2020. TechCabal Insights counts $2.49 billion continent-wide across 315 disclosed deals, with Kenya at $297 million. Business Radar, via Radarr Africa's 23 October analysis, also lands Kenya at $297 million — but notes much of it arrived as debt and asset-backed deals rather than traditional equity rounds. Disrupt Africa's Q3 count: 58 startups, $582.8 million.

None of these is wrong. They are answering different questions. The Big Deal filters for deals above $100,000 and separates debt from equity cleanly — which is why its debt number collapsed when energy-startup borrowing fell from $585 million to $93 million. TechCabal Insights counts disclosed deals more broadly, debt included. Benin sitting third in one ranking at $345 million is the giveaway: a single outsized deal can put an entire country on the podium.

◈
When two trackers disagree, the disagreement is information

One is counting venture cheques above a floor; the other is counting everything disclosed, loans included. Ask which question each one answers before you borrow its answer for your pitch deck.

04 — FIVE HEADLINE CHECKS

Five questions before you read any funding headline.

Here is the desk's checklist for turning a funding headline into a planning input — the five checks that separate the total from what it means for a raise like yours.

1 · What is the deal-size filter?

A $100,000 floor means your pre-seed round is invisible in this data. The Big Deal's 269 funded ventures are companies already past the earliest stage. If you are raising $150,000, this dataset barely knows you exist — its trend is not your trend.

2 · How much is debt, how much is equity?

They are different capital with different appetites. Debt favours asset-backed models with predictable cash flows — M-Kopa's $30 million debt round in July is the shape of that money. Equity favours venture bets. The split tells you what kind of company investors want right now, which may not be yours.

3 · How much is one deal?

In Nigeria, four deals took 69%. Continent-wide, five August deals took 84% of the month. If more than half the total is five companies, the “hot market” is five companies. Check the top of the deal list before you read the total.

4 · Who raised it — and where are they going?

Run the Moove test on the top deals: does the raiser's operating news match the celebration? A raise validates the company, not your market. Money raised by a company exiting your country is not money available in your country.

5 · What does my stage's row look like?

The count of funded companies is your statistic; the total is theirs. Nigeria had 77 ventures raise at least $100,000 — up 10% and more than any other African country. Fintech recorded 80 funded ventures continent-wide, the most of any sector, even as logistics overtook it by value. Those counts describe a market you can enter. The $2.16 billion describes a market you can admire.

05 — DO THE ARITHMETIC

Do the arithmetic the headline skips.

Try it on Nigeria's numbers, because the arithmetic is the lesson. $635 million total, minus $440 million in the four mega-deals, leaves $195 million for the other roughly 73 ventures that raised at least $100,000. That is about $2.7 million on average — a far more useful reference point for a founder sizing a seed round than the $635 million headline. (This is the desk's arithmetic on the reported figures, shown to demonstrate the method — it is not a tracker statistic.)

The long tail is not nothing, either. Seventy-seven funded ventures is the highest count on the continent and up from 70 last year — breadth improved even as the total concentrated. But keep it in proportion: a handful of companies still define the story, and the average round tells you more about your odds than the total ever will.

Your Monday-morning move: before you set a target for your raise, write three numbers from the latest tracker for your market — the count of ventures funded at your stage, the equity/debt split, and the share taken by the top five deals. Size your round and your timeline to the first two. Treat the third as a warning about whose momentum the headline really describes.

06 — WHERE THE PICTURE BREAKS

Where this picture breaks down.

This data is about three weeks old at publication; the fourth quarter decides the year, and one December mega-deal can redraw every ranking. Tracker methodologies differ by design — cite the tracker, never blend two totals into one. Concentration can coexist with a healthy early market: Nigeria's rising count of funded ventures suggests the long tail is alive even as four deals dominate the total.

And what this piece does not do: tell you whether to raise now, advise on valuation, or predict Q4. Timing a raise is a company decision, informed by your runway, your traction, and your alternatives — funding data is only the weather report. As established above, it is not even reliable weather.

THE TAKEAWAY

Read the deal list, not the total — the total tells you how the ecosystem is photographed; the list tells you whether you are in the picture, and on what terms.

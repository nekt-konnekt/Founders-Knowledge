Build It or Rent It: The Real Cost of Writing Code You Have to Keep

Your software bills went up again this year. Most founders' did. Building your own tool is not the free alternative it looks like — the build week is the cheapest part. Price the keeping, not the building.

Your software bills went up again this year. If you rent your tools — software you pay for by the month, instead of owning — they almost certainly did. Zylo, a company that tracks what organisations spend on software, found that 79 out of every 100 IT leaders saw a price rise when a contract renewed in the past year. Nearly as many, 78 out of 100, were hit with unexpected charges for AI features or for how much they used. And 61 out of 100 cut a project because a software bill they had not planned for arrived. So the temptation is obvious: stop renting, build it yourself. One founder posted in early November that he had cancelled a $600-a-year tool and built his own replacement. It sounds like freedom. It is not free. The mistake is comparing the wrong two numbers — the weeks it takes to build against the monthly price to rent. The build is a one-off cost. The keeping is forever. This lesson prices both properly, so you can decide with the real numbers.

01 — THE WRONG COMPARISON

“Two weeks to build” against “$49 a month” is not a comparison.

Here is how the decision usually happens. A founder looks at a tool that costs $49 a month — about $600 a year — and thinks: my developer could build that in two weeks. Two weeks of work, once, against $600 every year, forever. Building looks like the obvious win. It is not, because the two numbers measure different things. The $49 is a price for a finished, working tool, kept alive by someone else. The two weeks is the price of a first version — unfinished, untested, and already starting to age the day it ships.

Think of it like a house. Building the house is one cost. Keeping the roof fixed, the pipes working and the doors locking is a second cost, and it never stops. Software is the same. Code does not sit still. The services it connects to change. The rules it follows change. The people who use it find new ways to break it. Every week after launch, the tool asks for a little more work — a fix here, an update there, a password reset, a report that stopped working. That work is called maintenance, and it is the real price of building. The build week is the cheapest part of owning code.

Renting has the opposite problem. The monthly price looks small and honest. But it is not fixed. It rises at renewal — the moment your contract rolls over and the vendor can change the price. It grows when you add people, because most tools charge per seat, meaning per user login. It jumps when you use an AI feature or pass a usage limit, because more vendors now charge by how much you use, not just by the month. And it is priced in dollars while you earn in naira, so the exchange rate moves your bill even when the vendor does nothing. A $20-a-month tool is roughly ₦28,000 a month at about ₦1,400 to the dollar. If the naira weakens, your bill rises and you did nothing wrong.

So neither side of the usual comparison is honest. Building is priced as if the work ends at launch. Renting is priced as if the first month's price is the price forever. The rest of this lesson prices both properly.

✳
The mistake

Comparing a one-off build cost with a monthly rent. One ends. The other never does. Compare both over two years, with the keeping included.

02 — WHAT RENTING REALLY COSTS

Five costs hide inside the monthly price.

Renting is not a bad choice. For most standard jobs it is the right one. But go in with your eyes open. Five costs sit behind the monthly number.

1. The subscription itself. The price on the pricing page. This is the only cost most founders count, and it is the smallest part of the story. Count it over two years, not one month: $49 a month is $1,176 over two years before anything changes.

2. The renewal rise. A renewal is when your contract rolls over, usually once a year, and the vendor sets the new price. Zylo's 2026 index found 79% of IT leaders saw a price rise at renewal in the past year. The rises are not small: Microsoft raised its Microsoft 365 Business Basic plan from $6 to $7 per user per month on 1 July 2026, and the new price applied to existing customers at renewal. Atlassian raised Jira Standard from $9.05 to $9.70 per user per month on 13 October 2026. Invoice data from Vertice, a company that processes software spending, put the annual rate of software price rises at 14.3% in September 2026 — though that figure is preliminary and may move. The pattern is the same in every source: the price you sign up at is not the price you keep.

3. The usage charges. More vendors now charge by how much you use, on top of the monthly price — AI features, credits, automation runs, storage. Zylo found 78% of IT leaders were hit with unexpected charges of this kind in the past year. The bill you budget for is not the bill that arrives. Notion added an AI credit system to its Business and Enterprise plans in October and November 2026. Teamwork.com started charging monthly credits for some AI features on 10 November 2026. The feature looks free until the credits run out.

4. The limits that arrive later. Vendors shrink the free and cheap plans. Asana cut its free Personal plan from 10 seats to 2 per project and team in autumn 2026. Basecamp added user and project caps to every plan below its top tier around 20 October 2026. A tool that was free when you started can stop being free when you grow — exactly when switching is hardest.

5. The exit cost. Your data lives in their system: your customers, your history, your records. Leaving means moving all of it, rebuilding the habits your team formed around the tool, and retraining everyone. Some vendors make export easy. Some make it slow. Ask before you sign, not when you want to leave.

None of this makes renting wrong. It makes renting a bill that grows. A founder who budgets for the first month's price will be surprised four times a year. A founder who budgets for the renewal price will not.

03 — WHAT BUILDING REALLY COSTS

Five costs hide inside “we'll just build it”.

Building feels like control. Nobody can raise your price, change your plan, or shut your tool down. That control is real. It is also expensive, in five ways founders rarely count.

1. The build itself. The weeks of work before launch. This is the only cost in the usual comparison, and it is the most honest one. But even here, founders underestimate: the first version is never the finished version. The edge cases — the strange inputs, the failed payments, the report nobody asked for until launch week — arrive after the demo, not before it.

2. The weekly keeping. This is the big one. A tool you own needs a few hours every week, forever: fixing bugs, updating the libraries it depends on, answering the teammate who cannot log in, changing the report when the business changes what it measures. Two hours a week sounds small. Over two years it is 208 hours — five full working weeks. Nobody invoices you for it, which is exactly why it gets missed. Your time has a price even when no invoice arrives.

3. The one-person risk. Small teams usually have one person who truly understands a tool they built. If that person leaves, falls ill, or is pulled onto something urgent, the tool has no owner. The knowledge lives in one head. A rented tool has a whole company behind it; your built tool has one person, and that person is also your developer.

4. The security and rules you now own. A rented tool's vendor handles security updates, backups, and keeping customer data safe. When you build, that job is yours. If customer data leaks from a tool you built, there is no vendor to blame and no vendor to fix it. For anything that touches money or personal data, this cost is not optional.

5. The rebuild. Every tool you build eventually needs to be rebuilt — the foundation ages, the framework it was built on stops being supported, the business outgrows the first design. The rebuild is a second build, usually bigger than the first, and it arrives just when the team is busiest. Renting spreads this cost across all the vendor's customers. Building puts it all on you.

AI coding tools have made the first cost — the build — cheaper. A developer can now produce a first version faster than before. But AI does not do the keeping. It does not answer the teammate who cannot log in, or notice that the report broke, or decide what the tool should do when the business changes. The build week shrinks. The maintenance does not.

04 — THE DECISION FRAMEWORK

The one-page sheet: price both over two years.

Here is the whole decision on one page. Take the real choice in front of you — one tool you rent, or one thing you plan to build — and fill in both columns. Price everything over two years, because two weeks is too short to see the keeping and two years is long enough to see the renewal rises. Price your own time at what you would pay someone else to do the work. Zero is not a price.

Now the worked example. Say you need an invoicing tool. Renting one costs $49 a month. Over two years that is $1,176, plus a renewal rise of about 10% in the second year — call it about $1,235 in total. Building a basic version takes about three weeks, or 120 hours. Then it needs about two hours a week of keeping: fixes, updates, the tax rules changing, the PDF layout breaking. Two hours a week for 104 weeks is 208 hours. Build plus keeping is 328 hours over two years. Price those hours at $25 an hour — a modest rate for a developer — and the build costs $8,200 of time. Price them at $10 an hour and it still costs $3,280. That is nearly three times the rent, and the rent comes with support, security and updates included. For invoicing — a standard job that every business does the same way — renting wins easily.

All the numbers in that example are illustrative: they show how the maths works, not what your tool will cost. Your numbers will differ. The shape of the answer usually does not. Building only wins when the keeping is small or the rent is huge.

Five rules fall out of the sheet. Use them in order.

  • 1. Build when it is the thing customers pay you for. If the tool is your product — the dispatch board your riders live on, the pricing engine your customers depend on — build it. Nobody else will build your advantage for you.
  • 2. Rent when the job is standard. Email, payments, accounting, invoicing, calendars. Every business does these the same way. A vendor doing it for ten thousand companies will do it better and cheaper than you will do it for one.
  • 3. Build when no rented tool fits. If using a rented tool means changing how your business works to fit the tool, the tool is costing you more than its price. Build the thing that fits the business.
  • 4. Rent when you need it working this week. A rented tool works today. A built tool works in three weeks, if the estimate holds. Estimates rarely hold.
  • 5. Run both exit tests. If you rent: can you export all your data in a day? If you build: can someone else keep it alive if you are away for a month? If either answer is no, fix that before you decide.
◈
The rule

If the build only wins because you priced your own time at zero, rent. Your time is the most expensive thing in the company, because there is only one of you.

05 — APPLY IT

Run two real cases through the sheet.

The sheet is only useful if it sorts real situations fast. Here are two cases, run through the same five rules.

Case one: the founder who built his own tool. In early November 2026, a solo founder posted on X that he had cancelled a $600-a-year subscription to a niche tool and built his own replacement. That post was shared widely, and it is easy to read as proof that building always wins. Run it through the sheet and the picture is narrower. The tool was small and simple, it sat at the centre of how he works every day, and $600 a year is a real saving for one person. The keeping is small because the tool is small. The decision was right for him. It does not prove that a team of five should build their own accounting software. Small tool, core workflow, low keeping — that is the build case. This account is reported, not independently verified by this desk.

Case two: a Lagos food-delivery startup. This example is hypothetical, built to show the sheet working. The startup has two needs. First, a dispatch board — the screen that shows where every rider is, which orders are late, and who takes the next delivery. No rented tool matches how their riders actually work across Lagos traffic, and the dispatch board is the heart of the business: if it is slow, food arrives cold and customers leave. Rule 1 and rule 3 both point to build. Second, invoicing for their restaurant partners. Every invoicing tool does the same job, the startup needs it working this week, and the keeping on a home-built invoicing tool would steal hours from the dispatch board every week. Rules 2 and 4 point to rent. Same company, same week, two opposite answers — because the sheet prices each job on its own, not the company as a whole.

Notice what the sheet never asks: which option feels more like a “real” technology company. Building everything feels serious. Renting feels like a shortcut. Neither feeling is evidence. The sheet asks only what it costs to keep, over two years, and whether the thing is your advantage or your plumbing.

06 — WHAT COULD CHANGE THE ANSWER

Five limits on everything above.

First, the big-company numbers are not your numbers. Zylo's index is built mostly from larger organisations — the average one in the study spends $55.7 million a year on software and runs 305 applications. Your team will not see those totals. The percentages are the useful part: most buyers see prices rise at renewal, most get surprised by usage charges. The dollar figures are not yours.

Second, AI coding tools keep getting better. The build week will keep shrinking, and some keeping work — small fixes, routine updates — is getting easier too. But the keeping that matters is judgement: deciding what the tool should do when the business changes, noticing that a report is wrong, answering the teammate who cannot log in. That work does not shrink with better code generation.

Third, the naira moves. Almost every rented tool is priced in dollars. Your revenue is in naira. A tool that costs $49 a month is about ₦68,600 a month at about ₦1,400 to the dollar — and that number moves with the exchange rate, in both directions. A tool you build is priced in your team's hours, which do not move with the dollar. For a Nigerian founder, that is a real point in building's favour, and it belongs on the sheet. But it does not outweigh a bad build decision: cheap in naira is still expensive if nobody can keep the tool alive.

Fourth, a vendor's scale can beat yours. A payments vendor has a fraud team bigger than your whole company. A security vendor patches problems before you hear about them. Renting buys you that scale for $49 a month. Building the same safety yourself would take a team you do not have. Rent can be right even when it is expensive.

Fifth, building your core is right even when it is slow. The sheet can tell you to rent something that is actually your advantage, if you fill it in carelessly. If customers choose you because of how a tool works — your dispatch, your pricing, your matching — that tool is not a cost to minimise. It is the business. Build it, even when the sheet says it costs more. The sheet prices the keeping. It cannot price being different.

07 — THE NEXT STEP

Price one real choice, this week.

  • Pick one tool you pay for, or one thing you plan to build in the next sprint. Just one.
  • Fill in the sheet for it: build hours, keeping hours per week, rent over two years with one renewal rise. Price your hours at what you would pay someone else to do the work.
  • Apply the five rules. Write the decision down in one sentence: build or rent, and why.
  • If you rent: check the renewal date and the export button today. Put the renewal in your calendar a month early, and confirm you can get your data out in a day.
  • If you build: name the second person who can keep it alive. If there is no second person, that is the first job — before the build starts.

Do this for one choice, not for your whole stack. One priced decision teaches the habit. The habit is what saves the money.

THE TAKEAWAY

The build week is the cheapest part. Price the keeping, not the building.

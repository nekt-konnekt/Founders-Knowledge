Price the FX Risk: Selling in Naira, Paying Costs in Dollars

You earn in naira and pay in dollars — for hosting, tools, ads, and remote help. Every move in the exchange rate rewrites your budget, even when nothing in your business changes.

Your customers pay you in naira. Your hosting bill, your design tool, your ad account and your remote contractor all charge in dollars. Nothing in your business changed this month — same customers, same tools, same team. But your costs went up anyway, because the naira moved. That is foreign exchange risk, usually shortened to FX risk: the chance that a move in the exchange rate changes what your business can afford. For a small team selling in naira and paying in dollars, it is not a finance-department problem. It is a budget line that moves on its own, every month. This lesson is about pricing that line: how to map your exposure in one page, and three hedges a small team can actually use.

01 — THE MISMATCH

You sell in one currency and pay in another. That is the whole risk.

Start with the shape of the problem. Most Nigerian startups earn in naira: local customers pay in naira, through a bank transfer, a card, or a payment link. But the tools that run the business mostly charge in dollars: cloud hosting, email and analytics software, app-store fees, online ads, and any contractor or freelancer paid from abroad. So the money comes in one currency and goes out in another — and the rate between them is set by a market you do not control.

When the naira weakens against the dollar, every dollar bill costs more naira. When it strengthens, every dollar bill costs less. Your revenue did not change. Your usage did not change. The bill changed anyway. A founder who does not price this is budgeting with a number that moves. The first step is to stop treating the exchange rate as background news and start treating it as a cost — one you can measure, and partly control.

Put a number on it. At the Central Bank of Nigeria's official rate on 6 November 2026 — ₦1,346.79 to one dollar — a tool that costs $100 a month costs ₦134,679 a month. A team paying $800 a month across hosting, tools and ads pays ₦1,077,432 a month. Those are today's numbers. The question this lesson answers is what happens when the rate moves, and what you can do about it before it does.

The rule

If your costs are in dollars and your income is in naira, the exchange rate is already deciding part of your budget. Measure it before it surprises you.

02 — HOW THE RATE MOVES

Small moves, stacked across every dollar bill.

The naira now floats. In June 2023 the Central Bank of Nigeria merged its separate exchange-rate windows into one market, so the rate moves with supply and demand each trading day — oil earnings, remittances, and how many dollars importers and investors want to buy. The official rate is published by the Central Bank each trading day from trades in the Nigerian Foreign Exchange Market. On 6 November 2026 it was ₦1,346.79 to the dollar. The parallel market — the street rate quoted by currency dealers — was around ₦1,415 to ₦1,428 the same week. The rate your bank actually gives you sits somewhere between the two, and it is worth checking: the official number is a benchmark, not the price on your invoice.

The moves look small until you stack them. In October 2026 the naira fell 1.2% in the month — from ₦1,330.09 at the start to ₦1,346.26 at the end. One percent sounds like nothing. On our $800-a-month example it is about ₦12,929 more every month, for the same tools, with nothing in the business changing. Over the last 30 days the official rate moved between ₦1,332 and ₦1,370 — a band of less than 3% — and the country's external reserves fell for five straight days to $55.1 billion. None of this is a crisis. That is the point: FX risk for a small team is rarely a crash. It is a slow, monthly tax on every dollar bill, and it compounds across a year.

Do the year maths once, so the size is honest. If the naira fell 10% against the dollar and stayed there, that $800-a-month stack would cost about ₦107,743 more each month — roughly ₦1.3 million more over a year. That is a junior hire's salary, or three months of runway for a small team, decided by a market, not by your product. You cannot stop the rate moving. You can decide how much of it reaches your budget.

03 — THE EXPOSURE MAP

Three columns, one page. This is the whole exercise.

An exposure map is a one-page list of what you pay in dollars, what you earn in dollars, and what is left over. The gap between the two is your exposure: the amount of dollars you must buy with naira every month. That gap is the number the exchange rate acts on. Here is how to build it.

  • Column one: every dollar cost. Go through the last month's bank and card statements and list everything billed in dollars: hosting, each software tool, ad spend, app-store fees, any contractor paid in dollars. Write the monthly amount next to each one. Add them up. This is your monthly dollar bill.
  • Column two: every dollar income. List everything you earn in dollars: foreign customers who pay in dollars, any export or remote work, grants or prizes paid in dollars. Add them up. This is your monthly dollar income.
  • Column three: the gap. Subtract income from costs. If the gap is $800, you must find $800 worth of naira every month, at whatever rate that month brings. Convert the gap at today's official rate and write the naira figure down. That figure is now a line in your budget — with a note that it moves.

Two things founders usually discover doing this. First, the dollar stack is bigger than they thought — small subscriptions of $9 and $19 a month add up to hundreds. Second, the gap is the only number that matters. A team earning $700 in dollars and spending $800 has an exposure of $100, not $800. A team earning nothing in dollars and spending $800 has the full $800 exposed. The map tells you which team you are.

04 — THREE HEDGES A SMALL TEAM CAN USE

Close the gap from both sides, and keep a buffer.

A hedge is a step that makes a bad move hurt less. Big companies buy financial contracts to hedge currency risk. A small team cannot — and does not need to. There are three hedges that fit a team of five, and they all work on the exposure map: earn more dollars, hold a buffer of dollars, or cut the dollar bill.

First, earn dollars. If any of your customers are outside Nigeria, price them in dollars and keep that income in dollars until you need naira. A dollar earned is a dollar you do not have to buy. Note the boundary: customers inside Nigeria should pay you in naira — the Central Bank's rules say foreign currency cannot be used to pay for goods and services inside Nigeria. So this hedge only works on foreign customers, and only if you can actually receive dollars. Since May 2026, money sent through international money transfer operators is paid out in naira, not dollars. To receive dollars you need a domiciliary account — a foreign-currency account at a Nigerian bank — and a direct bank transfer into it. Ask your bank what it charges for the account and for incoming transfers before you promise a customer you can take dollars.

Second, hold a dollar buffer. Keep one to three months of your dollar costs sitting in dollars, in that domiciliary account, so a bad week in the rate never forces you to convert at the worst moment. The Central Bank's foreign exchange manual — rewritten in June 2026 — gives ordinary domiciliary account holders unrestricted access to their own funds, and you can open one at your bank with your ID and Bank Verification Number. Two practical warnings: branches do not always have dollar notes on hand, so if you will need cash, ask the branch first; and money sitting in dollars earns nothing and is not funding growth, so size the buffer to your costs, not to your hopes. One to three months of the gap is enough. More than that is speculation, not a buffer.

Third, cut the dollar bill. Audit every subscription on the map. Cancel the tools nobody opened this month. For the ones you keep, check whether paying once a year costs less than paying monthly — an annual payment also fixes the price for twelve months, so the rate cannot touch it until renewal. And look for the naira-priced option where the local tool is good enough: a Nigerian payment or messaging tool billed in naira does not move with the rate at all. This is the only hedge with no downside and no waiting: the savings start the month you cancel.

The line

Earn dollars to shrink the gap. Hold dollars to survive a bad month. Cut dollars to shrink the bill. The three hedges work together — the map tells you which one matters most for you.

05 — WHAT EACH HEDGE COSTS

The same protection, three different prices.

  • Earn dollars. Who pays: the customer, in a currency they may not hold. What it costs you: some foreign customers would rather pay in their own currency or by card, and you need a domiciliary account and a direct transfer to receive the money — transfer operators pay out in naira. What you give up: nothing in cash, but you take on the work of invoicing and receiving in a second currency. When it works: when you already have customers outside Nigeria, or a product that sells abroad.
  • Hold a dollar buffer. Who pays: you, in tied-up cash. What it costs: money sitting in dollars is not hiring, building, or marketing, and your bank will charge for the account and for transfers in and out. What you give up: the use of that cash for anything else. When it works: when your dollar costs are steady and predictable — one to three months of the gap, no more.
  • Cut the dollar bill. Who pays: you, in time and features. What it costs: a cheaper tool may do less, and paying annually takes a large sum now instead of small sums monthly. What you give up: some convenience, sometimes some capability. When it works: always worth the audit. The savings are immediate, certain, and entirely in your control.

Notice what none of these hedges does: none of them predicts the rate. You do not need a view on where the naira is going. You need a smaller gap, a buffer, and a smaller bill — and then the rate can move without deciding your month.

06 — TWO FOUNDERS, ONE BILL

The same $800 stack, handled two ways.

Illustrative examples. Ada sells software to Nigerian shops for ₦50,000 a month per shop. Her hosting, tools and ads cost $800 a month. She never mapped it. When the rate moved from ₦1,330 to ₦1,346 in October, her monthly bill rose by about ₦13,000 and she noticed it only when the bank statement arrived. She pays each bill the week it lands, converting naira at whatever rate that day brings, and she has no idea how much of her runway is FX. When a big annual hosting bill arrives in a bad week, she will feel it all at once.

Bola sells the same software, with the same $800 stack — but she mapped it. She found two foreign customers who now pay $150 a month in dollars, so her gap is $650, not $800. She keeps $1,300 — two months of the gap — in a domiciliary account, so a bad week never forces a conversion. And she cancelled two tools and moved one to annual billing, cutting the stack to $700. Her gap is now $550. The rate still moves. It moves against $550 instead of $800, and she has two months to absorb it. Nothing about her product changed. The budget did.

The difference is not money. It is one afternoon with a spreadsheet, one bank visit, and two conversations with customers. That is the size of this job for a small team.

07 — WHAT COULD CHANGE THE ANSWER

Four limits on everything above.

First, the rate can move in your favour. A stronger naira cuts your dollar costs the same way a weaker one raises them. That is why the buffer is one to three months, not everything you own: if you convert all your cash to dollars and the naira strengthens, you have paid to protect yourself from a fall that never came. Hedge the gap, not the whole company.

Second, the rules change. The Central Bank rewrote its foreign exchange manual in June 2026, and it changed the remittance payout rules in May 2026. Bank practice also varies: branches do not always have dollar notes, charges differ between banks, and what your bank allows today may change. Check with your own bank before you assume any of the account details in this lesson. This lesson explains the decision; your bank's current terms decide the mechanics.

Third, dollar income brings its own rules. Earning in dollars is not a way around tax, export documentation, or the rules on what a domiciliary account can be used for. If your dollar income grows, talk to your accountant about how it is declared and what records to keep. A hedge that creates a compliance problem is not a hedge.

Fourth, this lesson is about costs, not speculation. Buying dollars because you think the naira will fall is trading, and trading is a different job with different risks. Nothing here tells you which way the rate will go, and no small team should bet its runway on a prediction. The hedges above work whether the rate rises or falls — that is what makes them hedges.

08 — THE MONDAY FX CHECK

One page, one hour, one hedge this week.

  • List every cost in dollars from last month's statements — hosting, tools, ads, contractors — one line each, with the monthly amount. Add them up.
  • List every income in dollars. Subtract it from the costs. That gap is your exposure. Convert it at today's official rate and write the naira figure next to your runway.
  • Pick one hedge this week: open the domiciliary account, or cancel one dollar tool, or ask one foreign customer to pay in dollars. Do the one your map says matters most.
  • Put the FX check in your weekly numbers review — the same review this lesson's companion piece describes — and repeat it monthly. The rate moves; the map must move with it.
  • Write the gap on the same page as your runway. If a 10% move in the rate would cost you a hire, say so out loud to the team. A risk nobody can see is a risk nobody manages.

If you do this and the gap is small, you have not wasted the hour. You have proved the rate cannot hurt you — and that proof is worth having in writing when the next budget conversation happens.

THE TAKEAWAY

Your costs are in dollars and your income is in naira, so the rate is already in your budget. Map the gap, hold a buffer of one to three months, and cut the bill — then the rate can move without deciding your month.

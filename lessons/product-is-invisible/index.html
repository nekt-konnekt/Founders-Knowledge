A product can solve a real problem and still fail because the people who need it never discover it, do not trust it, or cannot justify paying for it. Distribution is not a task to postpone until after launch. It is part of the business you are building.

01 — THE PROBLEM

A good product does not automatically find its market.

A founder spends months building a product. The interface is polished, the features work, and early testers say they like it. The launch goes live, a few people sign up, and then growth stalls.

The founder responds by adding features: a better dashboard, more integrations, a redesigned onboarding flow. But the obstacle may not be the product. The business may have no reliable way to reach customers.

Customers already have habits, suppliers, trusted recommendations, and limited attention. Even when your product is better, switching takes effort. People must discover you, understand the offer, believe the claims, assess the risk, and decide that changing is worth it.

✳
Your product competes with doing nothing.

It is not competing only with similar products. It is also competing with customer inertia, existing routines, limited budgets, and other priorities.

Imagine building appointment-booking software for small clinics. The product may be excellent, but clinics may already use phone calls, paper records, WhatsApp, or a receptionist they trust. You are competing with an established routine—not just other software.

02 — WHAT DISTRIBUTION MEANS

Distribution is more than advertising or posting.

Advertising can create awareness. Distribution is the broader system that brings the right customers to your product and helps convert interest into business.

01Trusted introductions

An association, consultant, reseller, or satisfied customer introduces you to people who already trust them.

02Useful content

A developer publishes a clear tutorial that helps teams facing the exact problem their tool solves.

03Direct sales

You contact likely buyers, demonstrate the outcome, and learn what blocks a purchasing decision.

04Built-in access

An integration or workflow puts your product where customers already work.

These routes have different costs, timelines, and trust requirements. A consumer application may spread through sharing; enterprise software may need demonstrations, security reviews, procurement, and implementation support.

↗
Ask a better question.

Instead of “How do we get more views?”, ask: “What repeatable path connects people with this problem to the solution we sell?”

03 — PICK THE FIRST CUSTOMER

Choose a specific customer group before choosing a growth strategy.

“Small businesses” is too broad to guide your first sales effort. A school, factory, church, and retail chain may all need attendance records, but they have different buyers, budgets, workflows, and reasons to act.

A school proprietor might care about staff punctuality and payroll accuracy. A factory manager may prioritise shifts, overtime, and compliance. A church administrator may need volunteer coordination. One product might eventually serve all three—but one vague sales message is unlikely to resonate equally with them.

TURN A VAGUE MARKET INTO A TESTABLE OFFER

Vague: “Attendance software for businesses.”

Sharper: “We help private schools with multiple campuses track staff attendance and lateness without relying on paper registers.”

The sharper offer names a buyer, a recurring problem, and a result. It also suggests where to find prospects: school proprietors, administrators, education consultants, and school-owner networks.

Narrowing the initial market is not necessarily limiting the business. It makes the first sale easier to explain, test, and repeat.

04 — VALIDATE THE ROUTE

Test access and willingness to pay before you build everything.

Asking people whether they like an idea is weak evidence. People can sincerely praise a concept without having the urgency, authority, or budget to buy it. A waitlist signup is not a purchase.

Investigate behaviour instead. Ask potential customers when they last faced the problem, how they handle it now, what it costs them, who approves spending, and whether they have paid for an alternative or tried to solve it.

A SMALL PRE-BUILD EXPERIMENT

  • Talk to 10–15 people in one defined customer group.
  • Identify a recurring problem that creates meaningful cost, risk, or inconvenience.
  • Show a basic prototype or deliver the result manually where practical.
  • Offer a clearly scoped pilot to suitable buyers.
  • Track conversations, serious evaluations, commitments, and actual payments.

These numbers are a starting experiment, not a universal benchmark. If nobody commits, investigate the problem's urgency, the buyer, the offer, trust, price, and implementation requirements before deciding what to change.

A paid pilot is useful evidence, but it does not prove that sales will scale. One person may buy because they know you personally or receive unusually intensive support. Test whether the process works with similar customers under repeatable conditions.

05 — MATCH THE CHANNEL

Choose a route that fits the customer and the product.

POSSIBLE STARTING HYPOTHESES

  • Local business software: direct outreach, demonstrations, referrals, and relevant industry networks. Validate whether you can reach the decision-maker and show a measurable benefit.
  • Developer tools: documentation, technical tutorials, open-source projects, and developer communities. Validate whether people adopt the tool, return to it, and recommend it to their teams.
  • Business software: targeted content, direct sales, and industry partnerships. Validate access to buyers, trust, and purchasing requirements.
  • Consumer applications: search, social content, communities, and referrals. Validate activation, repeat use, and whether customers recommend the product.
  • Schools and associations: institutional relationships, member networks, and pilot programmes. Validate whether one trusted relationship can introduce several qualified buyers.

These are hypotheses, not guarantees. A developer tool can benefit from enterprise sales, and a consumer product can grow through partnerships. Let observed customer behaviour—not fashionable advice—guide the choice.

For African markets, investigate the specifics that change the buying decision: payment preferences, purchasing power, internet reliability, device constraints, local trust networks, and support expectations. These factors vary by country and customer segment; do not assume every African customer behaves the same way.

A strategy built for a venture-backed company selling to large US enterprises may be poorly suited to a bootstrapped founder selling to Nigerian small businesses. Copying a strategy without understanding its conditions is imitation, not strategy.

06 — MEASURE THE JOURNEY

Follow customers from first contact to repeat value.

Website visits, followers, downloads, and registrations can be useful signals, but they are not interchangeable with business progress. A product can attract thousands of visitors and produce almost no paying customers. A smaller product can serve a few dozen customers profitably.

01Reach

How many relevant potential customers did you reach—not just how many people saw an impression?

02Interest

How many engaged seriously by booking a demo, requesting details, or trying the product?

03Conversion

How many became paying customers, and what objections stopped the others?

04Retention and economics

Do customers keep receiving value, and can you acquire and serve them at sustainable cost?

If prospects never respond, examine targeting, access, and messaging. If they attend demos but do not buy, investigate the offer, urgency, trust, price, or buying process. If they buy and leave, study activation and whether the product delivers recurring value. If they stay but cost too much to serve, revisit pricing and operations.

A lost sale is not automatically evidence of a bad product. A successful sale is not automatically evidence of product-market fit. Look for repeated patterns across customers.

07 — A 30-DAY DISTRIBUTION PLAN

Replace vague activity with a bounded experiment.

DAYS 1–7 · CHOOSE THE CUSTOMER

Define one customer group, its buyer, the problem, and the result you promise. Talk to existing and prospective customers. Record how they solve the problem today.

DAYS 8–14 · TEST ONE ROUTE

Choose a plausible channel. Contact prospects, ask for introductions, publish targeted material, or arrange demonstrations. Track qualified responses, not vanity metrics.

DAYS 15–21 · ASK FOR COMMITMENT

Present a concrete offer. Where appropriate, seek a paid pilot or purchase. Record objections, delays, implementation concerns, and reasons prospects decline.

DAYS 22–30 · DIAGNOSE AND DECIDE

Review the evidence. Improve the offer, change the customer segment, reduce buying friction, fix a product weakness, or test another channel. Decide what to repeat based on what you learned.

Thirty days will not prove every business is viable. Some products have long sales cycles or regulatory and procurement requirements. The purpose is to replace assumptions with a clearer picture of what prevents progress.

EVIDENCE & LIMITS

Why networks and trust belong in the distribution plan.

Distribution is not only a media-buying problem. It is also a problem of access and credibility: who can introduce you to a buyer, what evidence makes that buyer comfortable, and whether you can deliver consistently after the introduction.

A 2022 study of 14 informal firms in two enterprise clusters in Ghana found that customer referrals, existing business relationships and walk-in buyers helped some firms reach foreign markets. The authors report that half of the firms in their sample traded in five to seven foreign markets, and more than half sold abroad within three years of starting. This is a small, specific qualitative sample—not a continent-wide benchmark—but it is a useful reminder that relationships and cluster reputation can carry products beyond a founder’s immediate reach.

A separate study published in October 2026 analysed 174 interviews with entrepreneurs and ecosystem actors in Nigeria, Kenya and South Africa. It describes how third-party endorsements, documented execution, contracts and intermediary relationships can help ventures become credible to funders and corporate buyers. Because this is qualitative research, it explains mechanisms rather than proving that one channel will work for every startup.

↗
What founders can take from the evidence

Do not treat “networking” as collecting contacts. Build a route where a relevant person can introduce you to a specific buyer, the buyer can verify your claims, and your product or service can demonstrate value reliably. Trust can open the door; delivery and repeat demand determine whether the door stays open.

Sources consulted 28 October 2026:

  • Boafo, Owusu & Guiderdoni-Jourdain (2022), “Understanding internationalisation of informal African firms through a network perspective,” International Small Business Journal.
  • Sarfo et al. (published 3 October 2026), “Distributed signal production in entrepreneurial ecosystems: evidence from Nigeria, Kenya, and South Africa,” The Journal of Technology Transfer.

THE FOUNDER'S TEST

Before your next feature, answer these questions.

  • Who specifically needs this product?
  • How do those people discover and evaluate solutions?
  • What evidence shows that they will pay?
  • What is the most credible way to reach the next ten customers?
  • Where does the journey from first contact to repeat purchase break?
  • Can the process be repeated without relying entirely on the founder's personal effort?

Modern tools have made it easier for small teams to build capable products. They have not made customer acquisition automatic. When more people can build comparable solutions, the ability to identify a valuable problem, earn trust, reach a specific market, and convert interest into sustainable revenue becomes even more important.

A technically impressive product can lose to a less sophisticated competitor that understands its customers, reaches them effectively, and delivers a clearer commercial outcome. That is not an argument against building well. It is an argument against treating building as the entire business.

YOUR NEXT STEP

Build the path to the customer, too.

Write down who your product is for, where those people already look for help, and the smallest credible experiment you can run this week.

LESSON / FOUNDER DECISIONS

Your First Five Hires Decide Your Burn and Your Culture

A hire is not a headcount decision. It is a burn decision with a culture attached — a monthly cost that repeats whether or not the person delivers, and a set of working habits the next twenty hires will copy. The order you hire in, and what you refuse to hire for yet, decides how long your money lasts and what kind of company it builds.

Ask a founder with a small team what they need and the answer is usually a title: a head of marketing, a CTO, a head of operations. Ask what is actually stuck and the answer is different: nobody is closing deals, the product has not shipped in six weeks, the founder spends every morning answering the same customer questions. Titles describe an organisation you do not have yet. Constraints describe the company you do. Hire for the constraint, in the order the constraints bite — and you will spend less, learn faster, and build a culture made of working habits rather than job descriptions.

01 — THE HIRE IS A BURN DECISION FIRST

Every hire is a monthly cost that repeats, whether or not it delivers.

Most founders price a hire as a salary. The real price is the all-in monthly cost — salary, statutory contributions, tools, management time, and the founder attention the person consumes — multiplied by every month they stay, including the months before they are productive. A hire that takes three months to contribute and leaves at month nine has cost you nine months of burn for roughly six months of output, plus the cost of replacing them. That is why the first five hires matter out of proportion to their number: at a team of seven, one person is a seventh of your capacity, a seventh of your culture, and a meaningful slice of your runway.

The arithmetic is unforgiving, and it is worth doing out loud before any offer. Runway in months equals cash divided by monthly burn. Every permanent hire raises the denominator. The illustrative example below is invented to show the mechanism, not a market salary survey:

Illustrative runway maths (all figures invented)

A team holds ₦60 million in cash and burns ₦3 million a month — twenty months of runway. It hires one person at an all-in ₦600,000 a month. Burn rises to ₦3.6 million and runway falls to about sixteen and a half months. One hire, doing nothing wrong, has cost the company more than three months of life. If that hire also turns out to be wrong — three months to discover it, three more to replace them — the cost is closer to six months. The question before every offer is therefore not “can we afford this salary?” but “is removing this constraint worth three to six months of runway?” Sometimes the answer is yes. Often, said honestly, it is not yet.

The timing matters more now than it did two years ago. Funding in Africa is real but concentrated: as this desk's Q3 reading showed, Nigeria raised $635 million in the first nine months of 2026, yet four deals took about 69% of it, and the count of ventures raising at least $100,000 fell across the continent. A market that funds fewer, bigger winners does not forgive a team that spends its runway on an org chart. The founders who survive it are the ones whose burn buys evidence — a shipped product, a paying customer, a repeatable sale — rather than headcount that looks like progress in a pitch deck.

02 — ORDER BEATS HEADCOUNT

Hire the missing capability, not the next rung of a ladder.

There is no universal first-five list, and any article that offers one without asking what the founders already do is selling a template. The order depends on the gap. A technical founder who can build but cannot sell has a different first hire from a non-technical founder who can sell but cannot build, and both differ from a marketplace founder whose problem is coordinating two sides of a market from day one. The practitioner guides agree on the shape even where they differ on the titles: Felicis's framework for a founder's first twenty hires starts with individual contributors in engineering and product, then early sales, marketing and customer success, with operations as the team grows — and warns against hiring managers before there is a team to manage. A founder's guide to the first ten makes the same point more bluntly: the wrong order is hiring the leadership team before the individual contributors.

The reason is simple. A manager with no team is an overhead layer: they need people to manage, meetings to run, and a function to justify, all before the function has a job to do. An individual contributor with a clear constraint removes a bottleneck on day one. At ten people the founders are still close enough to the work that a management layer slows decisions rather than speeding them. The exception proves the rule: where the founders genuinely cannot evaluate a function — a regulated payments role, say, or a specialist engineering hire — the right first hire is an experienced individual contributor with the judgement to build the function later, not a head of department with nobody to head.

Three common sequences show how the gap decides the order:

A technical founding team building a product nobody has bought yet usually needs a seller and a storyteller first: someone who can run customer conversations end to end, and someone who can turn what those conversations teach into positioning, a waitlist, and a narrative an investor or partner can repeat. Engineering depth can wait until there is something to scale; distribution cannot, because without it there is nothing to scale.

A non-technical founding team with customers waiting needs a builder first — one senior engineer who ships alongside the founders, not a CTO hired to hire engineers. The test of this hire is not a title but a shipped artefact: a working product in the hands of real users within the first quarter. If the builder cannot ship without a team beneath them, they are the wrong builder for this stage.

A marketplace or operations-heavy business needs its operator earlier than either of the above: the person who absorbs the coordination work — supply, fulfilment, customer issues, vendor wrangling — before it swallows the founders whole. Operations hires look unglamorous next to engineers and sellers, and they are the reason the other two can do their jobs at all.

In each case the discipline is the same: name the constraint that is actually blocking the next milestone, hire one person against it, and let the next constraint reveal itself. Hiring three people at once because the money arrived is how a team ends up with a marketing lead, a sales lead and an operations lead, and nobody who can close, ship, or deliver.

03 — FIVE FUNCTIONS, NOT FIVE TITLES

Think in functions. Titles come later, when the function has a team.

Strip away the titles and an early team needs five functions covered, by founders or hires, in some order. The table is a diagnostic: find the function nobody owns, and you have found your next hire. Find a function two people both claim, and you have found your next argument.

Build — turns the idea into a product users can touch. First proof: a shipped artefact in real users' hands. You hired too early if the hire needs a team, a roadmap and a budget before shipping anything.

Sell — turns conversations into paying customers. First proof: a repeatable sale the founder did not personally close. You hired too early if there is no product to sell, or the founder has not sold it first.

Tell — turns what you learn into a story others repeat. First proof: a waitlist, a narrative, a partner who can pitch you back. You hired too early if the story is ahead of any product truth it can point to.

Run — turns chaos into repeatable operations. First proof: founders get their mornings back. You hired too early if there is no volume yet for a process to organise.

Challenge — tests the founders' thinking before the market does. First proof: a decision changed by dissent, not by hierarchy. You hired too early if the challenger has no team to challenge through — use an adviser instead.

Notice what the table leaves out: human resources, finance, and strategy as standalone early hires. At five people those are founder jobs, or fractional jobs (section 05). A finance lead before there is revenue to manage, or an HR lead before there is a team to develop, is a salary spent on a function that has not arrived yet. The same logic applies to seniority: hire the person who can do the work, not the person who has managed people doing the work. Early teams need hands, not layers.

The fifth function — challenge — deserves a note because founders resist it most. A team of people who all agree with the founder is not a team; it is an audience. The challenger is not a contrarian hired to argue, but someone with enough standing and evidence to change a decision: a senior hire, a co-founder-shaped contributor, or, at the earliest stage, a formal adviser with a real mandate to dissent. What matters is that dissent has a channel before the market provides one, because the market's version of feedback is a failed round or a churned customer.

04 — WHAT A WRONG HIRE COSTS

A wrong hire is not one salary. It is runway, morale, and a slot occupied.

Founders routinely underprice a bad hire because they count only the salary. The fuller accounting includes recruiting time, onboarding, the work that did not get done, the rework, the replacement search, and the months in which the rest of the team quietly absorbed the slack. The US Department of Labor's long-cited estimate puts the floor at 30% of the hire's first-year earnings — a figure widely repeated in hiring guides, and one to treat with care: it is a US estimate for a different labour market, not a Nigerian benchmark, and it counts direct losses only. Fuller accountings that add lost output and replacement costs run far higher. The exact percentage matters less than the shape of the cost: it accrues monthly, it compounds the longer you wait to act, and it is paid in the one currency a startup cannot print — time.

Three costs deserve more weight than founders usually give them. First, the occupied slot. A wrong hire does not just fail to deliver; they block the right hire from being made. Every month spent managing around them is a month the constraint in section 02 stays unremoved. Second, the morale tax. Nothing frustrates strong early employees faster than carrying a teammate who does not deliver — and in a team of seven, everyone can see it. Third, the founder's attention. Managing a problem hire is the most expensive work a founder does: it is senior time spent on a person instead of on customers, product, or the next round, and it rarely appears in any burn calculation.

The honest pattern, repeated across founder guides, is that most founders know within ninety days and act at month nine. The gap between knowing and acting is where most of the cost lives. A ninety-day review with a pre-agreed standard — the outcomes in section 06, written down before the hire starts — turns a painful personal conversation into a check against a standard both sides saw in advance. If the standard is not met, the kindest and cheapest move is an early, honest exit, not six more months of hope.

◈
The test for any hire you are keeping out of loyalty

Ask: “If this person resigned tomorrow, would I fight to keep them, or feel relief?” If the answer is relief, you already know. The remaining question is not whether to act but how quickly — and every month of delay is burn spent on a seat that could hold someone who moves the company forward.

05 — EMPLOYEE, CONTRACTOR OR FRACTIONAL?

Not every seat needs a full-time employee. Some seats should never be one.

The most common early hiring mistake after hiring too early is hiring the wrong shape: a full-time employee for work that is a defined project, a contractor for work that is the core of the business, or a senior generalist for a judgement call that needs ten hours a month. Three shapes cover most early needs, and the choice between them is a decision about control, continuity, and cost — not a loophole to shop for the cheapest label.

Employee fits work that is core, ongoing, and integrated: the builder building the product, the seller owning the pipeline. The founder sets the hours and the method, provides the tools, and the person works for this company. That control brings payroll duties — tax withholding, pension contributions, and the statutory entitlements that come with employment — and it brings continuity: the person learns the business deeply and stays for the next problem, not just this one.

Contractor fits defined, bounded work: a brand sprint, a specific integration, a launch campaign. The contractor controls how the work is done, works for multiple clients, invoices per project, and bears their own commercial risk. In Nigerian law the distinction turns on substance, not labels: a “contract of service” (employment, where the employer controls how, when and where work is done) versus a “contract for services” (an independent contractor engaged for a result). Nigerian courts have consistently looked past the label to how the relationship actually operates — who controls the time and the method, whether the person is integrated into daily operations, whether they can send a substitute. A long-running “contractor” who works only for you, on your hours, with your tools, on your premises, may be treated as an employee, with taxes and contributions payable in arrears. If you set someone's hours, tell them how to do the work, and expect exclusivity, treat them as an employee until a qualified adviser tells you otherwise.

Fractional fits senior judgement in small doses: a part-time finance lead who closes the month and challenges the burn, a fractional head of people who designs the first compensation bands, a legal adviser on retainer. Fractional hires buy experience a startup could never afford full-time, for the ten or twenty hours a month the work actually needs. They are the right answer to the “challenge” function in section 03 at the earliest stage, and to any specialist need that recurs monthly but never fills a week.

A practical rule for choosing: match the shape to the work's shape. Core and ongoing — employee. Defined and bounded — contractor, with a written scope, deliverables, and an end date. Senior judgement in small doses — fractional, with a monthly review point. And revisit the shape at every funding stage or every six months, whichever comes first: the contractor who built your first product version may need to become the employee who owns its second, and the fractional finance lead may need to become full-time when revenue makes the numbers a daily job. This section is general information, not legal advice — employment classification has real tax and liability consequences, so take local advice before structuring a relationship you expect to last.

06 — THE MONDAY-MORNING TEST

Before you open a role, write the paragraph that would make it real.

Here is the test, and it takes twenty minutes. Write one paragraph: the outcome this person owns in ninety days, three to five responsibilities, and the two or three things you will measure. If the founders cannot agree on that paragraph, the role is not ready — and interviews will produce conflicting signals from every interviewer, because each one is hiring for a different job. A role description built from outcomes, not buzzwords, is also the fairest thing you can hand a candidate: it tells them what success looks like before they stake their next year on it.

Then run the checks that de-risk the decision before it becomes a salary:

  • Paid trial work first. A short, real assignment — a week of actual work, paid at a fair rate — reveals more than four interview rounds. It shows how the person communicates under pressure, how they handle ambiguity, and whether they ship. Candidates who will not do real work are telling you something; candidates who do it badly are telling you more.
  • Hire for the constraint, interview for the proof. Ask for evidence of the specific constraint: the seller who closed a comparable sale, the builder who shipped a comparable product, the operator who ran a comparable process. General brilliance is not evidence for a specific seat.
  • Check the reference that matters. One honest conversation with a former manager — “would you hire them again for this exact job?” — beats three generic references. Ask what the person was like at month three, not at their best.
  • Decide the review date before the start date. Ninety days, against the paragraph you wrote. Both sides know the standard, both sides know the date. This is kindness disguised as process.

Finally, ask the question founders skip: should this be a hire at all? Some constraints dissolve without a salary — a tool that automates the work, a partner who already owns the channel, a contractor for a six-week project, a founder habit that needs changing rather than a hire that papers over it. A hire is the most expensive way to remove a constraint, and the hardest to reverse. Use it where the constraint is core, ongoing, and worth three to six months of runway — and nowhere else.

✳
Your Monday-morning exercise

List every recurring task your founding team does that is not building, selling, or learning from customers. Circle the one task that, if removed, would free the most founder time for the next milestone. That task — and only that task — is your next hire's job description. If nothing on the list is worth three months of runway, your next hire is not a hire: it is a tool, a contractor, or a decision to stop doing that task.

THE TAKEAWAY

Hire one person against one named constraint, in the order the constraints bite — and let the org chart arrive later, when the functions have teams to organise. Every hire should be worth the runway it consumes.

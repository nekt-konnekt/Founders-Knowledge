LESSON / MONEY

How to Build Revenue-First When Your Market Stops Getting Funded

When investors go quiet, customers are still there. A funding drought changes what money is available — it does not remove your market. Build for paying customers first, and let the next cheque be a bonus, not a lifeline.

“

In the first nine months of 2026, startups in Senegal raised $5 million. In the same nine months of 2025, they raised $153 million. If you are building in Senegal, that headline can feel like a verdict: the money has left, so the opportunity must have left with it. It has not. A funding total is not a measure of how many customers exist. It is a measure of how many investors wrote cheques, and how big those cheques were. This lesson shows how to read that number honestly — and how to build a company that survives it.

01 — READ THE NUMBER BEFORE YOU FEAR IT

A market total is a handful of deals, added up. In a small market, one deal can be the whole total.

Start with what the number is. Venture funding is money investors put into young companies in exchange for a share of the company. A tracker is a service that counts those deals — Africa: The Big Deal is the tracker behind the figures in this lesson. Each tracker counts differently: some count only deals above a size, some count debt as well as shares, some count a deal in a different month. So a total is one tracker's method, not the market itself. That matters most exactly where the total looks most dramatic.

Senegal's fall is real, and it is also a small-market story. In the same nine months of 2026, Tanzania raised $53 million — but $50 million of that was one debt raise by NALA, a money-transfer company. That is 94% of the country's total in a single deal. Côte d'Ivoire raised $48 million, and $45 million of it was one raise by GoCab. Again, about 94% in a single deal. When one deal is nearly the whole total, the total does not describe a whole market. It describes whether one or two big deals happened.

The same concentration shows up at the top. Nigeria raised $635 million in the first nine months of 2026, up from $247 million in the same period of 2025 — but two very large rounds did most of the work: Moove's $250 million Series C and Flutterwave's $100 million investment from Ripple. Kenya went the other way, from $744.4 million to $201 million, a 73% fall that reporting links to a drop in energy deals, which had driven Kenya's totals in recent years. South Africa fell 58%, from $433.3 million to $182 million. And the four biggest markets — Nigeria, Egypt, Kenya and South Africa, known as the Big Four — still took 85% of all the money counted, up from 83% the year before.

Read that pattern as a founder, not as a spectator. First, money is concentrating: fewer companies are getting funded, and the ones that are tend to be bigger. Second, a country's total can rise or fall on one sector or one deal. Third, outside the Big Four, the investor route is thin in a normal year — so a "collapse" there often means a thin route went quiet, not that customers vanished. None of this makes fundraising easy. All of it changes what you should do about it.

✳
The rule

Before you react to a funding total, ask what made it up. If one deal is most of the total, you are not looking at a market. You are looking at a deal.

02 — WHAT A DROUGHT CHANGES, AND WHAT IT DOES NOT

Three things change when investors go quiet. Your customers' problems are not one of them.

First, fewer first meetings. When money is tight, investors choose fewer companies to talk to seriously, and they choose bigger, safer-looking ones. The count of African ventures raising at least $100,000 fell from 332 to 269 in the first nine months of 2026. That is the funnel narrowing at the top: your pitch deck now competes against more companies for fewer meetings.

Second, more proof is demanded before money moves. The funds still writing early cheques want to see paying customers first. Madica, a pre-seed programme for underrepresented African founders, pairs cheques of up to $200,000 with an 18-month programme of mentorship and coaching — and on 2 November 2026 its backer Flourish Ventures added $6 million so it can back 25 to 30 more companies over three years. Flourish itself notes that pre-seed deals in Africa have declined since 2021. The message is consistent: early money now follows evidence, not just an idea.

Third, raises take longer, so your runway must stretch further. Runway is how many months your company can keep going on the cash it has, before it must earn more or raise more. If a raise that took three months now takes nine, a company with six months of runway does not have a fundraising plan. It has a countdown. Every month of delay is a month you must pay for from sales, savings, or spending cuts.

What does not change is the customer. A pharmacy in Dakar still needs to manage stock. A farmer still needs a buyer. A small shop still needs to get paid. A funding drought changes which investors are available, on what terms, and how fast. It does not delete the problem your product solves, and it does not delete the people willing to pay for a solution. That is the whole case for building revenue-first: it is built on the thing that did not change.

◈
The line

A cheque buys time. A customer buys the business. When cheques are scarce, buy the business first.

03 — REVENUE-FIRST, IN PLAIN TERMS

Revenue-first means your customers pay your way before investors do. It is a sequence, not a slogan.

Revenue-first means building the company so that money from customers — not money from investors — covers the costs of staying alive, before you try to grow fast. It does not mean refusing investment forever. It means investment becomes a choice you make from strength, to grow something that already works, rather than a deadline you must meet to survive. Five moves make it real.

  • 1. Sell something small and paid, now. Not a free pilot with a promise, but a small paid version: one feature, one customer group, one price. A paid pilot — a short, paid trial with a real customer — tells you whether anyone will pay, and it pays for the next step. Free users tell you your product is interesting. Paying customers tell you it is worth money.
  • 2. Price to cover your real costs. Add up what it actually costs to serve one customer: tools, hosting, payment fees, support time, and the cost of money itself if you pay suppliers in dollars and earn in local currency. A price that only works if a future investor covers the gap is not a price. It is a hope.
  • 3. Tie spending to cash in, week by week. Keep a simple rolling 13-week sheet: money expected in, money going out, for each of the next 13 weeks, updated every Monday. When the sheet shows a gap, you see it 13 weeks early — while you can still fix it — instead of discovering it on the day the bank balance hits zero.
  • 4. Choose customers who can pay quickly. In a drought, a customer who pays in 30 days beats a customer who might pay in six months. Businesses with a budget and an urgent problem usually pay faster than consumers trying a novelty. Ask, for each customer group: who feels the pain this month, and who holds the money to fix it?
  • 5. Keep one non-investor money route open. Once you have real sales, other money becomes possible that was not possible before. A lender can fund working capital — the money tied up between paying suppliers and getting paid by buyers — because there are sales to repay it from. On 2 November 2026, Nigerian agritech company Pullus Africa secured its first debt financing — a loan, not a share sale — to fund poultry purchases and working capital, after building a business that buys from farmers and sells on. The amount and lender were not disclosed, so treat the size as unknown — but the shape is the lesson: debt follows revenue. Prizes and grants can top up the same way: Ecobank's InnovateX student hackathon paid out ₦22 million in prizes to 20 finalist teams, as reported on 2 November 2026. Prize money is real, but it is a top-up, not a business model.

Notice what revenue-first is not. It is not growing slowly on principle, and it is not a virtue signal about bootstrapping. It is a response to a specific condition: when investor money is scarce, slow, or demanding proof you do not yet have, the cheapest proof is a paying customer. Build the proof first, and the investor conversation — if you still want it — gets easier, not harder.

04 — PUT A NUMBER ON IT

Two small calculations: one about your market, one about your company.

First, the market calculation. Take Tanzania's $53 million and subtract NALA's $50 million debt raise. What is left for every other Tanzanian startup combined is $3 million. Do the same for Côte d'Ivoire: $48 million minus GoCab's $45 million leaves $3 million. The lesson is not that Tanzania or Côte d'Ivoire are bad markets. It is that a headline total can hide how little money reached anyone else. If your plan assumes "the market raised $53 million, so there must be money for me," you have read the headline, not the market. Always ask: minus the biggest deal, what is left?

Second, the company calculation. Illustrative example. Amina runs a small software service in Dakar that helps pharmacies track stock. She charges 20 pharmacies $40 a month each. That is $800 a month. Her costs — hosting, tools, and part-time support — are $500 a month. She keeps $300 a month. That $300 does not pay a salary, and it does not replace a funding round. But it proves three things a pitch deck cannot: pharmacies will pay, the price covers the cost, and each new pharmacy adds money rather than burning it. Her next job is not to raise money to hire five people. It is to find the next 20 pharmacies, at the same price, until the monthly surplus pays for one hire. Then the next hire. That is revenue-first as arithmetic: grow the surplus, then spend it.

Amina is invented, and her numbers are illustrative — but the decision rule is the point: if each new customer adds more money than they cost, you have a company that can grow without a cheque. If each new customer costs more than they pay, no cheque fixes that — it only delays the day you face it. Work out which one you are before you write another pitch.

05 — THE NO-RAISE PLAN

Before Monday ends, write the plan that assumes no investor comes.

Write the answers down. A plan that only exists in your head will not survive the week.

  • Name your next 10 paying customers — real names, real businesses, not a market segment. If you cannot name 10, finding them is this week's work, and it outranks everything else.
  • Write the price each one pays, and the cash that arrives in the next 90 days if half of them say yes. That number is your real runway extension.
  • List one cost you can cut this week that only made sense if a raise was coming — a hire, a tool, an office, a feature. Cut it, or set the date it gets cut.
  • Pick the one thing you will stop doing: the free tier, the side feature, the second market. Revenue-first is also about what you stop funding.
  • Set a review date, four weeks out, to check: did the 10 names become paying customers? If yes, name the next 10. If no, find out why — price, product, or the wrong customers — before spending anything else.

If the first answer is blank — you cannot name 10 people who would pay — then your problem is not fundraising. Your problem is that you have not yet found the people your product is for, at a price they accept. That is fixable, and it is fixable without an investor. It starts with conversations, not capital.

THE TAKEAWAY

A market that stops getting funded has not stopped having customers. Build the company customers pay for first — and let fundraising become a choice, not a deadline.

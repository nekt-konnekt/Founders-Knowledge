What a Pre-Seed Programme Actually Buys You

A pre-seed programme offers up to $200,000 — but the money is the smallest part of the deal. Judge a programme by its entry bar, its structure, and what its graduates raise next, before you sign away equity and months.

A founder hears that a programme will invest up to $200,000 in her startup, and the number fills her whole view. It should not. The money is real, but it is the smallest part of what she is buying — and the most expensive part is what she gives back: a share of her company, and months of her working life, spent on the programme's terms. This lesson shows how to judge a pre-seed programme the way a careful buyer judges any deal: by what it actually transfers to you, and what it costs you to get it.

01 — THE CHEQUE IS THE SMALLEST PART

Start with the words, because the words hide the deal.

Pre-seed means the earliest stage at which a founder takes outside money — before a seed round, the first proper priced round. At pre-seed, most founders have a product and a few customers, but no proven business yet. A programme is a structured offer: money plus coaching, a set of milestones, and introductions, delivered over weeks or months to a small group of founders at once. A cheque is simply the investment amount. A follow-on round is the next round a company raises after the programme's investment.

Here is a live example. Madica is a pre-seed programme for African founders, backed by Flourish Ventures, an investor in financial technology. On 27 October 2026, Madica announced a $6 million extension from Flourish, extending its pilot programme by three years to back 25 to 30 more companies, at up to $200,000 each. Since it started in 2022, Madica says it has backed 18 startups across 10 countries and 12 sectors.

Read that announcement the way most founders read it, and one number jumps out: $200,000. Read it the way a buyer reads it, and three other facts matter more. First, the entry bar: Madica looks for founders working full-time on the business, with a working product that already has, ideally, some paying customers, and with little or no money raised from institutions before. Second, the structure: the programme gives priority to founders who will not raise further money during the programme itself — it wants your full attention for its own course. Third, the signal: Madica says its portfolio companies have gone on to raise, on average, about twice the programme's first cheque in follow-on funding. That average is the programme's own report, not an independent result — but it is exactly the kind of claim a founder should learn to read properly, because it is the programme's real sales pitch.

✳
The rule

When a programme announces money, read past the cheque. The entry bar tells you who else is in the room. The structure tells you what your months will look like. The graduates' next round tells you whether the market believes the programme's stamp.

02 — WHAT A PROGRAMME ACTUALLY SELLS

A programme sells four things. Only one of them is money.

First, a filter. A programme that accepts a few dozen founders out of thousands of applicants is selling selection. The bar is the product: when Madica says it wants paying customers and full-time founders, it is telling the next investor that everyone in its group has already passed a test. That is why a low bar should worry you, not please you. A programme anyone can join certifies nothing, and its stamp carries no weight with the next investor.

Second, a structure. A programme gives your early months a shape: weekly milestones, a curriculum, mentors who have seen your problem before, and a deadline to be ready for the next round. For a founder working alone, that shape is often worth more than the money. It forces the conversations — pricing, hiring, unit costs — that are easy to postpone when nobody is watching.

Third, a network. You join a group of founders at your stage, and you borrow their lessons, their introductions, and their warnings. You also join the programme's investor list. That list is only as good as its record: do its investors actually write the next cheque, or do they only attend the final presentation day?

Fourth, a signal. The next investor reads your programme as a short-cut: someone serious already checked this founder. Madica's reported follow-on average — about twice the first cheque — is a signal claim, and it is worth unpacking. An average follow-on of twice the first cheque means the typical graduate raised a next round roughly double the programme's money. It does not mean the programme made money, it does not mean any company exited, and it says nothing about the graduates who raised nothing at all. Averages hide the spread. Ask what share of graduates raised a next round at all — that number, not the average, tells you how often the signal works.

Notice what this means for the cheque itself. $200,000 is a lot of money to a founder with no revenue, and it is a small amount next to what a seed round costs to raise and spend. Its real job is to buy you the months to earn the next round. If the programme's structure and signal do not raise your odds of that next round, the cheque is just an expensive loan against your company's shares — equity, a slice of ownership you give up and never get back.

03 — FOUR TESTS BEFORE YOU SIGN

Score any programme offer on these four tests, before you sign anything.

  • 1. The entry bar — do you clear it honestly? Write down, in one line each, the programme's stated bar and your evidence against it. Paying customers: how many, paying how much, since when? Full-time: are you, really, or is the business still a side project? If you have to stretch the truth to clear the bar, the programme is not for you yet — the stretch will show up in the first month, when mentors ask for the numbers.
  • 2. The cohort value — what do graduates get besides money? Ask two graduates, not the programme's staff: what did the programme change in your business, week by week? If the answers are vague — "the network" with no names, "mentorship" with no decisions changed — the structure is decoration. Good answers name a pricing change, a hire avoided, a customer introduced.
  • 3. The follow-on signal — read the average properly. Ask for three numbers: how many graduates raised a next round, how many did not, and what the typical next round was. A programme that reports only an average is reporting the good half of the story. Also ask how long after the programme the next rounds came — a signal that takes two years to appear is a weaker signal than one that appears in six months.
  • 4. The cost — equity, time, and conditions. Write down the share of your company the programme takes, the months it demands, and every condition attached — including whether you may raise money during the programme. Then ask: would I pay this price for the structure and the signal alone, with no cheque? If the answer is no, you are buying the cheque, not the programme — and a cheque alone can come from an angel investor, a grant, or your own customers, usually with fewer strings.
◈
The line

"Up to $200,000" does not mean $200,000. It means the top of a range. Ask what the typical cheque actually is, and what a founder must show to get the top of it — before you plan your spending around the headline number.

04 — TWO FOUNDERS, ONE PROGRAMME

The same offer is a good deal for one founder and a bad deal for another.

Illustrative examples. Amara runs an invoicing tool in Lagos. She works on it full-time, has 14 small businesses paying monthly, and has raised nothing from any institution. She clears Madica's bar cleanly. For her, the programme's structure is the prize: her bottleneck is not money, it is turning 14 customers into 100, and a programme that forces that work, week by week, with founders at her stage, is worth the equity. The cheque funds the months. The signal helps her next round.

Tunde has a logistics idea and a prototype. He still works a full-time job, has no paying customers, and wants the programme to help him find his first ones. He does not clear the bar, and a programme would not fix his real problem — proof that anyone will pay. For him, the honest next step is cheaper and earlier: ten customer conversations, one paying pilot, then a programme application with evidence. Applying now costs him weeks of application work for a rejection that teaches him nothing his first customer could not teach him faster.

The difference is not ambition. It is timing. A programme multiplies what you already have — customers, full-time focus, a product people pay for. It does not create those things from nothing. Apply when you have something to multiply.

05 — WHAT COULD CHANGE THE ANSWER

Three limits on everything above.

First, the numbers are the programme's own. The $6 million extension, the 25 to 30 companies, the follow-on average of about twice the first cheque — all come from Madica's announcement, as reported by BusinessDay, ITWeb Africa and TechMoran on 27 October 2026, and from Madica's own programme page. No independent audit of the follow-on average exists, and no exit data is claimed. Treat the figures as the programme's case for itself, and check them against graduates before you rely on them.

Second, programmes differ more than their cheques suggest. Some take a larger share for a smaller cheque. Some demand exclusivity. Some are, in practice, a demo day with a cheque attached. The four tests travel across programmes; the answers will not. Never compare two programmes by cheque size alone — compare them by bar, structure, signal and cost, side by side.

Third, a programme is one channel, not the only channel. An angel investor — a wealthy individual who invests early — a grant, or revenue from your own customers can fund the same months, sometimes with fewer conditions. A programme is worth choosing when its structure and signal are what you lack. When what you lack is only money, a simpler cheque is usually the cheaper buy. For the other half of the fundraising question — what your company is actually worth before revenue — read the companion lesson, What Is Your Startup Worth Before Revenue? That lesson is the number; this one is the channel.

06 — THE MONDAY CHECKLIST

Before you apply to any programme, do this in one sitting.

  • Write the programme's entry bar in your own words, and your evidence against each part — customer count, monthly revenue, full-time status.
  • Score the offer on the four tests: bar, cohort value, follow-on signal, cost. If any test scores zero, stop and fix that gap first.
  • Talk to two graduates. Ask what changed week by week, and whether they would take the same deal again.
  • Write down the real cost: the equity share, the months, and every condition — including whether you may raise during the programme.
  • Ask what the typical cheque is, not the maximum. Plan your spending around the typical figure.
  • If you do not clear the bar yet, write the one proof that would clear it — usually a number of paying customers — and a date to reach it. That is your real application plan.

If the four tests pass and the cost is one you would pay for the structure and signal alone, apply with confidence. If they do not, the programme is not rejecting you. It is telling you, early and cheaply, what to build next.

THE TAKEAWAY

Do not buy a cheque. Buy a filter, a structure and a signal — and only pay for them if you clear the bar honestly. The cheque is the smallest part of the deal.

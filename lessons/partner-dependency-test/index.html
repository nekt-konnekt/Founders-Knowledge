The Partner-Dependency Test: Five Questions Before Your Product Depends on Someone Else's Rails

Your product works. Customers pay. Then one morning the partner behind it changes something — a price, a credit rule, a timeline — and your product stops working, even though you changed nothing. That is the moment most founders discover what they actually built. Not a product that uses a partner. A product that lives inside a partner's business. This lesson gives you a five-question test to run on your biggest partner, before that morning arrives.

01 — TWO COMPANIES, ONE LESSON

Two closures in ten days. Two different kinds of dependence.

Klump. Klump was a Nigerian buy-now-pay-later startup — "buy now, pay later" means a shopper takes the product home today and pays for it in parts over weeks or months. It was founded in 2021. Its product, "Pay with Klump", let shoppers split a purchase into instalments at the checkout, with a credit decision in under five minutes. Hundreds of merchants used it, including Jumia and Konga. It raised about $1.2 million.

At first Klump lent its own money. It could not raise the borrowed money — called debt capital — needed to fund a growing loan book, so it changed its model. Banks and licensed lenders would put up the money; Klump would supply the checkout technology. The lenders named in the founders' own account were First Bank, Credit Direct, Stanbic IBTC, Polaris Bank, Wema Bank, Fidelity Bank, RenMoney, Vida by VeendHQ, and Propel.

The new model stalled on partner speed. Connecting Klump's technology to each lender — an integration — took 6 to 18 months, and some took close to three years. The lenders also set their own rules: which sectors they would lend to, how much risk they would take, and how large a loan they would allow. Those rules were outside Klump's control. Early in 2026 the founders chose between raising money again or selling the company. They talked to many financial-technology companies, received one offer, and turned it down. On 25 October 2026 the co-founders, Celestine Omin and Olufunbi Falayi, announced the shutdown. It was voluntary. No regulator, bank, or court forced it.

Baxi. Baxi was the Nigerian agent-banking business of Onafriq, a payments company. Agent banking means local shopkeepers act as small bank branches: they take deposits and pay out cash for people who live far from a bank. On 16 October 2026 Onafriq said it was winding Baxi down. Its reasons: inflation, a weaker naira, rising operating costs, fewer transactions, shrinking margins, and the fast growth of instant transfers — money sent straight from one bank account to another, with no agent in the middle. The business had more than 460,000 agents. Those agents were moved to another principal bank — the bank whose licence the agents operate under — and given 30 days to move their money to a bank of their choice. Onafriq is not leaving Nigeria: it continues its cross-border payments and card services.

The numbers behind the decision were stark. Revenue from the agency business had fallen 56% in a year, to $13.4 million in 2024, according to UK company filings reported by TechCabal.

Keep the two stories separate. Klump died because its partners moved too slowly and set the terms. Baxi died because the market moved — customers needed agents less. One is dependence on a partner's speed and money. The other is dependence on a channel whose economics changed. The test in this lesson covers both, because both start the same way: with a founder who believes the partner's strength is their own.

02 — THE MISTAKE

A partnership feels like distribution. It is not.

A big bank's logo on your website feels like proof. A telecom company's reach feels like your reach. A payment provider's brand on your checkout feels like trust you have earned. None of it is yours.

Distribution is a channel you can change. Dependence is a channel that can change you. When your product cannot work without a partner's money or pipes, the partnership is not a way to reach customers. It is your business model — and the partner's timeline, costs, and decisions become yours.

Two words in this lesson need plain definitions, because founders use them loosely. Rails are the pipes that move money or data: a bank's payment system, a telecom company's network, an app store. You build on rails the way a shop builds on a road it does not own. A balance sheet is whose money is at risk. If your customers' loans are funded by a bank's money, the bank decides who gets a loan, how much, and when — not you. You own the customer experience. The bank owns the economics.

✳
The rule

If your product stops working when your partner changes their mind, you do not have a partnership. You have a landlord.

03 — THREE KINDS OF DEPENDENCE

Name the kind before you price it.

First, balance-sheet dependence: someone else's money funds your product. This was Klump. The lenders' money paid for the loans; Klump's technology only arranged them. When the money came slowly, or with rules Klump could not change, the product could not grow. The founder's job in this case is not to build a better checkout. It is to ask: whose money is this, and what do they want for it?

Second, rails dependence: someone else's pipes carry your product. Baxi's agents operated under a principal bank's licence. A payment product rides on a provider's system; a shop's deliveries ride on a logistics company's network. When the pipes change price or rules, your margins move with them, and you find out in the same month your customers do.

Third, rule dependence: someone else sets the rules and can rewrite them. A regulator can suspend a service overnight. In 2026, regulators suspended MTN Nigeria's Airtime Advance — airtime and data bought on credit — and MTN's financial-services revenue fell 26.6% over nine months, as reported by Techpoint. MTN is one of Africa's biggest companies, and one decision switched off part of its business. Your startup is not bigger than MTN.

Most partnerships mix two or three kinds at once. A lending product usually depends on a partner's money, their licence, and the regulator's rules — all three. That is why the test has five questions and not one: each kind of dependence fails in its own way, and on its own timetable.

04 — THE FIVE-QUESTION TEST

Ask these about your biggest partner. Write the answers down.

Run this on one partner at a time, starting with the one your product depends on most. A good answer is specific and written down. A worrying answer is vague, or "we will sort it out later".

One: Whose money or licence makes your product work? Name the company, and the person you call there. If the answer is "the platform handles that", you have not looked closely enough. A good answer: "First Bank's money, under a written agreement, with a named contact." A worrying answer: "Our partner sorts out the money side."

Two: How long do they take to move? Ask for a real timeline from a founder who has already connected to them — not the sales timeline. Klump waited 6 to 18 months for each lender, and some took nearly three years. A partner that takes a year to connect is a partner that takes a year to fix a problem. If you have never timed them, you do not know the number. Find it out before you need it.

Three: What can they change without asking you? Prices, credit rules, loan limits, terms, fees. Get the list in writing, from the agreement, not from a sales call. If everything is changeable, your price and your product are both rented. If the agreement says they can change the price with 30 days' notice, your margin has a 30-day expiry date.

Four: What happens to your customers if they stop? Klump's farewell said nothing about open instalment plans or outstanding repayments. BusinessDay reported that the announcement gave no details on what would happen to customers still paying. Your customers deserve an answer before the day comes, not after. Write it down now: who tells the customer, who refunds them, who carries the loan, and in what order. If you cannot write it, you do not have an answer.

Five: What is your fallback, and have you tested it? A second lender, a second provider, a manual process, a smaller version of the product that works without them. A fallback you have never tested is a hope, not a plan. Test it once, while you do not need it — move a small share of real volume through it, and time how long the switch takes.

The line

Five questions, one page, before you build. The answers are the price of the partnership — read them before you pay it.

05 — APPLY IT

Run Klump and Baxi through the five questions.

Klump. Question one: the banks' money and their licences. Question two: 6 to 18 months per lender, some close to three years — a fail, in plain terms. Question three: the lenders set the sectors, the risk limits, and the loan sizes. Klump could not change them. Question four: no public answer on open instalment plans. Question five: the fallback was a sale, and the sale never came. The founders had talked to buyers since late 2023, and in 2026 they received one offer and turned it down.

The test would not have saved Klump by itself. The market for standalone buy-now-pay-later narrowed — the founders themselves said the model works best inside a larger financial service, not on its own. But the test would have shown the price of the marketplace model in year one, not year five: every new lender was a year of waiting, and every rule was someone else's to set.

Baxi. Question one: the agents operated under a principal bank's licence. Question two does not quite apply — the channel itself was the product. Question three: the market changed the rules. Instant transfers replaced the cash agent, and no partner decision caused it. Question four: Onafriq answered it. The agents were moved to another principal bank, with 30 days to move their money. Question five: the fallback was the rest of Onafriq's business. Cross-border payments and card services continue, so the company survives the closure of one unit.

Notice the difference. Baxi's parent had an answer for its agents. Klump's customers got silence. That is the standard your customers will judge you by when your partner moves: not whether you saw it coming, but whether you had an answer ready.

Illustrative example. Adaeze runs a three-person startup in Onitsha. Her product tracks deliveries for market traders, and payments run through one provider. She runs the test. The provider can raise its fees with 30 days' notice. Her fallback is a second provider she has never connected to. Her fix is small: she connects the second provider now and moves one in every ten payments through it. The cost is an afternoon of work. The value is that the switch, if she ever needs it, takes a day — not a quarter.

06 — WHAT COULD CHANGE THE ANSWER

Four limits on everything above.

First, dependence is sometimes the right call. You cannot build a bank at pre-seed. Lending, payments, and agent networks exist as services you rent for a reason: building them yourself is slower and far more expensive. The test does not say "never depend". It says "know the price, and decide with your eyes open".

Second, a slow partner can also be a moat. If you survive an 18-month integration, a competitor faces the same wait. The delay is a cost and a barrier at the same time. The question is whether you can afford the wait — in money, in time, and in customers who will not wait with you.

Third, the test is a routine, not a guarantee. A partner can still surprise you. A regulator can still act overnight. What the test buys is time: you see the risk while you can still act, instead of discovering it in the week it bites.

Fourth, your leverage changes as you grow. At a hundred customers you take the partner's terms. At a hundred thousand you negotiate them. Re-run the test every six months. The answers change as you grow, and a partnership that was safe at one size can become the risk at the next.

If you want the companion check — how to tell whether a partner is real, licensed, and permitted to do what it claims — read the desk's earlier lesson on verifying a fintech or investment partner before you trust it. That lesson checks the partner. This one prices the dependence.

07 — THE MONDAY CHECK

Audit your biggest partner this week.

  • Write down your biggest partner's name, and what exactly they supply: money, a licence, rails, or rules.
  • Ask them — or a founder who uses them — how long connecting really took, in months, not in sales promises.
  • List what they can change without asking you, from the agreement itself.
  • Write the customer answer: what happens to your customers if they stop, who tells them, and who carries the cost.
  • Name your fallback, and put a date on testing it. Move a small share of real volume through it before you need it.
  • If you cannot answer any of these, that is your first fix. Not the fallback. The answer.

If you do this and the answers are good, you have not wasted an afternoon. You have priced the most important relationship in your business — and you will know, before the morning it moves, exactly what it costs.

THE TAKEAWAY

Your product is yours only if it survives your partner changing their mind. Run the five questions on your biggest partner this week — and write down the price before you pay it.

AFRICAN BUSINESS INTELLIGENCE / DISTRIBUTION

The distribution gap: how African startups found early customers.

Ten founder-led cases across five markets show how relationships, proof, direct selling and operational fit opened early customer routes. The evidence is useful, uneven and not a continent-wide formula.

◷ 9 minute read✳ Monthly investigationScheduled 30 November 2026

✳

A channel creates access. Trust, proof and delivery create a customer.

“

Your product can solve a real problem and still have no reliable route to buyers. A launch post can create attention; it cannot decide who trusts you enough to pay, what proof they need, or whether you can deliver the promise. In ten founder-led cases from five African markets, early customer access came through different routes: prior client relationships, one-to-one selling, visible product proof, peer referrals and products built around the buyer’s existing routine. The common thread is not one magic channel. It is the work required to make a first transaction feel safe and worth the effort.

01 — THE DISTRIBUTION GAP

Distribution is the route from access to a transaction—not the channel name.

Founders often ask whether they should use WhatsApp, paid ads, influencers, partnerships, field sales or referrals. That question starts too late. A channel is only a way to reach someone. It does not explain why the buyer should trust you, what would make them act, or how you will keep the promise after they pay.

Think about a small retailer considering a new inventory tool. A social post might put the product in front of the owner. The owner may still worry that the records will be wrong, that staff will not use it, or that the founder will disappear after setup. A trusted introduction can get a meeting. A live demonstration using the retailer’s own workflow can reduce the risk. A careful first setup can prove the product works. The route is the whole sequence.

!
Do not copy the channel. Find the trust gap.

Two businesses can use the same channel and get different results because their buyers, risks, proof requirements and delivery obligations are different.

That is why this investigation looks at what founders say they actually did, rather than ranking channels from anecdotes. It asks who opened the door, what made the buyer comfortable, what the product had to do in the real world, and what the available evidence does not tell us.

02 — WHAT WE EXAMINED

Nine public source documents produced ten company cases—not a representative survey.

We selected nine public founder interviews, first-person accounts or reported founder profiles published between 2015 and 2025. One 2018 report covers two separate Rwandan businesses, so the evidence set contains ten company cases. The markets are Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa, Ghana and Rwanda.

The selection is purposive: a case was included when the accessible source described a concrete early route to customers, a customer-access problem, or a product and operating change that shaped early adoption. This is not a random sample of African startups. It over-represents companies whose founders were interviewed publicly and whose stories were available in English.

Most figures and descriptions below are founder-reported or reported by the publication. They are not audited acquisition datasets. Several accounts describe a waitlist, an early experiment, regular customers or later growth—not the first paying customer. We keep those distinctions visible and do not fill missing fields with guesses.

Sample disclosure
10 company cases · 9 public source documents · 5 markets · source dates 2015–2025 · purposive selection · mostly founder-reported evidence.

What we did not calculate
No comparable customer acquisition cost, conversion rate, time-to-first-payment, founder-hours cost or first-100 paying-customer count is disclosed across this sample. We do not estimate them.

These cases are not interchangeable. A payments product selling to merchants, a savings app earning consumer trust, a grocery service coordinating fulfilment and a B2B messaging tool face different buying risks. The point is to inspect the mechanism, not average the stories into a single channel ranking.

04 — WHAT THE ROUTES SHARE

Four mechanisms appear across the cases. None is a universal channel prescription.

1. Existing relationships can open a door that advertising cannot.

Paystack’s prior bank-software work gave its founder context and access to people who understood the problem. Ongair’s existing agency relationship with Chase Bank created a real project that exposed a product use case. Boost’s earlier inventory-data work helped its co-founder understand retailers before building a new ordering workflow.

The lesson is not “sell to your friends”. It is to identify what a relationship transfers: credibility, access to a decision-maker, knowledge of the workflow, or permission to test. A relationship can shorten the search for a buyer, but it does not automatically shorten procurement. Ongair’s founders still described bank and telco sales cycles of up to six months.

2. Direct selling can be a way to learn, not just a way to close.

Yoco’s founder describes one-to-one sales before the team learned how to reach SMEs at scale. The conversations and early usage exposed what mattered to a restaurant: a card machine that worked inside its service flow, plus honest communication when the service went down. Store2door’s founder describes reaching households and businesses directly rather than relying on a self-serve website.

Manual selling is not automatically inefficient. It becomes wasteful when the founder repeats the same explanation without learning what blocks the purchase or what must change in the product.

3. Proof can travel after a customer experiences the promise.

PiggyVest’s founder says successful withdrawals gave early users something concrete to tell friends. Store2door and Park and Pick also describe customer recommendations. In these accounts, referral is not a substitute for value. It follows an experience the customer was comfortable recommending.

A referral reward can help prompt sharing, but the presence of a reward does not tell us the conversion rate or whether the route is profitable. Measure the actual paid action and the cost of serving the referred customer.

4. The product and the operating model may need to fit the route.

Wasoko’s early pilots failed before the company added its own logistics. Boost’s WhatsApp ordering used buttons and images rather than assuming every retailer would learn a conventional app. Chowdeck began with a small operating experiment—few bikes and two restaurants—before expanding its service.

For these businesses, distribution was partly an operations problem. A promise to deliver stock, accept a payment or make ordering easy is only credible if the team can perform the work behind it. The cost of fulfilment belongs in the acquisition decision, not in a separate conversation after the sale.

✳
Trust is sometimes product work.

As a supporting example outside the ten-case sample, TechCrunch reported in 2017 that Nigerian B2B marketplace Releaf manually verified prospective businesses, asked for registration details and references, and called those references. The same report said the company had signed up about 1,000 businesses after public launch; that is a reported signup figure, not 1,000 paying customers. The useful lesson is the verification mechanism, not the signup count.

05 — MAP YOUR OWN ROUTE

Trace the trust path before you choose a channel.

Use these questions to describe how a specific buyer moves from first contact to a second transaction. Do not answer for “the African market”. Choose one buyer segment and one product offer.

Access — who can get you a real conversation?
Where does this buyer already ask for help, compare suppliers or learn about tools? Is there a customer, supplier, accountant, agent, association or existing client who can introduce you?

Risk — what makes the first purchase feel unsafe?
Is the buyer worried about losing money, stock, time, customer data, reputation or control of an existing workflow?

Proof — what will this buyer believe?
Would a reference, working demonstration, sample delivery, successful withdrawal, verified business identity or visible service history reduce the risk?

First paid action — what exactly counts?
Define the event: an invoice paid, a completed paid order, a settled transaction or a paid pilot. A waitlist, registration, demo or free trial is not a paying customer.

Fulfilment — what must happen after payment?
List onboarding, data migration, delivery, inventory, support, reconciliation or other work needed to keep the promise. If the promise depends on the founder doing everything, record that.

Repeat — what would prove the route is worth repeating?
Look for a second order, another paid period, regular use, a referral that becomes a paid customer, or a buyer who can complete the workflow without rescue.

A route is stronger when you can explain who introduces the buyer, what proof earns the first commitment, what work fulfils the promise, and what makes the customer return. If one of those steps is unknown, that is the next thing to test—not a reason to buy more reach.

06 — WHAT THE EVIDENCE CANNOT SAY

These stories do not tell you which channel has the lowest customer acquisition cost.

The sources are historical, dated from 2015 to 2025, and selected because founders publicly described part of their early route. They cover five markets and a small number of business models. They do not represent African startups as a whole, and they do not describe every country, language, buyer or sector.

Most sources do not disclose the cash cost of acquisition, founder hours, number of conversations, paid conversion, time to first payment or retention by channel. The figures that do appear are not measured on the same basis. A waitlist, a customer count, a regular account, a pilot and a paying customer are different things.

We also screened other leads and left them out of the core sample. A Saya interview could not be retrieved in full; a Selar interview described how demand for a creator tool emerged but not a sufficiently specific acquisition route; and the full Kab-Fam interview was paywalled. A 2024 Techpoint story about Scandium describes a LinkedIn post that attracted a bank CTO, but the article is marked “in partnership with” and does not document the contract or payment, so it was not counted as core evidence.

Nothing here proves that WhatsApp, referrals, field sales or partnerships work for every African startup. The evidence supports a narrower conclusion: early routes were shaped by the buyer’s existing relationships, the proof they needed, and the operating work required to deliver the product.

07 — PUT IT TO WORK

Write one route you can test without pretending the unknowns are facts.

Complete this sentence for one buyer segment:

We can reach [specific buyer] through [existing access point]. They will trust us when [observable proof]. They will pay for [specific first result]. We can deliver it by [operational promise]. We will know it is working when [repeat or referral signal].

  1. Choose one buyer segment and one specific job they need done.
  2. Name the person or place that can credibly introduce you. Do not assume a social platform is the access point.
  3. Write down the buyer’s biggest first-purchase risk and the proof that could reduce it.
  4. Define the first paid action. Do not count sign-ups, waitlists or unpaid pilots as revenue.
  5. List the delivery work and founder time required to keep the promise.
  6. Record whether the buyer repeats, refers someone who pays, or stops—and why.

Download the First-Customer Route Audit (CSV). Use one row for each buyer segment and route you test. Put “not disclosed” or “not measured” where you do not have evidence.

For the broader distinction between attention and demand, read Distribution Is Not Posting. For learning from customers you already have, see Your First Ten Customers Are a Research Project, Not a Sales Target.

! Reality check

A route that produces one sale may be a useful start, but it is not yet a repeatable channel. You still need to understand the trust it borrowed, the work it required, and whether the next customer can be reached and served without the same founder intervention.

THE TAKEAWAY

Do not ask which channel worked for another founder. Ask which trust gap your buyer needs closed. Find the access point, show proof the buyer believes, make the first paid action clear, and deliver the promise well enough that a second transaction becomes possible. The channel is only one part of that route.

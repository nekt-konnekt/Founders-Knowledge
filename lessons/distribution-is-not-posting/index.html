Distribution is a path to the right customer—not a checklist of marketing activities.

“

You can post every day, pay an influencer, and run advertisements—and still make no sales. Marketing activity is not the same thing as distribution effectiveness.

01 — THE CONFUSION

Founders mistake the activity for the outcome.

When people talk about distribution, they often use the word as if it means social media networking and posting. For many makers, the plan is to publish consistently, get attention, perhaps pay an influencer, and wait for customers to arrive.

Those actions can be useful tactics. But they are only parts of a larger system: identifying the people who need the product, reaching them in a relevant context, earning enough trust to help them act, and learning whether the route can work repeatedly.

✳
Activity is not evidence of progress

Posting frequency, impressions, likes, and follower counts describe activity or attention. They do not, by themselves, tell you whether qualified people are becoming customers.

02 — ATTENTION ≠ DEMAND

You can earn attention. You cannot force demand.

People do not owe a product a purchase because its founder spent money promoting it. Buying depends on the urgency of the problem, clarity of the offer, trust, timing, alternatives, price, and the effort required to adopt the product.

An influencer may expose a product to thousands of people who are not likely buyers. An advertisement may earn clicks from curious visitors who never intended to purchase. A popular post may spread because it is entertaining rather than because the product solves an urgent problem.

FOLLOW THE CUSTOMER JOURNEY

  • Reach: Did the right people see the message?
  • Interest: Did qualified people want to learn more?
  • Action: Did they sign up, request a demo, try the product, or buy?
  • Value: Did they successfully use it and return—or recommend it?

If people never see the offer, improve access. If they see it but do not respond, investigate relevance and messaging. If they respond but do not buy, examine the offer, trust, price, product experience, and buying friction before buying more traffic.

03 — THE SPENDING PROBLEM

Why do founders eventually stop spending?

Sometimes the money runs out. But often, founders cannot connect what they spent with what the business gained. After several disappointing campaigns, marketing begins to feel like a recurring bill with uncertain returns.

01Financial exhaustion

Limited runway cannot support open-ended experiments with no clear stop conditions.

02Unmeasured results

The founder sees views and clicks but cannot tell which source produced qualified leads or paying customers.

03Broken conversion

People arrive, but onboarding, product value, trust, pricing, or checkout prevents them from moving forward.

04Lost confidence

Repeated weak results lead the founder to conclude that marketing does not work at all.

Stopping every effort may be premature. Continuing to spend without learning is equally risky. The useful decision is whether the last attempt taught you something that changes the next one.

04 — SETTING THE BUDGET

How much should a founder spend?

There is no universal amount or percentage that suits every product, stage, market, or business model. A useful budget depends on available runway, the cost of reaching likely customers, the value of a customer, the time it takes to convert, and how much uncertainty remains.

WHAT THE BUDGET MUST HELP YOU LEARN

  • Acquisition cost: What money and founder time are required to reach and convert a customer?
  • Conversion: What proportion of qualified prospects take the next meaningful step?
  • Customer value: What revenue or validated business value does a customer generate over time?
  • Repeatability: Can the result happen again with similar customers and conditions?

For an early-stage founder, the first goal is usually not to maximise reach. It is to run a small, bounded test that reduces uncertainty without putting essential operating money at risk. Do not borrow a budget percentage from another company without understanding its economics and stage.

↗
Budget for the stage you are in

Before product-market fit, spend to test assumptions and learn. When a channel produces repeatable results and the unit economics make sense, consider increasing spend gradually while checking whether performance holds.

05 — BUY EVIDENCE, NOT EXPOSURE

Give every experiment a question and a limit.

A practical distribution experiment starts with one defined audience, one hypothesis, one route, one desired customer action, and a budget or time limit. Decide in advance what you will measure and what result would justify continuing, changing the approach, or stopping.

Audience·Hypothesis·Test·Evidence
  1. Choose a specific group with a plausible need—not “everyone who uses the internet.”
  2. Choose a route that can realistically reach that group.
  3. Define the next meaningful action: a qualified conversation, trial, demo, purchase, or repeat use.
  4. Set a spending ceiling and a review date before launching.
  5. Record results and investigate where qualified people drop off.
  6. Continue, change, or stop based on what the test revealed—not on vanity metrics alone.

For a bootstrapped founder, this can begin with direct conversations, referrals, a focused community, or a small advertising test. The right route depends on the buyer and the product; paid advertising is neither automatically necessary nor automatically wrong.

PUT IT TO WORK

Write your next distribution experiment.

Complete these five lines before spending:

  1. Audience: Who specifically needs this?
  2. Hypothesis: Why do I believe this route can reach them?
  3. Action: What should a qualified person do next?
  4. Limit: How much money or time can I afford to risk?
  5. Decision rule: What result would make me continue, revise, or stop?
! Reality check

A single sale can be encouraging, but it does not prove a repeatable channel. Consider sample size, buying cycles, margins, retention, and the limits of your evidence.

THE TAKEAWAY

Do not ask only how much to spend. Ask what the spending is meant to teach you. The aim is a repeatable path from the right customer discovering a product to choosing, using, and paying for it.

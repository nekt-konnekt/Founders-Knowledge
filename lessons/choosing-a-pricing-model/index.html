The right model follows how customers receive value—not a trend.

“

Once you accept that pricing is part of product design, the next question is how customers should pay. The answer is not automatically a subscription, a free tier, or a one-time fee. The model should reflect the problem, the buyer, the frequency of value, and the economics of delivering the promise.

01 — HOW CUSTOMERS PAY

The main pricing models

A pricing model describes how a customer is charged. Each model creates different incentives and obligations for the customer and the business.

One-time payment

Customers pay once for a product, version, or deliverable.

Can fit: standalone tools, digital products, and deliverables with limited ongoing service costs.

Watch for: revenue may not recur even when maintenance and support continue.

Subscription

Customers pay monthly or annually for continued access or service.

Can fit: products that deliver recurring value, ongoing updates, or continuous service.

Watch for: customers may cancel if the value does not justify the recurring charge.

Freemium

A useful free tier remains available indefinitely, while customers pay for additional capabilities, capacity, or benefits.

Can fit: products where free access encourages discovery, collaboration, or eventual upgrades.

Watch for: free users may consume resources without enough converting to paid plans.

Free trial

Customers receive temporary access to a paid experience before deciding whether to purchase.

Can fit: products whose value becomes clear through actual use.

Watch for: customers may not reach the key outcome before the trial ends.

Usage-based pricing

Customers pay for measurable consumption, such as API requests, processing time, transactions, or documents handled.

Can fit: products where consumption or cost scales with use.

Watch for: unpredictable bills can make purchasing harder and revenue less predictable.

Tiered pricing

Customers choose among plans with different capabilities, limits, or service levels.

Can fit: products serving customers with meaningfully different needs and budgets.

Watch for: arbitrary restrictions can make the plans confusing or feel unfair.

Per-seat or per-user pricing

Customers pay according to how many people use the product.

Can fit: collaborative software and organizational tools where each additional user brings value.

Watch for: customers may limit adoption to control cost.

Outcome-based or performance-based pricing

Payment depends partly or entirely on an agreed result, milestone, or performance measure.

Can fit: measurable outcomes where the provider can influence and verify results.

Watch for: disagreements over attribution and factors outside the provider's control.

Other structures include licensing, transaction fees, advertising-supported access, pay-as-you-go, and hybrids. Models can be combined when the combination is understandable and aligns with the way value is delivered.

!
Freemium is not a free trial

Freemium preserves a free tier. A free trial gives temporary access to a broader paid experience. They solve different adoption problems.

02 — HOW THE PRICE IS SET

Pricing models are not pricing strategies

A pricing strategy is the approach used to decide the amount charged and how the offer is positioned. It is a separate decision from how payment works.

  • Value-based: estimate what the outcome is worth to the customer, taking alternatives and willingness to pay into account.
  • Cost-plus: calculate delivery costs and add a target margin. Useful for understanding the floor, but it may not reflect the customer's perceived value.
  • Competitor-based: compare prices and offers in the market. Competitors are reference points, not proof that their pricing is right for you.
  • Penetration pricing: use a deliberately low introductory price to encourage adoption, with a clear plan for what happens later.
  • Premium pricing: charge more when differentiation, service, quality, or trust credibly supports the price.
  • Price skimming: start at a higher price for buyers with high willingness to pay, then adjust as the market or offer changes.

These approaches can overlap. A subscription product may use value-based pricing, competitor comparisons, and tiers at the same time.

For a niche product, value-based pricing deserves close attention. The price should reflect the importance of the problem, the value of the outcome, available alternatives, and the buyer's willingness and ability to pay—not simply the number of hours spent building the software.

03 — MATCH THE MODEL TO THE PRODUCT

Choose based on how value is delivered

01Is value recurring?

If customers receive ongoing benefits, a subscription may fit. If the outcome is complete after one purchase, a one-time fee may be more natural.

02Does value scale with use?

If customers consume different amounts of a measurable resource, usage-based pricing may align price with consumption—provided bills remain understandable.

03Do customer needs differ?

If different segments need distinct capabilities or service levels, tiers may work. Keep the differences meaningful rather than inventing complexity.

04Must customers experience it first?

If real use is needed to reduce uncertainty, a trial may help. If the outcome can be demonstrated credibly, a paid pilot or direct purchase may be simpler.

Then test the economics. Include hosting, AI inference, storage, payment fees, onboarding, support, refunds, and ongoing development. A model that customers like but cannot sustainably deliver is not a good fit.

04 — TEST BEFORE SCALING

Run a pricing experiment

  1. Choose one customer segment. Avoid averaging together buyers with very different needs and budgets.
  2. Define the outcome. State what the customer gets, what is included, and what is not.
  3. Choose a model and a defensible starting price. Make the offer easy to understand and explain the billing terms.
  4. Ask for a real commitment. Depending on readiness, sell the product, a pilot, or a clearly described pre-order with responsible delivery and refund terms.
  5. Observe what happens after payment. Track activation, outcome completion, support needs, repeat use, renewal, refunds, and reasons for cancellation.
  6. Change one major variable at a time. If results are weak, investigate price, positioning, trust, buyer, and reach before assuming the whole product is wrong.

Do not rely only on survey answers or compliments. Behavioural evidence is more useful, and actual payment is stronger evidence of willingness to pay than stated intent. Even so, a small experiment cannot tell you everything about market size or long-term retention.

05 — DECISION CHECKLIST

Before you choose a pricing model

  • Who receives the value, and who actually pays?
  • Is the value one-time, recurring, or linked to usage?
  • What alternative is the customer paying to replace?
  • What uncertainty must be removed before purchase?
  • Can the customer predict and understand the cost?
  • Can the business serve customers profitably at this price?
  • What evidence would make us keep, change, or abandon this model?
✳
The takeaway

Let the problem, customer, value, and economics determine how you charge. Do not choose a pricing model simply because it is fashionable or familiar.

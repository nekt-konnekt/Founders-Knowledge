Accelerator Maths: What $50,000 and a Programme Actually Cost You

Three Nigerian startups were picked for a global accelerator. Each one gets $50,000. Tomorrow, 12 November, they pitch to more than 80 investors at the programme's demo day. It reads like a gift: money, mentors, and a room full of investors. It is not a gift. It is a price — written in equity, months, and conditions. This lesson shows you how to work that price out on one page, before you sign anything.

01 — THREE STARTUPS, $50,000 EACH

The cheque is real. The price is in the fine print.

On 21 October 2026, MARL — a United States venture capital firm that runs a global accelerator — announced its fourteenth cohort. Three Nigerian startups were selected: Solarcorp, which builds shared solar-and-storage systems to replace diesel generators in malls and commercial clusters; Bravoo, a consumer platform built around rewards and games; and Zendtrax, software for procurement, inventory and supply-chain management. Each gets $50,000. Together, $150,000.

MARL's own programme page states the standard terms. The programme lasts 12 weeks. The $50,000 comes as a SAFE — a Simple Agreement for Future Equity, which means money now that turns into shares later, when the company raises its first priced round. In exchange, MARL takes 5% of the company. There is also up to $100,000 of follow-on money, on a SAFE with a $2.5 million valuation cap — a cap is the highest value at which that money can turn into shares — but only if the startup hits a revenue target within six months of the cohort starting. MARL does not publish what that revenue target is.

Three things about this deal are not confirmed, and the lesson keeps them that way. The reports on the three Nigerian startups did not confirm whether the standard 5% applies to all three deals — the $50,000 cheque is confirmed, the 5% is the programme's published standard. The programme page does not say whether the cohort is remote or in person, or where. And the revenue target for the follow-on money is unpublished, so a founder cannot price that second cheque in advance. A price you cannot work out is not a price you can agree to blindly.

02 — THE MISTAKE

Founders compare cheques. They should compare costs.

The usual conversation goes like this: one programme offers $50,000, another offers $200,000, a third offers $500,000. The biggest cheque wins. That is the wrong comparison. A programme's price is not the size of its cheque. It is what the programme takes in return — and what it costs you to take it.

Think of it the other way round. A cheque of $50,000 for 5% of your company prices your whole company at $1 million. That is the implied valuation: divide the cheque by the share. $50,000 divided by 5% is $1 million. The same arithmetic on other programmes gives very different numbers. Antler's Kenya programme offers $100,000 for 10% — which also prices the company at $1 million. Y Combinator's $125,000 for a fixed 7% prices it at about $1.79 million. If an angel investor would pay $50,000 for 3% of your company, that angel prices it at about $1.67 million — and the programme taking 5% for the same money is charging you two extra points of your company for the network, the mentors and the demo day. That is the real price of the programme. The question is whether the network is worth those two points.

✳
The rule

Divide the cheque by the share. If the answer is lower than what anyone else would pay for your company, the programme is expensive money — however big the cheque looks.

03 — THE FOUR PRICES

Every programme offer has four prices. Most founders count one.

First, equity — the share of your company you give away. Equity is ownership: a share of the company. The headline number is the floor, never the total. Most big programmes pair a fixed share with a second, uncapped SAFE. Y Combinator's standard deal is $125,000 for a fixed 7%, plus $375,000 on an uncapped SAFE with a Most Favoured Nation clause — MFN means Y Combinator gets the best terms any later SAFE investor gets. Y Combinator's own worked example shows the result: if the next round values the company at $15 million, the $375,000 turns into 2.5% more — so Y Combinator ends up with about 9.5% in total, not 7%. Techstars works the same way: $20,000 for 5% in common stock — the same class of shares founders and employees hold — plus $200,000 on an uncapped MFN SAFE, which adds about 1% more at a $20 million next round. The headline says 5%. The real number is above 6%.

Second, time — the months the programme takes from your company. MARL runs 12 weeks. Y Combinator and Techstars run three months. Antler's Nairobi residency runs 10 weeks, in person. Madica runs 18 months, with no cohorts — it works with each company on its own timetable. MEST in Accra runs seven months of training plus four months of incubation, residential, with flights and housing covered. Ask what stops while the programme runs: sales calls, product work, hiring. Three months of a founder's full attention is a real cost, and it is not on the term sheet.

Third, conditions — the money that is promised but not guaranteed. Follow-on money with conditions attached is not money yet. MARL's extra $100,000 needs a revenue target the programme does not publish, hit within six months. Antler's extra $150,000 is pre-committed to match your next venture-led round, on that round's terms — it only arrives if you raise a priced round at all. Madica's new $6 million extension, announced 27 October 2026, goes entirely into new pre-seed deals — none of it is held back for follow-on rounds, so its founders must plan their next raise with other investors from day one. Count only the money that arrives on acceptance. The rest is a maybe.

Fourth, the hidden costs — the things the programme page does not price. Y Combinator invests only in companies registered in the United States, Canada, Cayman or Singapore. A Nigerian company must flip — create a parent company in one of those countries — to take the deal. That costs legal fees and weeks. Techstars has the same requirement. Antler's Nairobi residency is in person, so a founder outside Kenya pays to relocate for ten weeks. Techstars' side letter also gives it pro rata rights — the right to invest more in later rounds to hold its share — and drag-along rights, which can force a sale of the company in some circumstances. Y Combinator takes pro rata rights too. None of these is a reason to refuse. All of them belong on the cost sheet before you sign.

04 — THE COST SHEET

One page, six lines, before you apply.

Here is the sheet. Fill it in for every offer, from the programme's own page — not from a blog post about it. If a line cannot be filled from the programme's own page, write "not published" and treat that as a finding, not a gap to guess over.

  • 1. The cheque that arrives on acceptance. Cash only. Not the follow-on, not the perks, not the credits.
  • 2. The share it buys, and the total share after the second SAFE converts. Work the headline share, then add what the uncapped SAFE becomes at a realistic next-round value. If the programme does not publish its share — Madica does not, and neither does MEST — write "not published" and ask for it in writing before you apply.
  • 3. The implied valuation. Cheque divided by share. Compare it with what an angel would pay. If the programme's number is lower, the programme is the expensive money.
  • 4. The months, and where you must be. Programme length, in person or remote, and what work stops while it runs.
  • 5. The conditions on the rest of the money. What must happen before the follow-on arrives, and by when. If the condition is unpublished — like MARL's revenue target — the money is worth zero in your plan until you know the number.
  • 6. The hidden costs. Flip, relocation, pro rata and drag-along rights, and any fee. Y Combinator and Techstars charge no programme fee. Others do not all say so — check.

05 — APPLY IT

Run two real offers through the sheet.

MARL, for one of the three Nigerian startups. Line one: $50,000 arrives on acceptance. Line two: 5% — but only if the standard terms apply to these three deals, which the reporting did not confirm. Line three: $50,000 for 5% prices the company at $1 million. Line four: 12 weeks, location unstated. Line five: up to $100,000 more, but only if an unpublished revenue target is hit within six months — worth zero in the plan until the number is known. Line six: no fee is stated, and no flip is stated. The honest verdict: a cheap-looking deal with two blanks that matter — the location and the revenue target. Ask for both in writing before accepting.

Antler Kenya, for a founder in Lagos. Line one: $100,000 arrives — but only after the 10-week residency and an investment-committee decision, not on day one. Line two: 10% for that $100,000. Line three: $100,000 for 10% also prices the company at $1 million — the same valuation as MARL's deal, for twice the money and twice the share. Line four: 10 weeks in person in Nairobi, so add relocation and ten weeks away from your market. Line five: $150,000 more, but only if you raise a venture-led round first. Line six: no fees deducted from the first cheque. The honest verdict: the same $1 million price as MARL, with a longer in-person commitment and a later payday. The extra $50,000 over MARL is not free — it costs five more points of the company and ten weeks in Nairobi.

Illustrative example. Tunde runs a two-person delivery startup in Lagos. An angel has offered $50,000 for 3% of his company. MARL offers $50,000 for 5%. The angel's offer prices the company at about $1.67 million; MARL's prices it at $1 million. The difference is two points of the company — about $33,000 of value at the angel's price. Tunde's question is simple: are MARL's mentors, network and demo day worth $33,000 of his company? If his answer is yes, he takes the programme with his eyes open. If it is no, he takes the angel's money and keeps the two points. Tunde is invented, and the numbers are illustrative — but the question is the point. Price the network, or you are paying for it blind.

The line

Follow-on money with conditions is not money. Count only what arrives on acceptance — and price the rest at zero until you know the number.

06 — WHAT COULD CHANGE THE ANSWER

Four limits on everything above.

First, a programme can be worth more than its price. The sheet prices the cost. It does not price the network, the mentors, the cohort, or the demo day. For a first-time founder with no investor contacts, those can be worth more than the equity they cost. For a founder who already has angels and customers, they may be worth nothing. The sheet tells you the price. Only you can say what the network is worth to you.

Second, nobody publishes the follow-on rate. No African programme the desk could find publishes the share of its alumni who raise again. Programmes publish cheque sizes and demo-day investor counts, not the number that matters most — how many companies get funded next. That absence is a finding. Do not let a demo day with 80 investors stand in for evidence that 80 investors write cheques.

Third, a big raise does not guarantee survival. TechCabal Insights reported on 9 November 2026 that more than 20 African startups shut down or wound down in 2026 — including Twiga Foods, GoLemon, FoodCourt and YoLa Fresh — while October alone brought $540 million of new funding. A separate analysis counted 13 collapsed Kenyan startups that together raised about KSh 93 billion, roughly $720 million. Money in does not mean a company that lasts. A programme's brand is not a guarantee either.

Fourth, the terms move. Antler's follow-on was $100,000 in an older listing and $150,000 in the current one. Y Combinator's standard deal has changed before, and will change again. Read the current offer on the programme's own page, on the day you apply — not a guide written about a past cohort. If the page does not state a term you need, ask for it in writing. A term that is not written down is a term you do not have.

If you want the companion check — how to judge what a programme actually gives you beyond the money — read the desk's earlier lesson on what a pre-seed programme actually buys you. That lesson judges the offer. This one prices it.

07 — THE MONDAY EXERCISE

Price one real offer before you apply.

  • Pick one programme you would actually apply to — or one offer you already hold.
  • Fill in the six lines of the cost sheet from the programme's own page. Write "not published" where the page is silent, and do not guess.
  • Work the implied valuation: cheque divided by share. Write it next to the best price an angel or another investor has offered you, if any.
  • Write down the conditions on the follow-on money, and the date by which each must be met. If a condition is unpublished, price that money at zero.
  • List the hidden costs: flip, relocation, rights you are giving away, and any fee.
  • Then decide, in one sentence: is the network worth the price? If you cannot answer that sentence, you have not priced the offer yet — and you are not ready to sign it.

Two deadlines to know while you do this: Techstars' Spring 2027 applications close on 18 November 2026, and MARL's cohort 15 applications close on 19 March 2027. Check both on the programmes' own pages before you plan around them — deadlines move.

THE TAKEAWAY

A programme's price is equity plus months plus conditions. Work it out on one page before you apply — and if the price is higher than the value, walk away.

A founder in Lagos sells through WhatsApp. Her customers are there, her orders arrive there, and her whole day runs in chat. It feels like her shop. Then the platform changes its price, or closes an account, or changes what it allows — and she learns what she actually owns: very little. This lesson is about that gap. Platforms are the fastest way to reach customers in Africa, and the most dangerous place to keep your whole business. The answer is not to leave them. It is to use them while building a way out, before you need one.

01 — THE SHOP YOU DON'T OWN

Your customers are on the platform. Your business is not.

A platform is a service owned by someone else that connects you to customers: WhatsApp, Instagram, an app store, an agent network, a marketplace. You do not own it, you do not set its rules, and you cannot vote on its price. What you own is your product, your reputation, and — if you have built it — a direct way to reach the people who buy from you.

That distinction is easy to forget, because platforms feel free. WhatsApp costs a small seller nothing to open. An app store gives you a shopfront in every phone. An agent network puts a sales point on every street corner. The cost arrives later, in a form you did not budget for: a new fee, a closed account, a rule change, a channel that shuts down. The platform's business model is the risk. When it needs more revenue, or its strategy changes, your costs or your reach change with it — whether or not your business did anything wrong.

Here is the sharpest recent example. From 1 October 2026, Meta, the company that owns WhatsApp, began charging businesses for service messages sent through the WhatsApp Business Platform — the paid version that bigger businesses connect to their own systems for automated replies, chatbots and customer service at scale. Service messages are the ordinary back-and-forth replies inside a customer-service chat. Those replies had been free since November 2024. They are not free any more.

Two details matter, and both are easy to get wrong. First, the charge hits the Business Platform only — the version used through a provider or a direct connection. A small seller replying from the free WhatsApp Business app on her own phone is not charged for those chats. Second, the price depends on the country and the type of message. Reports put a chargeable utility message — an order update or payment confirmation, for example — to a Nigerian number at about $0.0101, roughly ₦14, and a marketing message at about $0.062, roughly ₦84. A business that sends thousands of messages a month has just gained a new monthly bill, on a channel it treated as free. Nothing about its product changed. The platform's price did.

✳
The rule

Free on a platform means free until the platform decides otherwise. Price every platform channel as if it will one day charge you — because one day, it usually does.

02 — THREE WAYS A PLATFORM MOVES

Platforms move in only three ways. Each one has already happened this year.

First, the platform reprices. WhatsApp's October change is one case. App stores are another, older one. Apple and Google take a cut of sales made inside apps — usually 30% of each sale, or 15% for smaller developers under their small-business programmes. That cut is not a tax you negotiate. It is the rent on the shopfront, and it applies before you pay your own costs. A founder who prices a product without the store's cut has priced for a shop she does not own.

Second, the platform restricts. It can limit who sees you, or whether you exist there at all. Nigerian technology press has reported that WhatsApp Business accounts are restricted or permanently banned in large numbers, often with little warning and no reliable way back — temporary bans for sending too many messages too fast or collecting spam reports, permanent bans for repeated violations or unofficial tools. A ban does not need to be fair to be final. If your only customer list lives inside a banned number, the list goes with it.

Third, the platform retires the channel. On 16 October 2026, Onafriq, the pan-African payments company, announced it was winding down Baxi, its Nigerian agent-banking business. Agent banking means small shops acting as agents for a bank, offering cash deposits, withdrawals and transfers to people nearby. Onafriq said inflation, naira depreciation, rising costs, thinner margins and the rapid growth of instant bank-to-bank transfers had made the agent business unsustainable. Agents were moved to another principal bank — the bank an agent works under — and given 30 days to move their funds to a bank of their choice. Onafriq is not leaving Nigeria; it continues with cross-border payments and card issuing. But for anyone whose business ran on the Baxi network, the channel itself closed. The decision was about the network's economics, not about any single agent's behaviour — which is exactly why no single agent could prevent it.

Notice what these three moves share. None of them is a judgement on your product. A price rise, a ban wave, a channel closure — each is the platform solving its own problem, and your business paying part of the cost. That is the mechanism of platform dependence: your fate is tied to decisions made in rooms you are not in.

◈
The line

Dependence is not about how much you like a platform. It is about what happens to your sales the week it moves — and whether you have anywhere else to go.

03 — CHANNEL OR BUSINESS? THE TEST

Four questions tell you whether a platform is your channel — or your whole business.

Ask these about your single biggest channel. Answer in numbers where you can; a guess with a number beats a feeling without one.

  • 1. What share of your sales comes through it? If one platform carries more than half your revenue, it is not a channel. It is your shop, and someone else holds the keys. Under half is still worth watching; over half is worth fixing first.
  • 2. Can you reach the same customers another way? Do you hold their phone numbers or email addresses yourself, outside the platform? Can you message them without the platform's permission? If the answer is no, you have an audience, not a customer list. An audience can be taken away. A list can be carried.
  • 3. Can you take your data with you? Your order history, your customer notes, your ratings, your content — can you export them in a usable form? If the platform keeps the data and you keep the memories, rebuilding elsewhere starts from zero.
  • 4. What does the platform's own small print allow it to do? Read the terms for your main channel, once, properly: can it change prices, suspend accounts, or close a product line without notice? Most can. Knowing the exact power is better than being surprised by it.

There is a useful counter-example, and it shows the trap is not the platform itself. ChatCash, a startup operating in Rwanda and Zimbabwe, turns WhatsApp into a digital storefront for small merchants — catalogues, orders and payments inside chat. It won a $40,000 third-place prize in the 2026 FINCA Ventures Prize competition, announced 6 October 2026. ChatCash is a real business, and it is also a business built entirely on someone else's rails: if WhatsApp reprices, restricts or changes its rules, ChatCash's merchants feel it twice — once through their own dependence, once through ChatCash's. Building on a platform is a legitimate strategy. Building only on a platform, with no second way to reach your customers, is the trap.

04 — BUILD THE EXIT RAMP EARLY

An exit ramp is not a second platform. It is a way to reach customers that no platform can switch off.

Build it while the main channel is working, not after it breaks. Four pieces, in order of value:

  • First, own the list. Every sale on a platform should end with a direct contact you control: a phone number, an email address, permission to message them outside the platform. Offer a reason — order updates, a discount, a restock alert — and store the list somewhere you control, not only inside the platform's tools.
  • Second, open a second channel before you need it. A simple website or storefront, a second marketplace, a phone line, a physical point of sale — anything that works if the first channel stops. The second channel does not need to match the first channel's volume. It needs to exist, with customers who know about it, so a shutdown is a bad week rather than the end.
  • Third, keep your data portable. Export customer and order records regularly, in a format you can use elsewhere. Test the export: if you had to rebuild on a new channel tomorrow, what would you actually have? If the answer is a spreadsheet of names, that is already a business. If the answer is nothing, fix that before anything else.
  • Fourth, price the platform into your unit economics. If a store takes 15% or 30%, or a message costs ₦14, those numbers belong in your price, not in your surprise. A product that is only profitable while a platform is free is a product with a deadline built in.

None of this means leaving platforms. For most early founders in Africa, WhatsApp, Instagram or an app store is where the customers already are, and ignoring that is worse than depending on it. The point is sequence: use the platform to find customers, then convert a share of them — steadily, every week — into customers you can reach directly. Rent the shopfront. Own the customer list.

05 — WORKED EXAMPLE: THE WHATSAPP BILL

Put a number on dependence, using the reported WhatsApp rates.

Illustrative example. Adaeze runs a small food business in Lagos. She takes orders and answers customers through the WhatsApp Business Platform, connected through a provider. In a normal month she sends about 1,000 chargeable service messages — order confirmations, delivery updates, replies to questions — and about 200 marketing messages about a weekend menu.

At the reported Nigerian rates, the maths is simple. Service messages: 1,000 × ₦14 = ₦14,000. Marketing messages: 200 × ₦84 = ₦16,800. Total: about ₦30,800 a month, on a channel that cost her nothing for replies a year earlier. That is not a large sum for a growing business — but it is a new cost line, it rises with every message she sends, and it arrives whether or not her sales rose. If she instead collected her customers' numbers into her own list and sent order updates by SMS or a cheaper channel for routine messages, part of that bill disappears — and, more importantly, the list survives any WhatsApp decision.

The numbers are illustrative — the rates are reported figures, and Adaeze is invented — but the decision rule is the point: count your platform messages, price them at the new rate, and ask whether the same customer could be reached a cheaper, owned way. If the answer is yes for routine messages, move those first. Keep the platform for what it does best: finding new customers and closing sales, not for every routine update.

06 — THE MONDAY EXERCISE

Before Monday ends, run the dependency audit on your main channel.

Write the answers down. A dependency you cannot see is one you cannot fix.

  • Name your biggest channel, and the share of last month's sales that came through it.
  • Write what breaks, in one sentence, if that channel reprices, restricts you, or closes tomorrow.
  • Count how many customers you can reach directly, without that platform — names and numbers you hold yourself.
  • Export your customer and order data from the platform, and check that the export is usable.
  • Pick one owned channel to start this week: a list, a simple storefront, or a second sales point — and put the first ten customers on it.

If the first answer is over half, and the third answer is close to zero, you have found your most important project for the month. It is not a new feature. It is a way back to your own customers.

THE TAKEAWAY

Use every platform that brings you customers — and build, from the first sale, a way to reach those customers without it. Rent the shopfront. Own the list.

Payment processing answers “Did the money move?” Your product must also answer “What does this customer now have permission to use?”

01 — THE GAP AFTER PAYMENT

A successful charge does not automatically deliver the product.

Imagine someone pays ₦5,000 for a course, a premium feature, a digital download, or a month of access. The payment provider confirms the charge, but your database never updates. The customer has paid and still sees a locked screen.

The reverse can happen too: your app unlocks a feature because the browser lands on a success page, even though the payment was never confirmed. That creates free access, support disputes, and potentially lost revenue.

These are separate responsibilities:

ACollect payment

The provider processes a transaction and reports its state.

BVerify payment

Your trusted server confirms the transaction and checks that it matches the expected order.

CFulfil the purchase

Your application records the entitlement and makes the purchased item available.

The principle: treat access as something your system grants because a verified business event occurred—not as a side effect of a redirect.

02 — THE SAFE FULFILMENT FLOW

Make the transaction-to-access path explicit.

  1. Create an order. Store the customer, product or plan, amount, currency, and a unique internal order ID.
  2. Start checkout. Create a payment attempt linked to that order and save the provider's reference.
  3. Wait for trusted confirmation. Use the provider's documented server-side verification and/or authenticated webhook process.
  4. Validate the details. Confirm the reference, successful final status, expected amount, currency, and relationship to the order. Do not trust values supplied only by the browser.
  5. Record the payment. Save the verified outcome in your database, with a clear transaction state and audit trail.
  6. Grant the entitlement once. Create or activate the user's access using an idempotent operation, ideally in the same database transaction as the state change where your architecture permits.
  7. Show the result. Update the customer-facing interface from your own stored order/access state.

Keep payment state and access state distinct. For example, an order can be paid while fulfilment is pending because a temporary database error prevented access from being granted. That gives you something specific to retry and monitor.

!
Never grant access based only on a return URL

A browser can be closed, a redirect can be forged, and a network request can fail. The return page is for user experience; trusted verification is for the payment decision.

03 — WEBHOOKS AND VERIFICATION

Assume notifications can arrive late or more than once.

A webhook is a server-to-server notification that an event occurred. It can help your application react when a customer closes the checkout tab or when confirmation arrives later. But webhook delivery can be delayed, retried, duplicated, or received out of order.

  • Authenticate it. Verify the signature or authentication mechanism specified by the provider before trusting the event.
  • Check the event and transaction. Confirm it refers to the expected provider, reference, amount, currency, and order. Use server-side verification when the provider's guidance requires it or the event alone is insufficient.
  • Make processing idempotent. Replaying the same event must not create a second entitlement, duplicate an order, or trigger repeated fulfilment.
  • Acknowledge appropriately. Follow the provider's webhook response and retry guidance. Avoid doing slow, fragile work inside a request if it can be safely queued.
  • Keep a recovery path. Reconcile transactions that stay pending or where payment was confirmed but access was not granted.

Do not assume every provider uses the same signature format, event names, retry schedule, or idempotency behaviour. Implement against the current documentation for the provider and payment method you actually use.

04 — MODEL WHAT THE CUSTOMER OWNS

Use an entitlement, not just a “paid” flag.

A single boolean such as isPaid = true may work for a tiny one-time purchase, but it quickly becomes ambiguous. Does it mean the customer paid once, has access forever, is on an active subscription, or has a refund pending?

An entitlement is the specific permission a customer receives: access to a course, a file, a feature, a seat, a usage allowance, or a service until a given date.

01One-time purchase

Link the verified order to the item purchased. Decide whether access is permanent, time-limited, or subject to another rule.

02Subscription

Track the subscription state and period. Grant access according to verified activation, renewal, cancellation, expiry, and payment-failure events.

03Usage or credits

Record a durable ledger or allocation so retries cannot add the same credits twice.

For subscriptions, a successful initial payment is not proof that every future renewal succeeded. Define how grace periods, failed renewals, cancellations, and expiry affect access, and communicate those rules clearly to customers.

05 — HANDLE THE UNUSUAL CASES

Decide what happens when the simple flow breaks.

  • Payment succeeds, access fails: keep the payment record, mark fulfilment as pending, and retry safely or raise a reconciliation task. Do not ask the customer to pay again by default.
  • The same webhook arrives twice: identify the event or transaction reference and return a safe response without granting access twice.
  • The customer pays the wrong amount or currency: do not grant the expected entitlement automatically. Flag it for an explicit business decision or support review.
  • Payment is refunded or disputed: apply a documented policy to access, keep an audit trail, and handle partial refunds or disputes deliberately.
  • A subscription renewal fails: apply the published retry and grace-period policy; do not treat a temporary failure as identical to a confirmed cancellation.
  • Two payment attempts exist for one order: reconcile both. Avoid fulfilling twice, and follow the provider's process for any duplicate charge or refund.

Automated retries should be safe to repeat. If an operation might charge the customer again, issue credits twice, send a paid download twice, or create duplicate fulfilment, first design a durable idempotency check.

06 — BEFORE YOU LAUNCH

Test the broken paths, not only the demo payment.

Payment and order
  • Every order has a unique reference and expected amount/currency.
  • The provider's documented verification or webhook authentication is implemented.
  • The application does not trust the success redirect alone.
Access and recovery
  • A verified purchase grants the correct entitlement exactly once.
  • Repeated webhooks and retries do not duplicate access, credits, or fulfilment.
  • A confirmed payment with failed fulfilment can be detected and recovered.
  • Refunds, cancellations, failed renewals, and expiry have explicit rules.
Customer support
  • The customer can see whether a purchase is pending, confirmed, or needs help.
  • Support can search by order ID and provider reference without asking for sensitive payment details.
  • Logs avoid storing card secrets, passwords, or unnecessary personal data.

Track at least two separate outcomes: verified payment success and successful fulfilment. Also monitor the number and age of paid orders whose access remains pending. That is where a “payment succeeded” dashboard can hide a product failure.

YOUR NEXT STEP

Trace one purchase from checkout to access.

Pick one paid feature in your product. Write down where the order is created, how payment is verified, where access is recorded, and how the system recovers if the last step fails. If any step exists only in your head, document it before adding more payment options.

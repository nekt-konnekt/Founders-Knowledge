How to Organise a Small Team Before Coordination Becomes the Cost

A team of three can decide over lunch. A team of twelve needs a meeting to decide who decides. Organise by product area, give each area one owner, and name the owner of the systems underneath — before the layers arrive.

When your team was three people, decisions happened over lunch. Now there are twelve of you, and a small change needs a meeting to decide who decides. Nobody got lazier. Nobody got less smart. The team got bigger — and bigger teams pay a tax that small teams do not. That tax is coordination: the work of keeping everyone pointed the same way. This lesson shows how to organise a small team so the tax stays low: by product area, with one owner per area, and one named owner for the systems underneath.

01 — THE COST ARRIVES QUIETLY

Your team did not get slower. It got bigger.

Coordination is the work of keeping people aligned: asking questions, giving updates, waiting for approvals, and checking that two people are not building the same thing twice. With three people, there are three pairs of people who might need to talk. With twelve people, there are sixty-six pairs. Each new person does not add one conversation. They add a conversation with everyone else.

That is why growth feels like slowing down. The founder becomes the switchboard: every question routes through one person, and every answer waits for that person to be free. Work that used to take an afternoon now takes a week, because it waits in three inboxes. And the worst part is that the cost is invisible. Nobody logs "waiting for approval" as a task. It just looks like a team that has lost its speed.

Most founders answer this by hiring more people, or by adding a layer: a manager to manage the managers. Both make the tax worse before they make it better, because both add more pairs of people who must stay aligned. The cheaper answer is to change the shape of the team, so that most decisions never need to cross the whole group at all.

✳
The rule

If a decision needs the whole team in the room, the team is organised wrong. Most decisions should need one person: the owner of that area.

02 — WHAT MONIEPOINT ACTUALLY CHANGED

A large company hit the same wall — and rebuilt its shape.

Moniepoint is a Nigerian financial services company. It started in 2015 as TeamApt, and became a unicorn — a startup valued at $1 billion or more — in October 2024, after a $110 million Series C round. It now spans payments, banking, credit and business management. In late October 2026, it announced it was rebuilding how its engineering and product teams work.

The change has three parts. First, the company is moving to platform groups: small teams, each owning one product area from building it to running it. "End to end" means the same team designs it, builds it, ships it, and fixes it when it breaks — no handoff to another team at any point. Second, it is creating platform architect roles: senior engineers who design and look after the shared systems underneath — the common pieces every product area uses — so each group does not build its own copy. Third, some existing roles will disappear in the new shape. The company says affected staff will be considered for other roles first, and a formal redundancy process — the process for ending a role the company no longer needs — starts only where no other role fits.

Two things matter about how the company described it. It said the change followed a regular review of its operations, and that it is not a layoff. No headcount figure was given. Separately, employees told reporters that staff were offered five months' salary to leave voluntarily, that the offer window closed on 23 October, and that staff would be told between 2 and 11 November whether their roles are affected. Those terms are employee-reported, not confirmed by the company, and this lesson does not treat them as fact. What is confirmed is the shape: small teams, one product area each, owning it end to end, with named owners for the shared systems underneath.

There is a second, quieter signal in the same season. Moniepoint stopped its remittance product, Monieworld — a service for sending money between the UK and Nigeria — in August 2026, after about eighteen months. A company that is narrowing what it builds, and rebuilding how it builds, is making the same choice twice: focus, then shape.

03 — WHY SMALL TEAMS THAT OWN END TO END MOVE FASTER

Three reasons, and none of them is "small teams work harder".

First, fewer handoffs. Every handoff is a wait and a chance to misunderstand. When one team builds a feature and another team runs it, the second team inherits decisions it did not make, and every bug becomes a negotiation about whose job it is. When the same team builds and runs it, the bug lands on the desk of the person who wrote the code. That is faster, and it is also fairer: the team that takes the shortcut pays for it.

Second, one person can decide. A team that owns its area end to end has one owner who can say yes or no without asking three other teams. Decisions that used to need a meeting now need a sentence. This is the whole point of ownership: it moves the decision to the person closest to the work, and it names who answers when the decision is wrong.

Third, the feedback loop is short. A team that runs what it builds learns fast. Customers complain to the people who can fix it. Usage numbers reach the people who chose the design. Compare that with a team that builds and throws its work over a wall: it learns nothing, because the complaints go to someone else.

There is an old rule of thumb from Amazon for team size: a team should be small enough to feed with two pizzas. It is not a law, and the exact number does not matter. What matters is the test behind it: if a team needs a meeting to know what its own members are doing, it is too big. Split it by product area, not by job type.

04 — THE SHAPE TO COPY

Three rules. They fit a team of six and a team of sixty.

  • 1. Organise by product area, not by job type. Do not build a "frontend team" and a "backend team" and a "design team" that must all agree before anything ships. Build a "payments team" that has the frontend, backend and design skills inside it, and a "merchant tools team" the same way. Each team can ship its own area without waiting on the others. The job-type split feels tidy on an org chart. It is slow in practice, because every piece of work crosses three teams.
  • 2. One owner per area. Every product area has one named person who decides and who answers when it breaks. Not two owners — two owners means no owner, because each one assumes the other decided. Not "the team owns it" — a team cannot answer a question. One name, written down, where everyone can see it.
  • 3. Name the owner of the shared systems. The things every area uses — login, payments, the database, customer data — need one owner too. If nobody owns them, every team builds its own copy, and the copies drift apart until nothing fits together. This is the job Moniepoint's new platform architects do. At your size, it may be one senior engineer with the title written into their job, not a new hire. The point is the name on the list, not the job title.
◈
The line

Copy the principle, not the structure. Moniepoint needs platform architects because it has dozens of product areas. You need one owner per area and one owner for the shared systems — that is the same idea, at your size.

05 — TWO FOUNDERS, ONE TEAM

The same nine people, organised two ways.

Illustrative examples. Ada runs a food-delivery startup in Lagos with nine staff: three engineers, two dispatchers, a designer, a customer-support lead, an operations lead, and herself. Everything routes through her. A rider app change needs the designer, then an engineer, then Ada's approval, then the operations lead to tell the dispatchers. The change takes nine days, and Ada spends her week answering questions she hired people to answer.

Bola runs a laundry-pickup startup, also with nine staff. She split them into three areas: "customer app", "rider operations", and "payments and data". Each area has one owner. The customer-app owner can change the app's booking screen without asking the payments owner, because the booking screen is hers. The payments owner looks after the shared payment system for all three areas. Bola still decides strategy and spending. She no longer decides the colour of a button.

The difference is not talent or effort. It is shape. Ada's team has nine people and one decision-maker. Bola's team has nine people and three decision-makers, each inside their own area. When Bola's team grows to twelve, she adds a person to an area. When Ada's team grows to twelve, she adds a person to her inbox.

06 — WHAT COULD CHANGE THE ANSWER

Three limits on everything above.

First, shape does not fix a product nobody wants. If customers are not buying, reorganising the team is rearranging chairs. The audit below is worth an afternoon. It is not worth a month, and it is not a substitute for talking to customers.

Second, a reorganisation costs attention. Changing who owns what means new habits, new meetings while people learn the shape, and a week or two when things feel slower, not faster. Do it once, deliberately, and then leave it alone for a quarter. A team that reorganises every month never learns any shape.

Third, at very small size, do not over-structure. With two or three people, the founder is the owner of everything, and that is correct — there is nothing to split yet. The shape in this lesson starts to pay at about five or six people, when one person can no longer hold the whole product in their head. And Moniepoint is a large company: its answer fits dozens of teams. Yours is smaller, so copy the principle — one owner per area — not the full structure. For the other half of the team question — who to hire, and in what order — read the companion lesson, Your First Five Hires Decide Your Burn and Your Culture. That lesson is the hiring sequence; this one is the shape the hires work inside.

07 — THE MONDAY AUDIT

One afternoon. A list, three columns, and one fix this week.

  • List every product area you have: the customer app, the rider app, payments, support, onboarding — whatever your business actually is. One line each.
  • Write one owner's name next to each area. If an area has no name, it has no owner. If it has two names, it has no owner either.
  • List the shared systems underneath: login, payments, the database, customer records, analytics. Write one owner's name next to each.
  • Circle the areas with no owner, and the areas with two. Those are the places where decisions are currently waiting on you.
  • Fix one this week. Give the area to the person already doing most of the work in it, tell the team, and write the name down where everyone can see it.
  • Put the list on the wall — or in the shared doc the team actually reads — and revisit it when you hire, not when things break.

If every area has one name and every shared system has one name, you are done. The next time a decision waits on a meeting, you will know exactly whose desk it is sitting on.

THE TAKEAWAY

Coordination is a cost, not a character flaw. Pay it once, up front, with one owner per area — or pay it every day in meetings, waits and dropped work.

FOUNDER OPERATIONS / PARTNER DUE DILIGENCE

Verify the partner, not just the pitch.

Before a fintech partnership, payment integration, or investment arrangement, check who you are dealing with, what they are authorised to do, and whether the offer matches that authority.

A polished website, a familiar logo, a referral, or a convincing pitch does not establish that a financial company is authorised to do what it is offering. Verify the specific activity before you commit funds, customer data, or your company’s reputation.

01 — START WITH THE ACTIVITY

Ask what the partner will actually do.

“Fintech” describes an industry, not a single licence. A partner processing payments, holding customer funds, offering investment products, arranging securities transactions, lending, or providing digital-asset services may sit under different rules and regulatory arrangements.

Write down the exact activity you expect the partner to perform. Then ask which legal entity will perform it, where it will operate, who holds customer funds or assets, and which regulator or regulators oversee that activity.

Make the offer specific
  • Will the company process payments, hold funds, lend, manage investments, arrange investments, or simply provide software?
  • Which country and customer group does the service cover?
  • Which entity signs the contract and is responsible if something goes wrong?
  • Does the proposed service require authorisation, registration, a partner arrangement, or another approval?

If the partner cannot explain the legal entity and regulatory basis for the service in clear terms, pause. Do not treat a vague answer as proof of wrongdoing; treat it as an unresolved due-diligence question.

02 — USE THE RIGHT REGISTER

Check the regulator that matches the service.

For Nigerian founders, these official resources are useful starting points. They are not interchangeable, and none should be treated as a universal directory of every kind of fintech business.

SECCapital-market activities

Use the SEC’s registered-operator search when the offer involves securities, investment solicitation, brokerage, investment advice, fund or portfolio management, or another capital-market activity.

Search SEC registered operators ↗

CBNPayments and payment services

Use the CBN’s Payment Service Providers page to inspect listed categories and entities. Confirm the exact licence category and current status for the activity you need.

Check CBN payment service providers ↗

The SEC also publishes a separate directory of fintech operators in its Regulatory Incubation and Accelerated Regulatory Incubation programmes. That directory describes operators in those programmes; do not assume it replaces the main registered-operator search or proves a firm has every licence it might need.

View SEC registered fintech operators ↗

Depending on the service, another regulator or a cross-border regulator may be relevant. If the classification is unclear, ask the regulator or obtain qualified legal advice before proceeding.

03 — MATCH THE LEGAL ENTITY

Make sure the record belongs to the company you are dealing with.

Brand names, trading names, app names, parent companies, subsidiaries, and contracted providers can differ. A listing for one company does not automatically validate another company using a similar name or logo.

Compare these details
  • Full legal name and registration or company identifier.
  • Regulator, licence or registration category, and permitted functions.
  • Current status, any conditions, and dates shown in the official record.
  • Website domain, official contact details, and the entity named in the contract.
  • Whether the actual service and country you plan to use fall within the recorded scope.

Search by legal name, not only by brand. If details differ, ask the company to explain the relationship and verify that explanation independently using official contact information. Do not rely on a certificate or screenshot sent by the prospective partner when the regulator has a searchable current record.

A corporate registration record by itself is not the same as a financial-services licence. Likewise, appearing in a regulator’s directory does not establish that every product, return, or claim made by the company is approved.

04 — READ REGULATOR NOTICES

Check warnings and restrictions, not just directory listings.

On 9 October 2026, Nigeria’s Securities and Exchange Commission issued a notice about Wayvepay. The SEC stated that Wayvepay was not registered with the Commission to solicit investments from the public or operate in any capacity within the Nigerian capital market, and advised the public not to invest with it in relation to that market.

This example illustrates why a founder should read the regulator’s actual wording. The notice is about the SEC registration and capital-market activities described by the Commission; do not expand it into claims the notice does not make.

Read the SEC’s Wayvepay notice ↗

Search the regulator’s current notices, circulars, enforcement announcements, and warnings. A clean search result is not a guarantee that a company is safe; notices may be updated and records may not cover every risk. Check close spelling variants and the legal entity name.

05 — VERIFY THE PARTNERSHIP ITSELF

A legitimate company name can still be used in a fake offer.

Even if the company exists and the relevant record looks valid, independently confirm that the person contacting you represents that company and that the proposed partnership is real.

01Contact through a verified channel

Use contact details published by the company or regulator—not only the phone number, email, WhatsApp account, or link in the pitch.

02Confirm the contract and money flow

Check the legal contracting entity, fees, settlement arrangements, custody of funds, refund obligations, and who is liable for failures.

03Ask for written scope

Request the service description, authorisation details, territory, limits, and any required approvals or third-party arrangements.

04Protect customer data and access

Review security, privacy, incident reporting, data handling, access controls, and exit or migration terms before sharing production data or credentials.

Be especially cautious if the pitch promises guaranteed investment returns, demands immediate payment, refuses to identify the contracting entity, directs funds to an unrelated personal account, discourages independent checks, or uses regulatory logos as a substitute for verifiable evidence.

One warning sign does not prove fraud on its own. It is a reason to pause, document the concern, and resolve it independently before proceeding.

06 — BEFORE YOU PROCEED

Use this checklist before signing, integrating, or transferring money.

  • I can name the exact legal entity and verify its identity independently.
  • I have identified the regulator relevant to the actual service and country.
  • I checked the official register and understood the recorded category, scope, and current status.
  • I searched for current warnings, restrictions, and enforcement notices.
  • I independently confirmed that the representative and proposed partnership are genuine.
  • The contract names the right entity and clearly explains fees, responsibilities, settlement, custody, refunds, and dispute handling.
  • I understand how customer funds, personal data, credentials, and production access will be protected.
  • I have documented unresolved questions and obtained legal or regulatory guidance where needed.

If an essential check fails or remains unclear, delay the commitment. A short pause is usually cheaper than trying to unwind a bad contract, recover funds, or explain a preventable incident to customers.

YOUR NEXT STEP

Verify one partner before the next commitment.

Write down the service, the legal entity, the regulator, the official record, and the unresolved questions. Keep a dated copy of the evidence and re-check it before signing or expanding the relationship.

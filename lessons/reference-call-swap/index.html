The Reference-Call Swap: Hiring Checks That Work in Informal Markets

The CV looks good and the referees say good things. In a market where most past employers keep no records, that tells you less than you think.

You are about to hire your first developer — or your first support person, or your first salesperson. The CV looks good. The interview went well. The two referees the candidate named say good things. A referee is a person you call to speak about a candidate's past work. You make the offer. Three weeks later the truth arrives: the work is slower than promised, "led the project" meant "sat in the meetings", and one referee was a friend. The hire costs you a month of runway and a painful exit. The problem was not that you checked. It was what you checked — the candidate's chosen story, not the work itself. This lesson is about a simple swap: do not only call the people the candidate named. Find one person who worked with them but was not named, and call that person too.

01 — THE LISTED REFEREE

The check that checks nothing.

Most founders ask for two or three referees, then call them. That feels careful. It is not. The candidate chose those names. They chose people who like them. In many cases the referee is a friend, a former classmate, or a manager from a different team who barely saw the work.

In Nigeria this problem is bigger than in a formal job market. About 93 in every 100 working Nigerians are in informal work, according to the National Bureau of Statistics' Labour Force Survey for the second quarter of 2024. Informal work means work with no written contract, no payslip trail and no human-resources office. Most small employers keep no file on who did what. So when you call a past employer, there is often no record to check against — only a memory, and a person who may have every reason to be kind.

Surveys from other countries show the same gap in formal markets too. A survey of 1,000 young Australians by Xref, a reference-checking company, found that 71% admitted taking advantage of weak spots in the reference-checking process, and 23% said they had asked a referee to lie for them. A fraud-prevention survey by Cifas, reported in the UK, found that one in five candidates admitted lying on their CV. And research by Standout CV found that nearly one in five people who lied about a reference had used a fake reference service — sometimes a paid actor pretending to be a past employer. These are British and Australian numbers, not Nigerian ones. No similar Nigerian survey was found for this lesson. But the mechanism travels: wherever the check is easy to game, some candidates will game it.

✳
The rule

A referee the candidate chose is a character witness, not a work check. Treat the list as a starting point, never as the check itself.

02 — WHAT A REFERENCE CALL SHOULD TEST

Not "good person?" — "did the work?"

A reference call has one job: to test whether the candidate did the work they claim, in the way they claim. Three things matter: what they actually did day to day; how they behaved when the work got hard; and whether the person would hire them again.

Most founders ask the wrong question. "Was she good?" gets you a polite yes. "What did she do on a normal Tuesday?" gets you the truth — or the silence that tells you there is no truth to get. The difference between the two questions is the difference between a reference check and a chat. One tests the work. The other tests manners.

Notice what this means for the interview too. An interview tests how a person talks about work. A reference call tests whether the work happened. You need both. But founders who interview well and check badly are hiring talk, not work — and in a team of five, one hire who cannot do the work is a quarter of your capacity.

03 — THE SWAP

Find your own referees.

The swap is simple. Do not only call the people the candidate named. Find one person who worked with the candidate but was not named, and call them too. This is called a back-channel reference — a check with someone the candidate did not choose. Here is how to find one.

  • Ask for the direct manager. Ask the candidate for the name of the person they reported to — not a colleague, the manager. Then ask that manager for the name of one other person who worked with the candidate every day. Each name leads to the next.
  • Use your own network. Who do you know who worked at that company, in that team, at that time? One message — "Did you work with X?" — is often enough. In a small market, two or three messages usually find someone.
  • Follow the work itself. A past project, a client, a supplier. A person who received the candidate's work can tell you whether it arrived on time and in good shape — even if they never managed the candidate.

Call, do not email. A call catches a pause. An email lets the referee write a safe paragraph and send it back. A call takes five minutes. Tell the referee at the start: "I am calling about X, who worked with you at Y. Five minutes, three questions." People say yes to five minutes.

Tell the candidate first. Say: "I check references by calling the people you name and one person I find myself. Is there anyone I should not call, and why?" That question alone is useful. A candidate with nothing to hide answers it easily. A candidate who objects to every name is telling you something too. And never call a candidate's current employer without their permission — that call can cost them their job before you have offered them one.

04 — THE THREE QUESTIONS

Ask these, word for word.

Use the same three questions with every referee. Same questions, different people, comparable answers. That is the whole method.

One: "What did they actually do, day to day?" A good answer names specific work: tasks, tools, numbers if the job had them. A worrying answer is vague praise — "a great team player", "very hardworking" — with no task attached. If the referee cannot name the work, they did not see the work.

Two: "Tell me about a time they had to do something hard, or something unpopular." A good answer is a real story, with a difficulty and a result. A worrying answer is "I can't think of one", or a story where the candidate did nothing hard. Every real job has a hard week. No story means no memory — or no hard week, which means the job was smaller than the CV says.

Three: "Would you hire them again — and for what role?" A good answer is "Yes, for this exact role" without a pause. A worrying answer is a pause, a laugh, or "for a different role". "For a different role" is a polite no. Also watch for the referee who answers a different question: "Well, they are a lovely person" is not an answer to "would you hire them again".

One bonus question, if the call is going well: "What should I know before I manage them?" Good referees answer this honestly. "Give clear deadlines — they drift without them" is gold. That answer is not a rejection. It is a management manual, free.

◈
The line

Three questions, two voices, before the offer. The questions test the work. The second voice tests the first.

05 — WHAT A BAD ANSWER SOUNDS LIKE

The warning signs.

  • The pause before the praise. A referee who needs three seconds to find something good to say has already said it.
  • Praise of character, no mention of work. "Lovely person", "great attitude" — and nothing about what they built, fixed or shipped.
  • The referee who cannot name the work. They do not know the candidate's manager, team or project. They know the candidate, not the job.
  • The referee who is clearly a friend. "We go way back." A friend is not a referee, however honest they are.
  • The title that does not match the work. "Head of Engineering" for a team of one, doing support. Titles are cheap in small companies. Ask what the title covered.
  • "I can't really say." That is often the most honest answer you will get — and it usually means something went wrong.

One bad answer is not a verdict. One referee may hold a grudge, or remember a bad week instead of two good years. The swap protects you here too: two independent voices — one named, one found — are much harder to fake, and much fairer to the candidate, than one voice either way.

06 — TWO FOUNDERS, ONE HIRE

The same CV, handled two ways.

Illustrative examples. Chidi hires a developer. The CV says "led the backend rebuild at a Lagos fintech". Two named referees praise him. Chidi offers. In week three, the developer cannot explain the codebase he supposedly rebuilt. The referees were a friend and a manager from a different team. Chidi spends a month managing him out, and the rebuild stalls.

Funke hires for the same role, from the same CV, with the same referees. But she also asks the candidate who he reported to, calls that manager, and asks the three questions. The manager says: "Good engineer — but he did not lead the rebuild. He built one service inside it, and he needs clear tickets." Funke hires him anyway — for the job he can actually do, at the level he can actually do it. She sets the work to match. No surprise, no exit. The swap did not reject a good hire. It priced him correctly.

The difference is not money. It is two five-minute calls and one question asked before the offer. That is the size of this job for a small team.

07 — WHAT COULD CHANGE THE ANSWER

Four limits on everything above.

First, a reference call is one input, not a verdict. One person's memory is not the whole truth. Two voices are better than one, but two voices can still be wrong — especially if they come from the same unhappy team.

Second, some referees can only confirm dates. In some companies, policy — or caution about lawsuits — means a past employer will only confirm job title and dates. If a referee says "I can only confirm dates", respect it. It is not a bad sign by itself.

Third, people soften bad news. In a small market where everyone knows everyone, few people will speak badly of a former colleague on the record. Expect soft answers. That is why the rehire question matters: it is the question that is hardest to soften.

Fourth, the call costs your time. For a five-person team hiring one person, ten minutes is cheap. For a large hiring round, calling every referee for every candidate does not scale — but you are not hiring at scale. You are hiring one person who will shape your burn and your culture. That is what the first-five-hires lesson argues, and it is why the check is worth the ten minutes.

08 — THE MONDAY CHECK

Do the swap on your next hire.

  • Before the offer, ask the candidate for the direct manager's name — not a colleague, the manager.
  • Find one back-channel referee through your own network, or by asking the manager who worked with the candidate daily.
  • Call both — five minutes each, the same three questions. Write down the answers, word for word, before you decide.
  • If the two voices disagree, call a third person — or run a small paid trial task instead of guessing.
  • Tell the candidate you are checking, and never call their current employer without permission.

If you do this and both voices agree the candidate is strong, you have not wasted ten minutes. You have bought confidence in the most expensive decision a small team makes: who to trust with the work.

THE TAKEAWAY

The candidate's list tells you who likes them. Your own call tells you what they did. Swap the list for the work — three questions, two voices, before the offer.

Evidence & limits

What is verified: about 93% of employment in Nigeria is informal — National Bureau of Statistics Labour Force Survey, second quarter of 2024, as cited in the European Union Agency for Asylum's Nigeria country focus. The Xref Recruitment Risk Index — a survey of 1,000 Australians aged 18 to 39 who had applied for a job in the last two years — found 71% admitted taking advantage of weak spots in the reference-checking process and 23% said they had encouraged a referee to lie on their behalf. A fraud-prevention survey by Cifas found one in five candidates admitted lying on their CV, and research by Standout CV found nearly one in five people who lied about a reference had used a fake reference service — both as reported by The Global Recruiter.

What is reported, not independently verified: the survey figures above come from the companies' own reports of their surveys; the desk did not re-run or audit them, and the samples are British and Australian, not Nigerian.

What is interpretation: the swap routine, the three questions and the warning signs are method — drawn from standard hiring practice, not from a study. No Nigerian survey of CV honesty or reference-checking was found for this lesson, so the non-Nigerian figures are used to show the mechanism, not as Nigerian rates.

What is invented for teaching: Chidi and Funke in section 06 are illustrative; the founders, businesses and team details are invented. Sources were consulted on 8 November 2026.

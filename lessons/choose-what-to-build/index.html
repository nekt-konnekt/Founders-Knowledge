FOUNDER DECISIONS / FRAMEWORK

Choose what to build next.

When every idea feels important, progress gets scattered. Use a simple decision system to protect focus and make the next move explicit.

A long backlog can look like ambition while quietly becoming a source of distraction. The goal is not to find the perfect priority list. It is to choose a useful next step, understand why it matters, and review what you learn.

01 — NOTICE THE DRIFT

Not every good idea deserves attention now.

Founders have limited time, money, energy, and access to customers. Starting another feature or project means something else moves later. If you never name that trade-off, urgent requests and exciting ideas can set your direction for you.

Before ranking tasks, ask what stage the work is actually in. Are you still trying to understand the problem? Do you need evidence that people want the solution? Is the product already useful but hard to find? Is reliability blocking trust? Different problems require different next moves.

02 — MAKE THE CHOICE VISIBLE

Sort work into four decisions.

A SIMPLE PRIORITY SYSTEM

  • Ship now: the smallest useful improvement or fix that removes a real blocker.
  • Build next: the next important piece supported by evidence and aligned with the current goal.
  • Test first: an uncertain idea where a small experiment can prevent expensive work.
  • Keep / evaluate: valuable possibilities that are not urgent enough to displace the current priority.

“Keep / evaluate” is not a promise to build later. It means preserve the idea, note what evidence would make it worth revisiting, and return to the work you already chose.

03 — USE EVIDENCE, NOT EXCITEMENT

Choose the next move by the problem it solves.

For each candidate, ask four questions: Which current goal does it support? What evidence says this is a real problem? What is the smallest version that could help? What will it displace?

01Impact on the current constraint

Will it address the main reason progress is blocked, or merely add polish?

02Confidence and cost

What do you know, what are you guessing, and how expensive is it to learn more?

Do not treat a scoring formula as an oracle. Scores can make trade-offs clearer, but uncertain estimates should not create false precision.

04 — PROTECT YOUR CAPACITY

Limit work in progress.

Too many active priorities create switching costs and make completion hard to see. Keep one main outcome for the current work period, define what “done” means, and avoid opening a new front unless something important has changed.

WRITE THE COMMITMENT

Until [review date], the main outcome is [specific result]. I will ship [small deliverable]. I will not start [distraction or competing task] unless [clear exception].

This is not a rule against changing your mind. It is a way to make changes deliberate rather than reactive.

05 — LEARN FROM THE RESULT

Review outcomes, not just completed tasks.

At the end of the work period, check whether the intended outcome changed. Did the fix remove the blocker? Did the experiment reduce uncertainty? Did the release reach the people it was meant to help? If not, decide whether to improve, change direction, or stop.

!
Reality check

Customer safety, security, legal obligations, and serious reliability issues can override a planned priority. Focus is a tool for judgment, not an excuse to ignore risk.

PUT IT TO WORK

Your next step.

Take the current list of things you could do and make one decision for each:

  1. Write the most important outcome for the next work period.
  2. Choose one item to ship now and define what “done” means.
  3. Move uncertain ideas to “test first” and write the smallest useful experiment.
  4. Put everything else in “build next” or “keep / evaluate,” with a reason.
  5. Set a review date and use the result to decide what comes after.
! Keep the system small

If maintaining your prioritization process takes more work than making the decision, simplify it. The system exists to protect execution, not become another project.

THE TAKEAWAY

Progress is not doing everything on the list. It is choosing the right next thing — and making room to finish it.

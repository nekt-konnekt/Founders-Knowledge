The Discount Question: What a Customer Really Means When They Ask for a Lower Price

A customer asks for a lower price. That is not a price problem. It is a question about value — and the answer is not always a smaller number.

A customer who asks for less is telling you something. Hear it before you answer it.

“

A customer looks at your price and says: “Can you do it cheaper?” Your first feeling is pressure. Say yes and you keep the sale. Say no and you might lose it. That pressure is real. But the question is not really about your price. It is about what the customer can see — and what they cannot. This lesson shows you how to hear what a discount request is really asking, what to do before you touch the price, and when a discount is honestly the right answer.

01 — THE REQUEST IS INFORMATION

“Can you do it cheaper?” is a question, not a verdict.

A discount request usually means one of three things. Each one needs a different answer, so the first job is to find out which one you are hearing.

First, “I cannot see the value yet.” The customer does not understand what the price buys. They see the number, not the result. This is the most common meaning, and it is good news: nothing is wrong with your price. Something is missing from your explanation.

Second, “I cannot afford it.” The price is genuinely above what this customer can pay. That is a different problem. It is not about your explanation. It is about their budget — and the honest answer may be a smaller package, not a smaller price for the same thing.

Third, “I am testing whether the price is real.” In many markets, the first price is an opening offer, not a final one. A customer who has bargained all their life will ask for less out of habit, whatever the number is. If you drop the price at the first ask, you confirm that the first number was never real — and the next ask will come sooner.

Here is the mistake: answering the price question before you know which meaning it carries. A discount given to the first customer — the one who cannot see the value — teaches them nothing about the value. A discount given to the third customer — the one who is testing you — teaches them that asking works. In both cases you lose money and learn nothing.

So do not answer the price question. Ask a question back. Two work well. “What are you comparing this with?” tells you the real competition — a rival, a free tool, or nothing at all. “What would make this worth the price to you?” tells you what the customer actually wants. The answer tells you which of the three meanings you are hearing. Then, and only then, decide what to do.

✳
The first move

Never answer a discount request with a number. Answer it with a question: “What are you comparing this with?” The reply tells you whether the problem is value, budget, or habit.

02 — WHY CUTTING FIRST COSTS TWICE

A small cut costs more than it looks.

Before you cut a price, work out what the cut actually costs. The numbers below are a made-up example with round figures, to show the maths. Use your own numbers before you agree to anything.

Say you sell a product for ₦10,000. It costs you ₦8,000 to deliver — your time, your tools, your running costs. Your margin, the money left over on each sale, is ₦2,000. That is 20% of the price. Sell 100 of them and you make ₦200,000.

Now cut the price by 10%, to ₦9,000. Your cost is still ₦8,000. Your margin is now ₦1,000 — half what it was. To make the same ₦200,000, you now need 200 sales, not 100. A 10% price cut needs twice as many customers to earn the same money. A 20% cut, to ₦8,000, leaves no margin at all: you would be working for nothing.

That is the first cost. The second cost is slower, and it lasts longer. The discount becomes the price. The customer who got 10% off expects 10% off next time. They tell other customers the “real” price is the discounted one. Every future sale starts from the lower number, and every future negotiation starts with “but last time you did it for less.”

The third cost is the quietest. You start competing on price instead of value. The customers you win by being cheapest are the customers who care least about what you do well — and they leave the moment someone cheaper appears. A business built on discounts has to keep discounting to keep its customers. A business built on value keeps its customers because the value is real.

✳
The maths

At a 20% margin, a 10% price cut halves your margin — so you need twice as many sales to earn the same money. Work it out on your own numbers before you agree to anything.

03 — THREE MOVES BEFORE YOU TOUCH THE PRICE

Ask, name, trade — in that order.

When a customer asks for less, make three moves before the price moves. In this order.

Move one: ask what they are comparing. “What are you comparing this with?” If they name a cheaper rival, you have learned the real competition — and you can talk about what the rival does not do. If they name a free tool or a spreadsheet, you have learned they misunderstood what you sell. If they name nothing, the request was a habit, not a judgement — and it often goes away on its own once you ask.

Move two: name the value in their terms. Not your features — their money, their time, their risk. “It has automated reminders” means little. “It chases the invoices you forget, and one recovered invoice a month is worth more than the fee” means something, because it is in the customer's own numbers. Translate the price into what it saves or earns them. If the value is bigger than the price, say so — in their numbers, not yours.

Move three: trade, don't cut. If you move on price, get something back. A smaller package for a smaller price — cut the scope, not the rate. Payment upfront, instead of monthly. A longer commitment. A public testimonial, or an introduction to three other buyers. A trade keeps the price honest: the customer gets less money out, and you get something real in. A cut just makes the number smaller, and teaches the customer that asking works.

Notice what the three moves do together. The first tells you what the problem is. The second answers it. The third protects you if you do move. Most discount conversations skip all three and go straight to the number. That is how a founder ends up selling at a price that cannot pay the bills — and wondering why the business feels harder every month.

✳
The script

“What are you comparing this with?” Then: “Here is what it saves you.” Then: “I can do this for that, if you do this.” Three moves, in that order, before the price moves.

04 — WHEN A DISCOUNT IS THE RIGHT ANSWER

Sometimes the right answer is yes.

A rule against discounting is not a rule against ever cutting a price. There are four honest cases where a discount is the right answer. What they share is a reason you can say out loud, a limit, and an end.

First, the price is genuinely wrong. You set it before you knew your real costs, or the market has moved since you set it. Then the honest move is to fix the price for everyone — not to hand out quiet discounts one customer at a time. A wrong price is a pricing problem. Fix it as one.

Second, a first customer whose name opens doors. A well-known customer — a respected school, a busy clinic, a company everyone in your market knows — can win you the next ten customers just by using your product. A discount for that customer is not a cut. It is marketing, paid in price instead of cash. Cap it: one customer, a clear end, in writing.

Third, a real affordability wall in a real segment. Some customers genuinely cannot pay your price — small schools, small clinics, small traders. The answer is a smaller package at a smaller price: fewer features, a lower tier, a different product. That is not discounting the same thing. It is selling a smaller thing.

Fourth, a commitment that lowers your cost. A year paid upfront. A bulk order. A guaranteed volume every month. When the customer's commitment makes your business cheaper to run, sharing some of that saving is fair — the discount comes from your lower cost, not from fear.

If your reason is none of these four, you are probably cutting the price because the conversation is uncomfortable. Discomfort is not a reason. Say so, politely, and hold the price.

05 — WRITE YOUR NO-DISCOUNT RULE

Decide the rule before the call, not during it.

The rule is one page. Write it before your next sales call, and read it before you dial. It has three lines.

Your floor. The lowest price you will accept, and why. The floor comes from your costs: below it, the sale loses money, and a sale that loses money is worse than no sale. Write the number down. A floor that lives only in your head moves when you are under pressure. A floor on paper does not.

Your trades. What you will accept in exchange for a cut: a smaller package, payment upfront, a longer commitment, a testimonial, introductions. Write the list before the call, so you are choosing from it — not inventing under pressure.

Your walk-away line. The sentence you say when the answer is no. Something plain, like: “I understand. At that price I cannot deliver the quality you need, so I would rather not take the work.” Say it calmly. Then stop talking. The silence is part of the answer.

One more thing the rule must cover: who on your team can give a discount? If everyone can, the price is whatever the last conversation made it. If one person decides — or the rule decides — the price stays the price. In a team of two or three, that one person is usually you.

✳
The rule, in one page

Floor: the lowest price that still makes money. Trades: what you will accept in exchange for a cut. Walk-away line: the sentence you say when the answer is no. Write it before the call, not during it.

06 — TWO WORKED CASES

The same request, read two ways.

Both cases below are illustrative — made-up stories built to show the script in use, not reports of real companies. The numbers are round and simple for the same reason.

First, the invoicing tool. A founder sells invoicing software to small businesses at ₦15,000 a month. A shop owner asks for ₦10,000. She asks what he is comparing it with: a free spreadsheet. She names the value in his terms: the software chases unpaid invoices, and one recovered invoice a month is worth more than the fee. He still cannot pay ₦15,000 — his budget is real. So she trades, not cuts: a smaller package at his price — invoicing only, without the payment reminders. He takes it. Three months later he comes back for the reminders, at the full price. The scope moved. The rate did not.

Second, the delivery app. A founder runs deliveries for restaurants at ₦800 per order. A restaurant asks for ₦500. She asks what they compare it with: their own rider, who costs less but misses orders. She names the value in their terms: her riders deliver in 30 minutes, and every late order costs the restaurant a customer who does not come back. She refuses the cut — and offers a trade: ₦700 per order if the restaurant guarantees 100 orders a month. The restaurant agrees. The price moved because the volume lowered her cost per order, not because she was afraid of losing the sale.

Notice what both founders did. Neither answered the price question first. Both asked what the customer meant. Both named the value in the customer's numbers. And when the price did move, something real moved with it — scope in the first case, volume in the second. That is the difference between a trade and a surrender.

07 — WHAT COULD CHANGE THE ANSWER

Five limits on everything above.

First, some markets expect bargaining. In a market where the first price is always negotiated, a firm “no” can end the conversation. The script still works — ask, name, trade — but expect the conversation to take longer, and set your floor before it starts. The rule does not change. The patience needed does.

Second, a customer with real buying power changes the maths. One customer who is half your revenue is not a normal negotiation. The rule still applies, but the walk-away line is harder to use — and that concentration is itself a problem to fix. If one customer can break you by leaving, the discount question is not your biggest problem. The dependence is.

Third, prices are not promises. If your costs rise — fuel, data, foreign exchange — a price that was right last year can be wrong now. Review the price itself every quarter. Do not let discount conversations do that job for you, one customer at a time.

Fourth, this lesson is about selling to businesses. Selling to individual consumers — a shop, a market stall — has different rules, and a posted price is usually the price. The script here is for the conversation before a business signs, not for the till.

Fifth, all of this assumes you know your costs. If you do not know what each sale costs you, no discount rule will save you. Work out the cost first. Our companion lessons on pricing set the price itself; this lesson defends it.

08 — THE NEXT STEP

Write the rule before the next sales call.

  1. Work out your margin on one product this week: price minus cost, in naira, per sale. Write it down.
  2. Write your no-discount rule on one page: your floor, your trades, your walk-away line.
  3. Before your next sales call, write the three questions: what are they comparing, what is it worth to them, what will you trade.
  4. The next time a customer asks for a lower price, ask one question before you answer. Write down what they say — and which of the three meanings it was.

A discount request is a question about value. Answer the question before you change the number. That is the whole lesson — and it is worth more than the sale you save by cutting.

LESSON 01 · PRODUCT & CUSTOMERS · 7 MIN READ

Why should users choose your product?

Your product does not compete only with similar products. It competes with whatever people already do to solve the problem — including doing nothing.

1. Different is not the same as better

When founders ask what makes their product different, they often list features. But customers are not automatically interested in what is new. They care whether the product helps them make progress on something that matters.

A useful question is not just “What is different about this?” It is “For whom does that difference make a meaningful improvement?”

2. Your real competitors are the alternatives

Look beyond companies that offer a similar product. A customer may currently use a spreadsheet, WhatsApp, a notebook, a friend, a workaround, or no solution at all.

  • What does the customer do today?
  • What is frustrating, expensive, slow, risky, or difficult about that approach?
  • Why have they continued using it?
  • What would make changing worth the effort?

3. Find the gap that matters

Do not try to be better at everything. Find a specific situation where a specific group of people has a problem the available alternatives do not solve well enough.

Your strongest differentiation may be speed, trust, simplicity, local relevance, access, reliability, price, or a better result. The label matters less than the improvement customers can actually experience.

Try this sentenceFor [a specific kind of customer] who struggles with [specific situation], this helps them achieve [valuable outcome] better than [their current alternative] because [credible reason].

4. Give people a reason to switch

Even if your product is better, adopting it costs attention, time, and sometimes money. People may need to learn a new workflow, move their data, convince a team, or take a risk on an unfamiliar product.

Make the improvement clear enough to justify that effort. Reduce the cost of trying it. Where possible, let people experience the value before asking for a big commitment.

5. Features are evidence, not the whole argument

A feature matters when it creates an outcome a customer values. Translate features into consequences:

  • Not just “automated reminders,” but fewer missed follow-ups.
  • Not just “one dashboard,” but less time spent switching between tools.
  • Not just “AI-powered,” but a specific task completed faster or more reliably — if you can demonstrate it.

6. Validate the reason people choose you

Do not rely only on your own explanation of why the product is valuable. Speak to people who fit the intended audience. Ask how they currently handle the problem, what they have tried, and what would make them consider an alternative.

Then observe behavior. Will they try it? Return to it? Recommend it? Pay for it, when payment is relevant? Compliments can be encouraging, but behavior is stronger evidence.

7. Differentiation needs distribution

A product can be genuinely useful and still struggle if the right people never hear about it. Decide where your intended customers already spend time, whose recommendations they trust, and what proof would make them pay attention.

Positioning explains why someone should care. Distribution helps that person discover you. You need both.

Your exercise

  1. Name one specific group of customers.
  2. Write down the three most likely alternatives they use today.
  3. Identify the biggest unresolved frustration in that situation.
  4. State the outcome your product improves and why you can credibly deliver it.
  5. Talk to five people in that group. Test your assumptions before adding more features.
Reality checkIf the answer is “everyone,” “more features,” or “we use AI,” keep investigating. You have not yet identified a strong reason for a specific customer to choose you.

The goal is not to be different from everyone. It is to matter to someone — clearly enough that choosing you makes sense.

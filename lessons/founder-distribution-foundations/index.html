“

Distribution should not be a founder's mystery. It is the work of understanding who needs a product, where those people can be reached, and what earns enough trust for them to act.

01 — THE MISSION

Distribution should not be a founder's mystery.

Founders are often told to post on LinkedIn, run ads, write emails, or go viral. Those are tactics, not a strategy. They start with a channel instead of the customer.

A useful distribution system helps answer five questions: Who is most likely to need this now? Where can I reach them? What earns their attention and trust? What is the best next step toward trying or paying? What can I learn from each attempt?

✳
The goal is not virality

The goal is a reliable, evidence-based way to reach relevant customers. Not every product will spread widely, and attention alone does not establish value.

02 — PRODUCT → CUSTOMER

Start with the problem, not the platform.

Before recommending any route, establish what the product does and for whom. Identify the painful problem, the person who experiences it, the person who pays, and the event that makes solving it urgent.

PRODUCT AND CUSTOMER CHECK

  • What specific problem does the product solve?
  • Who feels this problem most acutely?
  • Who uses the product, and who decides or pays?
  • What do they do today instead?
  • Why might they look for a solution now?
  • What evidence supports these answers—and what is still an assumption?

A healthcare tool for clinics, an education product for parents, and software for small retailers may all need different trust signals, sales cycles, and routes to reach buyers. Generic advice to “use social media” ignores those differences.

03 — AUDIENCE INTELLIGENCE

Find where the people already are.

Map how the intended customers already learn, ask for help, compare solutions, and make decisions. Look for real behaviour rather than inventing a persona from demographics alone.

01Places

Relevant communities, professional groups, marketplaces, search results, trade associations, events, and offline gathering points.

02People

Trusted practitioners, peers, community leaders, existing customers, advisers, distributors, and referral sources.

03Signals

Questions asked repeatedly, workarounds, buying triggers, active requests for recommendations, and current spending.

04Access

Whether the founder can reach the audience respectfully, affordably, and with permission where required.

For each possible audience, note the source, date checked, evidence of relevance, and what remains unverified. A list of names is not useful if the people are not qualified prospects.

04 — ROUTE SELECTION

Choose the route that fits the buying situation.

Consider multiple route families, but do not assume every one is appropriate or available.

FOUR ROUTE FAMILIES

  • Relationship distribution: referrals, trusted individuals, word of mouth, professional associations, and existing communities.
  • Digital distribution: search, WhatsApp, email, social platforms, niche online groups, and relevant marketplaces.
  • Embedded distribution: partnerships with organisations, businesses, institutions, or platforms that already serve the audience.
  • Direct distribution: targeted outreach, demonstrations, field sales, and conversations with prospective customers.

Assess each candidate route by customer concentration, access, trust, intent, cost and effort, feedback speed, and potential repeatability. These are decision prompts, not universal weights. Learn which factors matter through actual experiments.

↗
Nigeria is a starting context, not a limitation

Distribution may be digital, relationship-led, institutional, or physical. Design for local realities first, then adapt the method to other markets instead of assuming one global channel mix.

05 — FROM INSIGHT TO ACTION

The first output should be a prioritized plan.

The first version does not need to be a content generator, social scheduler, ad dashboard, or oversized CRM. It should take a founder from product → audience → routes → prioritized plan, with evidence attached to each recommendation.

Product·Audience·Routes·Plan

Every recommendation should explain why it fits this product and buyer, what it costs in time or money, how to attempt it, and what result would help the founder decide whether to continue. Advice that leaves execution entirely to the founder is incomplete.

Keep the first operating system simple: founder pipeline, interview notes, customer hypotheses, prospect sources, experiment log, and service time/cost. Automate only after repeated work proves valuable.

PUT IT TO WORK

Write your first distribution hypothesis.

Before choosing a channel, write down the buyer, the problem, why it matters now, and where you can realistically reach them.

  1. Name one specific group that experiences the problem.
  2. Describe what they do today instead.
  3. List two places or relationships that could help you reach them.
  4. State what you still need to verify.
! Reality check

One reply or sale is useful evidence, not proof of a repeatable channel. Keep sample size, buying cycle, product quality, and the limits of your data in view.

THE TAKEAWAY

Start with the customer and work backwards. The right route depends on the product, buyer, trust required, founder resources, and evidence.

Open your support inbox. Read five messages. One customer cannot log in. Another cannot find the button to add a second child. A third says the app is slow on their phone. Your first feeling is probably dread — more problems, more work, more unhappy people. But look again. Those five messages are the cheapest research you will ever get. Your customers are telling you, in their own words, what hurts — and what to build next. This lesson shows you how to read complaints as evidence, sort them into three kinds, and turn the count into your next build.

01 — THE COMPLAINT IS EVIDENCE

A complaint is research you did not pay for.

Founders pay for research. They run interviews, send surveys, and hire testers. All of that is useful. But the inbox is research that arrives on its own, from people who already paid you. A customer who writes to complain has done three things a survey respondent has not: they used the product, they hit a real problem, and they cared enough to tell you. That is a lot of information, free.

Most founders waste it. They treat each complaint as a fire to put out: answer it, apologise, move on. The complaint gets closed. The lesson inside it gets thrown away with it. A week later, another customer writes about the same thing, and the founder is surprised. Again.

The mistake is reading complaints one at a time. One complaint is a story. It might be one person's bad day, a phone with a weak connection, or a misunderstanding. Three complaints about the same thing are a pattern. Ten complaints are a roadmap item. The number is the signal. A single complaint tells you almost nothing about what to build. A counted pile of them tells you almost everything.

So the first change is a change of mind. A complaint is not an attack on your product. It is a customer doing your research for you, in plain words, about a real moment of pain. The founder who reads the inbox this way stops dreading it — and starts mining it.

The shift

Do not ask “how do I close this complaint?” Ask “how many other customers hit the same thing?” One complaint is a story. Three about the same thing are a roadmap item.

02 — THREE KINDS OF COMPLAINT

Sort every complaint into one of three piles.

Not all complaints are the same kind of problem, and each kind needs a different fix. Before you count anything, sort each complaint into one of three piles: a bug, a confusion, or a missing thing.

First, a bug: something is broken. The app logs the customer out. The payment goes through twice. The page will not load on a small phone. A bug is a fact, not an opinion. It either happens or it does not. Bugs are the easiest pile to act on: reproduce it, fix it, tell the customer it is fixed. A bug you leave unfixed is a customer you are slowly losing.

Second, a confusion: the product works, but the customer cannot see how. “I cannot find where to add a second child.” “I did not know I had to confirm by email.” “I thought the price was per month, not per year.” Nothing is broken. The customer is lost. Confusion is usually cheaper to fix than a bug — clearer words, a better label, one step fewer — but it is easy to miss, because the founder already knows how the product works. To you, the button is obvious. To a new customer, it is invisible. Confusion is the most common pile in a young product, and the most ignored.

Third, a missing thing: the product works, the customer understands it, and something they need is not there. “Can I pay in two parts?” “Can I export the list?” “Can I add my assistant?” A missing thing is a real request for a build. It is also the pile that most needs counting — one customer asking for an export is a wish; eight customers asking for it is a feature with a waiting list.

Sorting matters because the piles are fixed differently. A bug is fixed in code. A confusion is fixed in words and design. A missing thing is fixed by building — or by deciding, honestly, not to build it. If you do not sort, you treat all three the same way: you answer the message and change nothing. Sorting is what turns a pile of messages into a pile of decisions.

03 — THE COMPLAINT LOG

Count, don't react.

The tool is a simple log. One page, or one spreadsheet. Four columns: the date, what the customer said (in their own words, one line), which pile it belongs in, and how many times this same thing has come up. That last column is the whole point.

Here is the weekly routine. Once a week — the same day, the same time — read every complaint from the past seven days. Sort each one into a pile. Add a mark to the count for that problem. Then look at the counts, not the messages. The question is not “what was the angriest complaint?” The question is “what came up three or more times?”

Three is the rule of thumb. One complaint can be a one-off. Two can be a coincidence. Three customers hitting the same wall in one week means the wall is real, and it is in the way of people who are trying to pay you. Fix that one first — before the clever feature, before the redesign, before the thing you personally want to build. The log tells you what to build. Your taste tells you what you want to build. When the two disagree, trust the log.

Two habits make the log work. First, keep the customer's own words. “It keeps logging me out” is better than “login issue”, because the words show you what the customer actually experienced. Second, count the problem, not the person. The same customer writing three times about the same bug is one problem, not three. The count is the number of different customers, not the number of messages.

A log like this takes about 30 minutes a week for a small team. It replaces the most expensive mistake in early product work: building what nobody asked for, while the thing three customers named stays broken.

04 — WHAT NOT TO DO

Four ways founders waste the inbox.

First, fixing the loudest complaint. The angriest customer writes the longest message. It is tempting to fix their problem first, to make the noise stop. But loud is not the same as common. One furious customer with an unusual problem is one customer. Three quiet customers with the same problem are three. Fix the counted problem, not the loudest voice.

Second, apologising and changing nothing. A kind reply is good manners, not a fix. If five customers hit the same confusion and the reply is “sorry, here is how it works” five times, the product is still confusing the sixth customer. The reply is a bandage. The fix is changing the product so the next customer does not need to ask.

Third, treating every complaint as a feature request. A customer who says “I want an export button” is describing a pain, not prescribing the cure. Maybe the real fix is a weekly summary email, not a button. Maybe the export already exists and the customer could not find it — which is a confusion, not a missing thing. Ask what the customer is trying to do, not what button they want. Build for the job, not the request.

Fourth, arguing with the customer. “It works on my phone.” “You must have done it wrong.” Even if true, it teaches the customer to stop telling you things. And the customer who stops complaining does not stop being unhappy — they stop being a customer. Our companion lesson on the quiet customer covers that silence. A complaint is a gift. Arguing with it is returning the gift unopened.

05 — TWO WORKED CASES

The same inbox, read two ways.

Both cases below are illustrative — made-up stories built to show the log in use, not reports of real companies. The numbers are small and simple for the same reason.

First, the food-delivery app. A founder runs deliveries for restaurants. In one week, the inbox has nine complaints. Three say “the app logs me out before I finish an order.” Two say “I cannot find where to change my address.” Two say “the food arrived cold.” One asks for cash on delivery. One says the app is slow. The founder's first instinct is to answer each one and move on. Instead, she sorts. The logout is a bug — three customers, same wall. The address is a confusion — two customers, same lost button. Cold food is a different kind of problem — a kitchen and rider problem, not an app problem — and she sends it to the operations side. Cash on delivery is a missing thing with a count of one: noted, not built. Slow app, count of one: noted. She fixes the logout bug first, then relabels the address button. Two weeks later, the inbox is quieter — and she knows why, because she can see the counts fall.

Second, the school-fees tool. A founder sells a tool that helps small schools collect fees. In a month, four schools write: “I cannot add a second child from the same family.” Each school phrases it differently — one calls it a bug, one asks for a new feature, one just says “it does not work for twins.” Sorted, they are one missing thing with a count of four. It goes to the top of the build list, ahead of the dashboard redesign the founder wanted to do. When it ships, she writes to all four schools. Two of them reply that they have added siblings the same day. The other two had already worked around it — but they noticed she remembered. The count told her what to build. The reply told the customers they were heard.

Notice what both founders did. Neither fixed the loudest complaint first. Neither built the thing one customer asked for. Both counted before they decided — and both told the customers what changed. That last step matters: a customer who complained and then sees the fix becomes a customer who trusts you. They also become the kind of customer who complains again next time — which is exactly what you want.

06 — WHAT COULD CHANGE THE ANSWER

Five limits on everything above.

First, complaints come from people who stayed. The customers who write are the ones who still care. The ones who left quietly never wrote — and their problems are invisible in the inbox. The log shows you what is hurting the customers you still have. It does not show you why the others left. Read the companion lesson on the quiet customer for the other half of the picture.

Second, complainers are not a random sample. The customers who write tend to be the most confident, the most online, or the most frustrated. A quiet majority may have the same problem and never say so. Three complaints can mean thirty customers hit the same wall. Treat the count as a minimum, not a total.

Third, some complaints are about price, not product. “Too expensive” is a pricing question, and counting it in the product log will not help. It belongs in the pricing conversation — our companion lessons on pricing set the price, and the discount lesson covers the negotiation. Keep the log for product complaints.

Fourth, a complaint can be a support problem, not a product problem. If customers keep asking the same question, the answer may be a help page or a better reply — not a code change. Before you build, ask: would a clearer answer, in the right place, have stopped this complaint? Sometimes the fix is a sentence, not a feature.

Fifth, the log is a guide, not a master. Three customers asking for the same thing is strong evidence. It is not a command. If the request would pull the product away from the customers it serves best, or if it only helps a segment you do not want, you may still say no — but say it as a decision, with the count in front of you, not as a reflex.

07 — THE NEXT STEP

Read this week's complaints. Fix the one three people named.

  1. Open your support inbox, WhatsApp, or email — wherever customers reach you. Read every complaint from the past seven days.
  2. Start the log: one line per complaint, in the customer's own words, sorted into bug, confusion, or missing thing. Count how many different customers named each problem.
  3. Pick the problem three or more customers named. If none reached three, pick the highest count.
  4. Fix it this week — in code if it is a bug, in words and design if it is a confusion, by building or by an honest no if it is a missing thing.
  5. Write back to every customer who named it. Tell them what changed. One sentence is enough.

Do this every week, on the same day. After a month, you will have four fixes that customers actually asked for — and a list, in their own words, of what to build next. That list is your roadmap. It was in your inbox all along.

THE TAKEAWAYComplaints are the cheapest research you will ever get. Count them before you answer them.

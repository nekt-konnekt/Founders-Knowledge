On 3 November 2026, Nedbank completed its purchase of two-thirds of NCBA, the Kenyan bank behind LOOP, a digital banking app. The name on the door has not changed. The app still works. But the person who ran LOOP left for a rival bank in October, before the deal even closed. And in Nigeria, customers of a small card company called Allawee have until 30 November to move their money — because the company that quietly bought Allawee is shutting it down on 1 December. Three different deals, one lesson for founders: when the company behind your rails changes hands, the promises you built on can change with it. The time to ask what changes is now, while the answers are still free.

01 — THE DEAL CLOSED

The logo did not change. Everything else can.

Start with what happened. Nedbank, a South African bank, bought 66% of NCBA Group, a Kenyan bank. The deal closed on 3 November 2026, through a block trade — one large, pre-arranged sale of shares — on the Nairobi Securities Exchange, Kenya's stock market. The final results were published on 5 November through the Johannesburg Stock Exchange's announcement service: shareholders offered 1,316,022,844 shares, or 79.88% of the company, and Nedbank took only the 66% it wanted. Nedbank paid with 43,631,052 of its own new shares plus KES 23.2 billion in cash — KES is the Kenyan shilling. The Central Bank of Kenya approved the deal on 28 August 2026. NCBA stays listed on the Nairobi exchange, with about a third of its shares still in public hands.

LOOP is NCBA's digital banking app — the product a founder building on NCBA rails would actually touch. Its chief executive, Eric Muriuki, ran LOOP since October 2019 and spent 19 years at the bank. In October 2026 he left to join Absa, a rival banking group, as Managing Executive for Pan-African Growth Strategy, Platforms and Digital Ventures. Absa confirmed the appointment on 7 October. No successor for LOOP has been named.

Read those two facts together. The deal is done, the logo is the same, and the person who ran the product you build on is already gone. That is not a disaster. It is a signal. The changes a deal brings start with people, and they start early — sometimes before the deal closes.

The sharper case is Allawee. Paystack — the Nigerian payments company owned by Stripe — quietly bought Allawee, a small card-issuing startup, in 2025. Neither company announced the purchase. Then, in August and September 2026, Allawee told its customers that all services close on 1 December 2026: cards stop working, payments to Allawee account numbers fail, and customers must withdraw their money by 30 November. Account numbers, history and verification records do not carry over on their own. Businesses are being pointed to Paystack's microfinance bank; individual customers to Zap, Paystack's consumer app.

Two deals, two different endings. In one, the company stays and the people move. In the other, the company itself closes. A founder cannot tell which one they are in from the press release. That is why the next section matters.

✳
The mistake

Treating a deal announcement as the end of the story. The announcement tells you who owns the company now. It tells you nothing about who will answer your call in six months.

02 — WHAT AN ACQUISITION ACTUALLY CHANGES

Five things move when the owner changes.

Most founders picture an acquisition as a logo swap: same company, new owner, business as usual. It is not. A buyer buys a company for their own reasons, and those reasons decide what happens next. Five things move.

1. The people. A deal buys the team as much as the platform. Key people often leave — sometimes before the deal closes, as Muriuki did. The person who approved your deal, who answers your calls, who knows your account may not be there in six months. A partnership is a relationship with people. When the people go, the relationship restarts from zero.

2. The priorities. The buyer bought the company for a market, a licence, a customer base or a team — not necessarily for your use case. What the buyer wants to grow gets money and attention. What they did not buy it for gets starved, sold or shut. Allawee's consumer accounts were not what Paystack bought it for. They close on 1 December.

3. The product roadmap. The acquired product stops being a neutral rail and becomes a feature of the buyer's platform. Xero, the accounting software company, bought Melio, a US bill-payments company, for $2.5 billion in October 2025. Within months, bill payments built on Melio appeared inside Xero for US customers — and Xero made standard bill pay free while raising subscription prices. Your integration can change shape without anyone asking you.

4. The pricing and terms. A contract usually survives a change of owner — the law keeps it in force — but renewal terms are new terms: price, minimums, support tier. The buyer sets them. Brex, the US startup-spending platform, shows both sides. Capital One closed its $5.15 billion purchase of Brex in April 2026, and Brex said in writing that its free tier stays free and its rewards stay unchanged. As of September 2026, that has held. But the same company, in June 2022, gave tens of thousands of small businesses about two months to leave when it changed who it serves. Promises hold until they do not. Written ones hold longer.

5. Support and access. Support teams merge. Account managers change. The direct line you had becomes a ticket queue. Response time is the first thing a founder feels after a deal, and the last thing a deal announcement mentions.

None of these five is a reason to panic. All five are reasons to ask. The founder who asks early gets answers while the old team is still there to give them.

03 — THE CONTINUITY CHECKLIST

Seven questions. Get the answers in writing.

Run this on every partner that carries your revenue, your customers' money, or your product's core function. Your bank, your payment provider, your platform, your cloud — anyone you cannot replace in a week. Send the questions by email. A call is a memory. An email is evidence.

  • 1. Who is my contact now — name and role — and who replaces them if they leave? A named person, not a department. If the answer is a support inbox, your relationship has already changed.
  • 2. Who stays for the next 12 months — the people who approved and run my deal, by name? The person who sold you the partnership and the person who runs it day to day are often two different people. You need both names.
  • 3. What changes on my contract — price, terms, minimums, support tier — and when is my renewal? Renewal is when the new owner's terms arrive. Know the date before it arrives.
  • 4. What happens to the product I use — does it stay, merge into another product, or close? What is the written timeline? “Stay” is an answer. “Merge” is an answer. “We don't know yet” is also an answer — write it down, with a date to ask again.
  • 5. What happens to my data and my customers' data — account numbers, history, verification records — do they move, stay, or close? Allawee's customers learned this one late: account numbers do not carry over on their own. Ask before the deadline, not after it.
  • 6. Who do I call when it breaks — and what is the response time, in writing? Support is a promise. Get the number of hours in an email, not in a sales call.
  • 7. What is my exit — can I export my data, my customer records and my history, and how long does it take? If you cannot leave, you cannot negotiate. Test the export before you need it.

One more check, before you send the email: read your contract's change-of-control clause — the paragraph that says what happens when the owner changes. If you do not have a copy of the contract, ask for it. That clause may already answer half the list.

◈
The rule

If an answer is “we don't know yet”, write that down with a date to ask again. “We don't know” is an honest answer. Silence is not.

There is a third case worth watching, because it is still live. Dropbox signed a deal on 3 November 2026 to buy Tigris, a storage company, for about $350 million. The deal has not closed, and Dropbox has said nothing about whether Tigris's no-egress-fees pricing — no charge for moving your data out — or its free tier survive. Customers who build on Tigris have to plan before the close, not after. And Dropbox has shut down a purchase before: it bought Mailbox in 2013 and closed it in February 2016.

05 — WHAT COULD CHANGE THE ANSWER

Five limits on everything above.

First, a purchase is not a shutdown. Most acquired products keep running. The risk is change, not disappearance — price, people, roadmap. Do not panic-move off a partner because a deal closed. Ask first, then decide.

Second, some of this is reported, not confirmed. The two Nedbank board appointments at NCBA are reported by one Kenyan business publication and have not been verified in a filing. Neither Nedbank nor NCBA has said anything in public about LOOP's future, or about M-Shwari and Fuliza — NCBA's savings and loan products that run on M-PESA, Kenya's mobile money service. Do not treat silence as a plan.

Third, promises in writing can hold. Brex's terms held for the first five months after Capital One closed. The lesson is to get the promise in writing, not to assume the worst.

Fourth, your contract may already answer some of this. Read the change-of-control and assignment clauses before you ask — the answers may be in the document you signed.

Fifth, this lesson is not legal advice. If a partner carries your revenue, a lawyer reads the clauses. The checklist finds the questions. A lawyer answers the ones that carry a penalty.

06 — THE NEXT STEP

Ask who stays. This week.

  • List your three biggest partners — the bank, the payment provider, the platform you cannot replace in a week.
  • Send each one the seven questions by email. File the answers where the team can find them.
  • If you or your customers use Allawee: move by 30 November 2026. From 1 December, cards stop working and payments to Allawee account numbers fail.
  • If you build on LOOP or NCBA rails: ask who runs LOOP now and what changes in the next 12 months — before you plan a launch on it.
  • Set a rule: every time a partner announces a deal, run the checklist within a week. Deals are announced in press releases. Their effects arrive in renewal letters.

Deals close in a day. Their effects take a year to arrive. The founder who asks in the first week gets answers from the people who still know them.

THE TAKEAWAY

Your partnership is with people, not a logo. Ask who stays — and get it in writing.

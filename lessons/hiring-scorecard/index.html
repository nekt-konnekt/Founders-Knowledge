The Hiring Scorecard: Assessing Delivery, Adaptability and Track Record

A good interview is not a good hire. Five criteria — delivery, adaptability, work quality, track record, service length — scored on evidence before the offer. That is the whole method.

Score the evidence, not the interview.

In late October 2026, Moniepoint — a Nigerian payments company that builds tools for businesses — restructured its engineering and product teams. TechCabal, a technology publication, reported that staff would be scored against written criteria: delivery and adaptability, work quality, track record, and length of service. The report cited employees with direct knowledge; the company itself said the change was a restructure for growth, not a layoff. The five criteria are worth borrowing. A founder with five people cannot run a human-resources department, but a founder with five people also cannot afford a wrong hire. One bad hire in a team of five is a fifth of your company. The scorecard in this lesson is those five criteria, adapted for a small team: written down before you hire, scored on evidence, not on how the interview felt.

01 — THE GUT-FEEL TRAP

An interview tests how someone interviews. Nothing more.

Most small-team hiring works like this. A founder reads a CV, meets someone for an hour, and decides by feel: they seemed sharp, they spoke well, they have worked at a name you recognise. The offer follows. Then the first hard month arrives — the deadline slips, the customer complains, the plan changes — and the person you hired is not the person you interviewed. That is not bad luck. It is the method failing. An interview measures confidence, fluency and preparation. Those are real qualities, but they are not the job. The job is finishing work that works, changing course when the plan changes, and doing it again next month.

The cost of getting it wrong is not the salary. It is the months. A wrong hire in a small team does the work slowly, or does it badly and someone else quietly fixes it, or leaves after four months and takes the knowledge of your customers with them. Then you hire again, from the same place, with the same method. African tech companies cut a record 2,574 jobs in the first half of 2026, according to TechCabal Insights figures reported by Businessday — up 236% on the same period a year before. Some of those cuts were restructuring and some were cost. But every cut is also a hiring decision being undone. The cheaper move is to hire slowly and well, once.

The gut-feel trap has a second half: it also rejects good people. The quiet candidate who ships solid work but does not perform in interviews loses to the confident candidate who talks well and delivers slowly. A scorecard fixes both directions. It forces you to write down what matters before you meet anyone, and to score what you can see, not what you felt.

✳
The mistake

Hiring on how the interview felt. The interview is a performance. The scorecard asks for evidence of the work instead.

02 — THE FIVE CRITERIA, ONE BY ONE

Five things that predict whether someone delivers when work gets hard.

Each criterion below has three parts: what it means, what good evidence looks like, and what it does not mean. The evidence is the point. A criterion without evidence is just an opinion with a label on it.

Delivery. Does the person finish work that works, on time, without being chased? This is the first criterion because it is the one a small team feels first. A developer who ships a working feature late is a problem; a developer who ships a broken feature on time is a bigger one. Good evidence: work you can see — a product, a feature, a report — delivered by a date the person agreed to. Ask what they shipped, when, and what happened when the date slipped. “I delivered the payment page two days late because the bank changed its rules, and I told the team on day one” is strong evidence. “I worked on payments” is not. Delivery does not mean saying yes to every deadline. It means agreeing to a date honestly and then meeting it, or warning early when it moves.

Adaptability. Does the person change when the work changes? Small companies change plans constantly: a customer leaves, a partner changes its rules, a tool breaks. The person who can only do the job as first described becomes a bottleneck the first time the description changes. Good evidence: a time they learned a new tool, switched to a different kind of work, or handled a plan that changed mid-way — and can say what they did differently, not just that they coped. Adaptability does not mean agreeing to everything. A person who says yes to every change and finishes none of them is not adaptable. They are agreeable. The evidence is a change they handled and what came out of it.

Work quality. Does the work hold up? For a developer, that means code that rarely breaks and that another person can read and fix. For a designer, work a customer can use without confusion. For an operations hire, records that are accurate and a process that does not depend on one person's memory. Good evidence: a sample of real work, checked by someone who understands it — or a walk-through where the candidate explains how they check their own work before handing it over. Ask: “How do you know your work is right before anyone else looks at it?” A person with a real answer — a checklist, a test, a second pair of eyes — is showing you quality. Work quality does not mean perfection. It means the work survives contact with real users and the next person who touches it.

Track record. What has this person actually done, over time, in context? A job title is not a track record. “Head of Engineering” at a ten-person startup and the same title at a bank describe different jobs. Good evidence: outcomes with their context attached — what the person was responsible for, what the result was, and what else was happening at the time. A reference call is the strongest check here: a former manager who can say what the person owned and what happened. Ask the reference what the person did that nobody else on the team could do, and what the team had to work around. Vague praise from a reference is weak evidence. Specific stories are strong. Track record does not mean a famous employer. It means a pattern of real responsibility and real results, wherever it happened.

Length of service. How long did they stay, and what did they build while they were there? Staying somewhere matters because real work takes time: the first months are learning, the value comes later. A person who stays two years and grows — more responsibility, harder work, a system they built — is showing you something different from a person who stayed two years and did the same task. Good evidence: tenure with growth attached to it. Short stays are not automatic rejection. A startup that shut down, a contract that ended, a team that was cut — all are honest reasons, and the current market has many of them. The question is whether the person can explain the move plainly, and whether the pattern across their whole history shows staying power or a habit of leaving at the first hard month. Length of service does not mean loyalty as a feeling. It means evidence that the person stays long enough for their work to matter.

03 — BUILD THE SCORECARD

One page. Five rows. Evidence before the score.

The scorecard is a table you fill in for each candidate, before you make the offer. Five rows, one per criterion. Three columns per row: the criterion, the evidence you actually saw, and the score. The score is simple: 1 means no evidence or weak evidence, 2 means some evidence, 3 means strong evidence. Write the evidence first, then score it. If you score first and find the evidence later, you are just writing down your gut feeling with numbers on it.

The scorecard works only if you follow four rules.

Rule one: minimums beat totals. Add the five scores and the total runs from 5 to 15. But never hire on the total alone. A candidate can score 13 out of 15 and still be a bad hire if the 2 is on delivery. Set minimums: no hire with a 1 on delivery or work quality, whatever the total says. The total tells you how strong the candidate is overall. The minimums tell you whether they can do the job at all.

Rule two: two scorers, not one. Moniepoint's reported process has engineering leaders scoring staff and the human-resources team reviewing scores across teams. Your version is simpler: two people score each candidate independently, then compare. If the two scores differ by more than one point on any criterion, that is the most useful conversation you will have about the hire — it means one of you saw something the other missed. A founder hiring alone should still get a second pair of eyes: a co-founder, an adviser, or a senior team member who will work with the hire.

Rule three: score the same things for every candidate. The moment you add a criterion for one person and drop it for another, the scorecard is decoration. Write the five criteria down before the first interview, and use the same sheet for everyone. If a criterion turns out to be wrong for the role — length of service matters less for a three-month contract, for example — change it for all candidates, and note why.

Rule four: the score is a guide, not a verdict. A scorecard cannot measure honesty, motivation, or whether someone will fit how your team actually works. It also cannot see the future: a candidate with strong evidence on four criteria and a thin track record because they are early in their career may still be the right hire. The scorecard's job is to make the judgement visible — what you know, what you do not know, and what you are betting on — so the decision is deliberate instead of accidental.

✳
The scorecard, in short

Five criteria. Evidence written before the score. 1 to 3 on each. No hire with a 1 on delivery or work quality. Two scorers. Same sheet for every candidate.

04 — TWO CANDIDATES, SIDE BY SIDE

What the scorecard catches that an interview misses.

Both candidates below are hypothetical — made up to show the method working, not real people. You are hiring a developer for a small Lagos fintech. The job: keep the payments dashboard working, ship one small feature a month, and answer when a customer reports a failed payment.

Candidate A interviews brilliantly. The CV lists a well-known bank and a big startup. They speak confidently about architecture and scale. On the scorecard: delivery scores 1 — they cannot name a feature they shipped on a date they agreed, and the bank work was a team of forty where their part is unclear. Adaptability scores 2 — they moved teams once, but cannot say what they did differently. Work quality scores 2 — they talk about testing but show no sample. Track record scores 2 — impressive employers, thin personal responsibility. Length of service scores 2 — two years at the bank, one year at the startup, both explained. Total: 9. The interview said hire. The scorecard says the evidence is thin where it matters most.

Candidate B interviews quietly. The CV lists two small companies you have not heard of. They are slow to answer and do not use impressive words. On the scorecard: delivery scores 3 — they show a dashboard feature they shipped, on the date they promised, and explain the one time they slipped and warned the team early. Adaptability scores 3 — when their first employer lost its biggest client, they learned the billing system and rebuilt the invoicing flow in three weeks. Work quality scores 2 — their sample is solid but they have no formal testing habit; they check their work by running it against real customer data before release. Track record scores 3 — a former manager says they owned the dashboard end to end and were the person the team called when a payment failed. Length of service scores 2 — two years at the first company, eighteen months at the second, which shut down. Total: 13. The interview said maybe. The scorecard says hire.

Notice what happened. The interview rewarded the person who performs well in interviews. The scorecard rewarded the person with evidence of doing the actual job. That is the whole point: the job is not the interview. The score is not perfect — Candidate B's testing habit is a real gap, and the scorecard shows it plainly, so you can plan for it: pair them with a reviewer for the first month. A gut-feel hire hides the gap. A scored hire names it.

05 — WHAT COULD CHANGE THE ANSWER

Five limits on everything above.

First, the five criteria are reported, not announced. TechCabal reported them citing employees with direct knowledge. Moniepoint's on-record statement covered only the restructure itself — moving to small platform groups that own what they build end to end — and did not confirm the criteria. Treat the five as a reported practice from a large company, not as a method Moniepoint has published. The scorecard in this lesson is the desk's adaptation for a small team, not Moniepoint's system.

Second, no weighting was reported. Nothing in the reporting says whether delivery counts more than length of service, or whether the five are scored at all. The 1-to-3 scale and the minimums rule are the desk's own method. They are a starting point, not a proven formula. If you use the scorecard for a year and find that adaptability predicts success better than track record in your team, change the weights — and write down why.

Third, the scorecard cannot see character. It measures what a person has done, not whether they are honest, kind, or steady under pressure. A reference call helps, but only if you ask the hard questions — what the team had to work around, not just what the person did well. In markets where formal references are thin, back-channel checks matter more; our lesson on reference calls in informal markets covers how to do them without a paper trail to follow.

Fourth, small teams cannot copy the large-company version. Moniepoint's reported process has leaders scoring and a human-resources team reviewing across teams. A five-person startup has no such team. The adaptation is two scorers and a written sheet — that is the whole of it. Do not build a hiring committee. Build a sheet, use it twice, and fix what does not work.

Fifth, the market context is moving. The 2,574 job cuts in the first half of 2026 are tracker figures from TechCabal Insights, reported by Businessday — the count depends on what the tracker counts, and the drivers cited were AI adoption, banking mergers and cost-cutting. More experienced people are looking for work than a year ago. That is a chance to hire well, and a warning: a candidate with a short stay may have been cut, not fired. Ask what happened before you judge the gap.

06 — THE NEXT STEP

Score one hire, this week.

  • Write the five criteria on one page, with the evidence column and the 1-to-3 scale. Keep the minimums rule on the page: no 1 on delivery or work quality.
  • Before your next hire, score them on the sheet before the offer — evidence first, then the number. Get a second person to score independently.
  • This week, score one person already on your team, using the same five criteria. It calibrates the scale: if everyone on your team scores 3 on everything, your scale is too easy. If your best person scores 1 on adaptability, you have learned something about your team, not just your hiring.
  • When a candidate scores below your minimums, say no — and write down why in one sentence. The sentence is for you, six months from now, when you wonder whether you were too strict.

One scored hire teaches the habit. The habit is what stops the next wrong hire.

THE TAKEAWAY

The interview is a performance. Hire the evidence, not the interview.

Where Does Your Startup's Data Actually Live?

On 1 January 2027, a rule takes effect that most founders have heard about and almost none have acted on. Payment data generated in Nigeria must be stored and managed in Nigeria. That is seven weeks from today. The banks are scrambling: a survey in October found only 59% of financial institutions had finished, or expected to finish, in time — and the banks have compliance teams. A five-person startup has a founder with a laptop. This lesson is the afternoon's work that puts you ahead of the deadline: a map of where your data actually lives, and a checklist to act on it.

01 — THE DEADLINE IS REAL

Seven weeks is not a long time to move data.

The rule comes from the Central Bank of Nigeria — the CBN, the country's central bank and the regulator that licenses banks and payment companies. On 15 June 2026 it issued Circular PSS/DIR/PUB/CIR/001/004, signed by Dr Rakiya Yusuf, Director of the Payments System Supervision Department. The data clause is short enough to read in one breath:

“All Financial Institutions and participants facilitating payments within Nigeria shall ensure that payments transaction data generated within Nigeria are stored and managed in Nigeria in accordance with data protection laws and regulations applicable in Nigeria. Accordingly, all affected Financial Institutions shall fully comply with this requirement effective January 1, 2027.”

Two words in that sentence do most of the work. Stored and managed. It is not enough for a copy of the data to sit in Nigeria while the system that runs it sits abroad. The data and the running of it both have to be in the country. And the deadline is a hard date: full compliance from 1 January 2027.

The circular binds the companies the CBN licenses for payments: deposit money banks, microfinance banks, mobile money operators, switching and processing companies, payment terminal service providers, payment solution service providers, super agents, and other licensed operators. The same circular carries two more items that share your compliance team's attention: banks and payment firms must disclose the ultimate beneficial owners of significant shareholders, and a firm with more than 25% of card issuing may not hold more than 15% of merchant acquiring — processing card payments for businesses — and the reverse. Those market-share caps take effect a day earlier, on 31 December 2026.

There will be no extension. The CBN restated in early October that the date has not changed — 1 January 2027 — and told institutions still hosting payment data abroad to start migrating immediately. And the industry is behind: a survey run by NITDA and the CBN from 13 to 27 October 2026, presented at a regulatory dialogue in Lagos in late October, found that 26 of 44 financial institutions — 59% — had finished or expected to finish in time. The survey was self-reported, not a compliance assessment, so the true figure may be lower. The CBN has said it will guide firms first, but will sanction deliberate non-compliance.

Here is the part that matters to a founder who is not a bank. The rule may not bind you directly. But your payment provider is a licensed participant, and their deadline becomes your problem through contracts and onboarding questions. When your provider renews your agreement, or a bank asks where your customer data sits, “I don't know” is no longer a good answer. The map in this lesson is how you get a good one.

✳
The rule

If you touch payment data, find out where it sits this week. If you do not, find out anyway — your payment partner's deadline will reach you through their contract.

02 — THREE RULES, NOT ONE

The confusion is the risk.

Most founders hear “data localisation” and picture one law that says all data must stay in Nigeria. There is no such blanket law. There are three different rules, from three different regulators, covering three different kinds of data. A founder who mixes them up either over-prepares — moves everything, burns money — or under-prepares, and ignores the one deadline that binds their partner.

The ruleWhose dataWhat it requiresWho it binds
CBN circular, 15 June 2026Payment transaction data generated in NigeriaStored and managed in NigeriaLicensed banks and payment participants — 1 January 2027
Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 (NDPA), with the GAID 2025Personal data — names, phone numbers, addresses, anything that identifies a personMay leave Nigeria only if the recipient is bound by adequate protection, or a stated condition appliesEvery company that handles personal data — already in force
National Digital Cloud Policy, 17 August 2026Government and formally designated regulated data, sorted into four levelsThe most sensitive levels stay in Nigeria under sovereign controlGovernment data and designated regulated data — the sovereignty part still awaits presidential approval

Read the table from the top. The CBN rule answers one question: where must payment data sit? The NDPA answers a different one: under what safeguards may personal data travel? Personal data may leave Nigeria if the recipient is bound by a law, binding corporate rules, contractual clauses, a code of conduct or a certification that gives adequate protection — or if one of the conditions in Section 43 of the Act applies. The NDPA also sorts companies by how much personal data they handle: a company that processes the personal data of more than 200 people in six months counts as a “controller of major importance” and carries heavier duties. Most startups cross that line in their first months.

The Cloud Policy answers a third question: which government data must stay? It does not impose general localisation on commercial data. Its sovereignty rules cover defined government and regulated data, and they only take effect after presidential approval — which, as of this writing, has not been reported. The policy's headline numbers are investment targets, not money spent: $250 million in private investment in the first 12 months, $750 million in 24 months.

One more rule is worth knowing, even though it binds few startups directly: telecom subscriber information cannot leave Nigeria without the Nigerian Communications Commission's prior written consent. If your product holds subscriber data for a telco partner, that consent is your problem too.

The mechanism is simple once the three rules are apart. The CBN rule is about place. The NDPA is about safeguards. The Cloud Policy is about sovereignty. Your data map has to answer all three questions, one system at a time — and that is what the next section does.

03 — THE SIX PLACES DATA HIDES

Your data is not in one place. It never was.

Most founders picture their data in one place: the database. In practice it sits in at least six. If you only check the database, you will miss most of it.

  • 1. The app database and file storage. Where the product runs. Customer records, orders, messages, uploads. This is the one everyone remembers.
  • 2. Backups and disaster-recovery copies. A backup is storage too, and it counts. A database in Nigeria with its backup in another country is data that lives in two countries, and the CBN rule covers the full data lifecycle — from creation through processing and storage — not just the live copy.
  • 3. Analytics and error tracking. Every event, crash report and session recording. Error logs often carry phone numbers and email addresses inside them, copied without anyone deciding to copy them.
  • 4. The support desk, email and CRM. Every ticket, chat and customer record. A support tool that syncs to a server abroad has moved your customer data, even if your own database never left Nigeria.
  • 5. The payment provider. The card and transaction records sit with the licensed provider, not with you. What sits with you is the order record around the payment: the customer's name, phone number, address, what they bought, and how much. That is personal data under the NDPA, and your provider will ask you about it.
  • 6. The tools that copy data on their own. A spreadsheet sync, a reporting tool, an automation that mirrors your database to another service. Nobody decided to move the data. The tool did it, quietly, on a schedule.

Then there is the cloud itself. “We are on the cloud” usually means “our data is abroad” — unless you chose otherwise on purpose. None of the big global cloud providers runs a full region in Nigeria. Amazon Web Services has a Local Zone in Lagos, which runs some computing and storage in the city, but it is an extension of its Cape Town region, not a Nigerian region. Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud's nearest full regions are in Johannesburg and Cape Town. Local capacity does exist in Lagos — data centres and local cloud providers host in the country — so a move is possible. But it is a choice you have to make, not a default you get for free.

The test for each system is three facts: what data, where it sits, and who controls it. Country and provider, not just the brand name. If you cannot answer for a system, write it down as unknown. Unknown is honest. “Fine” is a guess.

The line

“We are on the cloud” is not an answer to where your data lives. The cloud has an address. Find it.

04 — THE CHECKLIST

One afternoon, ten checks.

Block two hours. Open a blank page. Work down the list in order — it starts with the payment flow, because that is where the deadline bites first.

  • 1. List every system that holds customer or payment data. Hosting, database, backups, analytics, support desk, email, CRM, payment provider, logs. If a system touches a customer record, it goes on the list. Expect six to ten. Fewer than five means you have missed one.
  • 2. For each system, find where it is stored — country and provider. Check the provider's status page, its data-processing terms, or the region setting in your dashboard. Do not guess from the brand name. A global brand can still store your data on another continent.
  • 3. Mark which data is which. Payment transaction data, personal data, or everything else. The CBN rule, the NDPA and the Cloud Policy each cover a different pile. A system can hold two piles at once — your order record is personal data and sits next to payment data.
  • 4. Read your contracts. Does your payment provider or bank pass the localisation duty to you? Read the data-processing clause, not the sales page. If the clause says you must keep data in Nigeria, that is a promise you have already made.
  • 5. Check the backups. Where do the backup and disaster-recovery copies live? A backup abroad is data abroad. If the live copy moves and the backup does not, the job is half done.
  • 6. Check the tools that copy data on their own. Analytics, error tracking, automations, spreadsheet syncs. Open each one and ask: what does it copy, and where does the copy go?
  • 7. Ask your hosting or cloud provider in writing where the data sits — and keep the answer. A written answer is evidence. A sales call is a memory. When a partner asks, you show the email, not your recollection.
  • 8. If payment data sits abroad and you are a licensed participant, get a migration plan with dates. Who moves it, to where, and by when — and a named person who owns it. “We will look into it” is not a plan. The deadline is 1 January, seven weeks away.
  • 9. If you are not licensed, ask your payment partner what they need from you. Put the answer in the contract file. Their renewal form is coming, and a one-page map answers it in five minutes.
  • 10. File the map where the team can find it, and set a date to redo it. Data moves every time you add a tool. Redo the map every quarter, and every time you add a system that touches customer data.

That is the whole audit. Ten checks, one page at the end: every system, what it holds, where it sits, and what has to move.

05 — APPLY IT

Run one real company through it.

Illustrative example. Tunde runs a four-person logistics startup in Lagos. His app takes orders and payments. He takes payments through a licensed payment provider, so the card data is not his to store — but the order records are: customer names, phone numbers, addresses, delivery notes, amounts. One afternoon, he maps six systems.

Read the table from the top. Four of his six systems sit outside Nigeria. None of that is illegal by itself — the NDPA allows transfers with safeguards, and the CBN rule does not bind him directly. But the picture is now clear, and the decisions are small. He moves the order database and its backups to a provider that hosts in Nigeria. He keeps analytics abroad, with the transfer safeguards written into the contract. He asks the support desk for its data-processing terms in writing. And he puts a quarterly date in the calendar to redo the map — because the next tool he adds will move data on its own, and he wants to catch it in the week it happens.

06 — WHAT COULD CHANGE THE ANSWER

Five limits on everything above.

First, the circular does not list which data counts. The CBN rule covers “payment transaction data” but does not spell out the categories. Industry analysis suggests the net is wide — transaction records, settlement and reconciliation data, switching logs, backups and disaster-recovery copies. If you are a licensed participant, that question is for your lawyer or the CBN's guidance, not this lesson.

Second, the rules are still moving. The Cloud Policy's sovereignty part still needs presidential approval. NITDA and the CBN are preparing wider localisation for government ministries, departments and agencies, with no date announced yet. The map is what makes you ready for the next move, whichever direction it comes from.

Third, the NDPA's transfer rules depend on decisions that are still being made. Whether a given country or contract clause counts as adequate protection is the Nigeria Data Protection Commission's call. Check the current position before you sign a new contract, not after.

Fourth, “not bound” is not “not affected”. If you are not a licensed payment participant, 1 January does not bind you directly. But your bank, your payment provider and your biggest customer may still ask where your data sits, and their deadline can become a condition of doing business with you. The map answers them either way.

Fifth, this lesson is not legal advice. Data rules carry fines and licence consequences. For a regulated decision — whether you are a licensed participant, what counts as payment transaction data, what your contract promises — talk to a lawyer who works with the CBN and the NDPC. The checklist finds the questions. A lawyer answers the ones that carry a penalty.

07 — THE NEXT STEP

Do the map this week.

  • Block two hours this week — one afternoon, not a project. Put it in the calendar now.
  • Start with the payment flow: your payment provider, your database, your backups. Those three answer the deadline question.
  • Work down the ten checks in section 04. Write the map on one page: every system, what it holds, where it sits, what has to move.
  • If payment data sits abroad and you are a licensed participant, name the person who owns the move and the date it starts. A plan without a name and a date is a wish.
  • File the map where the team can find it. Set the quarterly redo in the calendar now — and a redo every time you add a tool that touches customer data.
  • If nothing needs to move, you have something just as useful: proof, in writing, that you checked — and the next partner question takes five minutes instead of five days.

The deadline is 1 January 2027. Seven weeks. The map takes an afternoon. Do it while the move is still a choice, not a rush.

THE TAKEAWAY

You cannot move data you cannot see. Map it first. The deadline is 1 January.

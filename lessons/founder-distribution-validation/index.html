Distribution is not a bag of channels. It is a repeatable path to the right customer.

“

A distribution plan is only a hypothesis until it meets real customers. The goal is not to look busy or publish more content; it is to learn which route creates meaningful customer progress and whether that result can be repeated.

01 — THE 30-DAY TEST

Produce evidence, not a launch announcement.

Do not build the platform for months and then discover founders do not use it. Use the same customer-first distribution principles on the venture itself: interview founders, deliver the work manually, measure outcomes, and automate only what repeats.

FOUR-WEEK VALIDATION PLAN

  • Days 1–7 · Discover: interview 10–15 founders who have shipped a product. Ask about actual attempts, budgets, friction, and outcomes.
  • Days 8–14 · Diagnose: complete five distribution audits and map reachable prospects or communities.
  • Days 15–21 · Execute: work with 3–5 pilot founders to run focused outreach experiments.
  • Days 22–30 · Decide: review demand, willingness to pay, delivery effort, and evidence of customer access.

These are proposed targets for learning, not industry benchmarks or achieved results. The aim is to decide whether the service merits another cohort.

02 — PHASE 1 · DISCOVER

Ask about what founders have already done.

Recruit founders with different products and acquisition experiences. Do not interview only friends who want to encourage you. Ask for stories, evidence, and specifics.

INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

  • Tell me about the last time you tried to get users for your product.
  • Where did your first users come from?
  • What have you tried that did not work?
  • How do you find people who might need the product today?
  • What happens after you identify a potential customer?
  • Have you paid anyone to help with acquisition? What happened?
  • What does it cost you to keep having too few users?
  • Can you show me outreach messages, analytics, referral records, or customer conversations?

Do not lead with “Would you use our platform?” Actual behaviour, shared evidence, time committed, a pilot agreed, or a paid engagement are stronger signals than hypothetical enthusiasm.

Use a simple 0–2 rubric for problem frequency, impact, existing effort, urgency, and willingness to engage. The maximum is 10. This is an internal note-taking aid, not a validated market instrument or a substitute for judgment.

03 — PHASE 2 · DELIVER

Start with one clear offer: The Distribution Sprint.

Offer a 7–10 day guided engagement that moves a founder from “I don't know where to find users” to a researched, prioritized, tested customer-reach plan.

WHAT THE FOUNDER RECEIVES

  • A concise review of the product, buyer, and problem.
  • Two or three candidate customer segments, with a reason to prioritize one.
  • A list of relevant, verifiable prospects or places where customers gather.
  • One or two channel hypotheses, including trade-offs and likely costs.
  • Personalized outreach messages or an appropriate introduction strategy.
  • A small live experiment and a report of responses, lessons, and next steps.

The founder must provide product access and context, approve messaging, and participate in customer conversations. Do not promise a fixed number of paying customers or send unsolicited bulk messages.

For the first two pilots, consider a discounted paid engagement or a clearly bounded research partnership. Track delivery hours, direct expenses, and perceived value before setting a standard price. Charge for demonstrated value, not the length of a report.

04 — PHASE 3 · LEARN

Separate activity from customer progress.

Sending 100 messages is activity. Replies show a response signal. Qualified conversations provide stronger evidence. A product trial, repeat use, or purchase tells you more about adoption and willingness to pay.

Reach·Response·Adoption·Revenue

RECORD FOR EVERY EXPERIMENT

  • Qualified prospects identified and successfully contacted.
  • Positive replies, referrals, and qualified conversations.
  • Product trials, activation, and repeat usage where measurable.
  • Paid conversions and acquisition cost when the buying cycle permits.
  • Time, money, objections, source quality, and next decision.

Not every pilot reaches revenue in 30 days. Define the experiment's intended outcome first, account for small samples and buying cycles, and do not call a channel validated after one lucky conversion.

05 — PHASE 4 · DECIDE

Set decision rules before enthusiasm takes over.

Use the first cohort to decide whether to continue, refine, or pause. These are proposed internal thresholds, not market benchmarks.

GOContinue to a second cohort

Complete 15 interviews, deliver at least three pilots, see credible improvement in customer access, and have two founders willing to pay or explicitly commit to a paid engagement.

REFINEChange the offer

Founders recognize the problem but do not commit, prospect data is hard to verify, or activity fails to produce meaningful conversations.

PAUSEReconsider the premise

Founders will not participate, work cannot reach relevant customers, or delivery effort exceeds demonstrated value.

SEPARATEValidate two things

Test whether founders will pay us and whether our work actually helps them reach their customers. One can be true without the other.

06 — WHAT COMES NEXT

Turn repeated work into a platform—not assumptions.

Let the manual service become the research environment. Record which tasks repeat, which decisions need expert judgment, and which information improves later engagements.

STAGE 1→Manual intelligence: validate the process
STAGE 2→Standardized service: validate repeatability and economics
STAGE 3→Founder-facing platform: automate proven tasks
STAGE 4→Distribution intelligence network: build verified, permission-based access

Potential risks include a customer base that is too broad, generic recommendations, stale prospect data, unrealistic growth expectations, service work that cannot scale, premature automation, and trying to solve acquisition when the product itself does not deliver its promise.

The defensible asset may become accumulated knowledge about which routes work for which products and customers, under what conditions. That only compounds if sources are traceable, customer data is protected, and results are distinguished from assumptions.

PUT IT TO WORK

Design one experiment you can actually measure.

Choose one audience and one plausible route. Define the action, the measure, the time limit, and the evidence that would make you continue or change course.

  1. Write a testable hypothesis, not a vague goal.
  2. Contact a small, relevant set of prospects respectfully.
  3. Record responses, conversations, trials, cost, and time.
  4. Choose in advance what result changes your next decision.
! Reality check

One reply or sale is useful evidence, not proof of a repeatable channel. Keep sample size, buying cycle, product quality, and the limits of your data in view.

THE TAKEAWAY

Treat every route as a test. Record outcomes, learn from weak signals, and repeat what produces real customer progress—not merely activity.

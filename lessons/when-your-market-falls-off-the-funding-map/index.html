When Your Market Falls Off the Funding Map

Senegal raised $153 million in nine months. A year later it raised $5 million. The companies did not get worse — the map moved. Here is how to tell whether a funding collapse is your problem, and where your customers actually are.

“

In the first nine months of 2025, startups in Senegal raised $153 million. In the same nine months of 2026, they raised $5 million. That is a fall of almost 97%. If you run a company in Senegal, that number looks like a verdict on your market — and on you. It is not a verdict. It is a map. And the map counts something narrower than your business. A funding total counts investment deals, sorted by the country the company is based in. It does not count your customers, your revenue, or where your customers actually pay from. This lesson shows you how to read a collapse like Senegal's, and how to tell whether it is your problem at all.

01 — THE NUMBERS THAT MOVED

Senegal raised $153 million. Then it raised $5 million.

Start with the numbers, because they are stark. Africa: The Big Deal is a tracker — a service that counts announced startup deals of $100,000 and above across the continent. Its figures for the first nine months of 2026, reported by TechNext, show Senegal falling from $153 million to $5 million. In the same period, Nigeria rose from $247 million to $635 million, Egypt took $445 million, and Kenya fell from $744.4 million to $201 million. Across the continent, startups raised about $2.16 billion in those nine months. The four biggest markets — Nigeria, Egypt, Kenya and South Africa, known as the Big Four — took 85% of the money counted for individual countries.

October added to the picture. TechCabal Insights reported on 9 November 2026 that African startups had raised $3.02 billion in the first ten months of the year, up 14.2% on the same period in 2025, with October alone bringing $540 million across 73 disclosed deals. So the continent's total is growing while Senegal's total collapses. Both things are true at once. That is the first clue that a country total is not a report on every company inside it.

Senegal does not even appear in the top ten destinations for the first ten months. Tenth place, Côte d'Ivoire, took $45 million — nine times Senegal's nine-month total. A year earlier, Senegal was raising more than three times that. The map moved fast. The question is what moved with it — and what did not.

02 — ONE DEAL WAS MOST OF THE TOTAL

In a small market, one deal can be the whole total.

Here is the mechanism, and it is simple. In a small market, the total is a handful of deals added up. One big deal can be most of the total. Wave, the Senegalese mobile money company, raised a $137 million debt facility in June 2025, led by Rand Merchant Bank with backing from British International Investment, Finnfund and Norfund. That single deal was about nine-tenths of Senegal's whole $153 million total for the first nine months of 2025. The arithmetic is on the two reported figures: $137 million of $153 million.

When a total is built that way, it can collapse without anything going wrong underneath. Wave did not raise a second $137 million in 2026, so Senegal's total fell by almost 97%. The companies in Senegal did not get 97% worse. One deal did not repeat. That is the whole story of the fall — and it is why a country total is a fragile number to plan a company around.

This is not a one-country pattern. Our earlier lesson on building revenue-first worked the same arithmetic for two other markets: Tanzania raised $53 million in the first nine months of 2026, and $50 million of it was one debt raise by NALA, a money-transfer company. Côte d'Ivoire raised $48 million, and $45 million of it was one raise by GoCab. In each case, one deal was about 94% of the total. If you want the full workings, read that lesson — the numbers are there. Here, the point is what the pattern means for you: a market total can be a report on one or two companies, not on a market.

✳
The arithmetic

Minus the biggest deal, what is left? Senegal: $153 million minus $137 million leaves $16 million for every other startup in the country combined. Always ask that question before you trust a total.

03 — BASED HERE, PAID ELSEWHERE

The map counts where you are based, not where your customers are.

Now the part that changes the decision. A tracker has to put each company in one country, and it usually picks the country the company is based in. A business, though, can earn in many countries. Wave is based in Senegal. It operates across West and Central Africa. On 17 November 2026, the Central Bank of Congo approved Wave as an electronic money institution in the Democratic Republic of Congo — the first new licence of its kind there since 2015. The same month Senegal's nine-month funding total sat at $5 million, its biggest company was opening a new country of about 100 million people. The company was not failing. The map had simply stopped counting big deals under Senegal.

NALA is the same shape in the other direction. Its $50 million debt raise is counted under Tanzania, because that is where the company is based. Its business is moving money across borders. The map files it under one country. The customers live in several.

So when a headline says a market “fell off the funding map”, read it carefully. It means fewer big investment deals were counted under that country's name. It does not mean the companies based there stopped earning. It does not mean their customers stopped paying. And it does not mean your company — if your customers are somewhere else — has the same problem as the total.

The map misses other money too. Afreenergy Solar, a Senegalese clean-energy company, raised a $13.4 million senior debt facility in November 2026 — debt, not a venture equity round, and the kind of deal the funding conversation usually skips past. Senegal's own state agency for entrepreneurs, DER/FJ, announced a $50 million vehicle for pre-seed and seed startups in June 2026, with seed tickets of about €50,000 to €60,000. And the money customers pay — revenue — never appears in a funding total at all. Africa: The Big Deal puts seed funding at just 1.5% of all the capital invested in African startups, against 4% to 6% typical in the United States. The map is mostly a map of big deals. Do not read it as a map of business.

✳
The distinction

A funding total is a map of investor deals, not a map of businesses. Read it as the first, never as the second.

04 — THREE MISTAKES WHEN THE MAP MOVES

Chase the hotspot. Wait for the money. Believe the verdict.

Founders make three mistakes when a total like Senegal's collapses. Each one feels sensible. Each one costs something.

First, move to chase the hotspot. Nigeria sits at the top of the table, with $688 million in the first ten months of 2026. The temptation is to move the company there — to be counted where the money is. Moving costs real money and real months: new registration, new hires, a new network, while your existing customers wait. And the hotspot moves. Kenya raised $744.4 million in the first nine months of 2025 and $201 million in the same period of 2026 — a fall of 73%. A company built around being counted in the hot market is a company built around a number it does not control. Move for customers, not for a ranking.

Second, wait for the money to return. Senegal's 2025 total was one deal, not a trend. Waiting for it to “come back” assumes the map is a cycle that repeats. Sometimes the big deal was a one-off, and the normal level for your market is small. A plan that needs the map to recover is a plan that needs luck. Build the plan that works if the map never recovers — then any recovery is a bonus, not a rescue.

Third, believe the verdict. The most expensive mistake is reading “$5 million” as “my company is worth nothing”. The total never measured your customers, your revenue, or your costs. It measured investment deals counted under one country's name. The question that matters is not “what did my market raise?” It is “who pays me, and where are they?” That question has an answer you can find in your own records. The total does not.

✳
The rule

The map is about investors. Your company is about customers. When the two disagree, trust the customers.

05 — BUILD YOUR CUSTOMER MAP

List your last twenty paying customers. Put each one on a map.

The tool is one page. Take your last twenty paying customers — the last twenty people or businesses that paid you, in order. Three columns: who they are, where they are, and what they pay you each month. Fill it from your payment records, not from memory. Memory flatters. Records do not.

Then read it three ways. First, where is the money? Add up what each country pays you. If most of your revenue already comes from outside the country you are counted in, your home market's funding total is not your problem. Your problem is reaching more customers where they already are. Second, where is the concentration? If one country is 80% of your revenue, you have the same lumpiness as the funding map — one market's trouble becomes your trouble, and you should know that before it happens. Third, where is the next customer? The country where you already have two or three paying customers — with no office, no team and no marketing there — is a market that found you. That is the cheapest place to grow.

The decision rule is simple. If more than half your revenue comes from outside your home market, stop planning around your home market's funding news. Plan around your customers' markets. If all your revenue is at home, the collapse is a real warning — and the answer is the revenue-first plan in our earlier lesson, including the 12-month test that prices a year with no outside money. The customer map tells you which of those two situations you are in. That is its whole job.

Both examples below are illustrative — made-up stories built to show the method working, not reports of real companies. The numbers are round and simple for the same reason.

First, the clinic software that was never really Senegalese. A founder in Dakar sells appointment-booking software to clinics. Ten clinics pay $50 a month each. She builds the map and finds three clinics in Senegal, four in Côte d'Ivoire and three in Ghana. The money splits: Senegal 30%, Côte d'Ivoire 40%, Ghana 30%. Senegal's funding total fell 97%, but 70% of her revenue never lived on that map. Her next move is not to move the company. It is one sales push in Abidjan, where four clinics already pay her and she has never advertised. The map told her the market was dead. Her records told her where the customers were.

Second, the move that cost a year. A founder in a quiet market reads the funding table, moves the whole company to Lagos to be counted in Nigeria's $635 million, and spends six months re-registering, hiring and rebuilding a network. His customers back home wait, then leave. A year later, the table has moved again — the hotspot is somewhere else — and he has a new office, no old customers, and the same product. The move bought him a place on the map. It cost him the customers who were already paying. Move for customers, not for a ranking.

✳
The customer map in one line

Where your money comes from is your real market. The funding map is someone else's.

06 — WHAT THE MAP CANNOT TELL YOU

Five limits on everything above.

First, selling across borders has real costs. Different rules, different payment methods, support in another time zone or another language. A customer in another country is not free revenue. Price the cost of serving them before you chase them.

Second, some businesses genuinely need the home market. If your product only works where you are — a licence, an agent network, a physical presence — then the home market's funding climate matters more, and the revenue-first plan matters more. The customer map does not change that. It only tells you the truth about where you stand.

Third, the map can be wrong in both directions. A tracker's total misses deals below $100,000, deals that were never announced, and revenue entirely. Senegal's $5 million is a count of counted deals, not a census of business activity. Do not treat it as one.

Fourth, a customer map is a snapshot, not a forecast. Twenty customers today can become five tomorrow if one big customer leaves. Update the map every quarter, and watch the shares move.

Fifth, the customer map is not a fundraising plan. Knowing your customers are elsewhere does not raise money. If you need capital, the fundraising work still has to be done — the map only tells you which market's investors to stop waiting for. Our lesson on reading a fund announcement is the next read if that is your situation.

07 — THE NEXT STEP

List your last twenty customers this week.

  • Open your payment records and list your last twenty paying customers: who they are, where they are, and what they pay each month.
  • Add up the revenue by country. Write down the share that comes from outside your home market.
  • Name the one market outside home where you already have a paying customer. That is your second map.
  • This week, have one conversation with a customer in that market. Ask what they would pay for next — not whether they like the product, but what they would pay for.
  • If all twenty are at home, read the revenue-first lesson and run its Monday plan. Your problem is real, and it has a plan.

A market can fall off the funding map. Your customers did not fall off anything. Find out where they are — that is your real market.

THE TAKEAWAY

A funding total counts deals by where a company is based. Your customers may live somewhere else entirely. Find out where your money comes from — that is your real market.
